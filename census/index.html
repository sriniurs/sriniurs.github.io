How Do You Count
1.4 Billion

India’s first census in 16 years is also its first digital one. Thirty lakh people, two phases, one frozen second. Here’s how it actually works.

ABOUT 8 MINUTES

01

One frozen second

Here’s a puzzle. Babies are born every few seconds. People move house, catch trains, die. How do you count a country that never stands still?

The answer: you pick one exact moment and pretend the whole country froze. For this census, that moment is 00:00 hours on 1 March 2027 for most of India. (Ladakh and snowbound parts of a few hill states use 1 October 2026, because winter makes them hard to reach.) [1]

Every answer is about that instant. A baby born at 00:05 on 1 March is not counted. A person who dies at 00:05 is counted, because they were alive at the census moment. [2]

This is the 16th census since 1872 and the 8th since Independence. [3] It was due in 2021, and the first phase was ready to begin in April 2020 before Covid stopped it. [1] The last actual count, in 2011, found 121.19 crore people. [3] Everything since then, including the “1.4 billion” in our title, is an estimate. That’s exactly why we count.

02

Count the houses before the people

You can’t knock on every door until you know where every door is. So the census runs in two phases.

Phase 1 · Houselisting · Apr–Sep 2026→Phase 2 · Population count · Feb 2027

Source: [4]

Phase 1 maps every building and household. India is cut into small houselisting blocks, each given to one enumerator, so no house is missed or counted twice. [5] This time the blocks are drawn on a digital map using satellite imagery. [6] At each home the enumerator asks 33 questions about the house itself: its condition, the amenities it has, and the things the household owns. [7]

Phase 2, in February 2027, uses that map to visit every household again and count each person: age, education, work, migration, and, for the first time since 1931, caste. [3] [8]

Who does all this? About 30 lakh field staff. Most enumerators are government schoolteachers, doing census work on top of their regular jobs. [4] The Union Cabinet approved a budget of ₹11,718 crore. [4] If India has about 140 crore people, that works out to roughly ₹84 per person counted. (Illustrative arithmetic.)

The census counts houses first, because a good map is what stops anyone being missed in the second round.
03

Karnataka went first

Karnataka was in the very first batch of states to start. [9] And for the first time, you could count yourself.

From 1 to 15 April 2026, any household in Karnataka could fill in its own Phase 1 details on the official census portal. Once done, the family got a Self-Enumeration ID by SMS or email. From 16 April to 15 May, enumerators came door to door to check those entries and fill in the rest. [10]

Across the 11 states and UTs in that first round, more than 5.72 lakh households had counted themselves within the first ten days. [11] Everything collected is confidential under the Census Act, 1948. [12] One honest trade-off that analysts point out: self-enumerating links your phone number to your house location in census records. [13]

By mid-2026, Phase 1 had already been completed in 29 states and UTs, with Tamil Nadu among the last to start. [7]

04

The night before the census

Phase 2 is the big one: every person, one by one. India has done this before, so the 2011 census shows how it works.

In February 2011, about 27 lakh enumerators visited roughly 24 crore households over three weeks, from 9 to 28 February. [14]

But some people have no door to knock on. So on the night of 28 February 2011, the last night before the census moment, enumerators went out after dark to count the houseless: people sleeping on pavements, under flyovers, beside railway lines, in buildings under construction and around big hospitals. [15] In Delhi, NGOs walked with enumerators because they knew where people slept. [16]

Then came a final check called the Revision Round. Enumerators went back to update any births or deaths between their visit and midnight, so the count matched the census moment exactly. [14]

The 2027 count happens in the same February window, with the same midnight on 1 March. [4] [1]

ZappySo the census literally works the night shift?

Prof. BoltThe people easiest to miss are counted last, and counted carefully.

05

1881: India’s first one-night count

India’s first all-India count took place between 1867 and 1872, but it was done at different times in different provinces. [17] The first count of the whole country at once came on 17 February 1881, led by Census Commissioner W. Chichele Plowden. His report called it “the first synchronous enumeration attempted for all of India.” [18]

The 1881 report is full of surprises. It listed 106 “Indian tongues”, plus 17 other Asian languages, 28 European ones and one African one. [18] And it has a Karnataka detail: Coorg (today’s Kodagu) had the highest share of men of any province, 56.33 for every 100 people, against an all-India average of about 51. [18]

The census has been taken every ten years since then, without a break, until Covid stopped the 2021 round. [17] [1] Paper slips have become a phone app, but the basic idea, one night and every person, is 145 years old.

The 2027 census is a digital version of a method first used in 1881.
06

The 1931 question

The biggest new question in 2027 is also one of the oldest: caste. This episode sticks to what happened, and when.

Up to 1931 · every caste counted→1951–2011 · only SC and ST counted→2011 · separate survey, caste data not released→2027 · all castes counted again

Sources: [19] [20] [4]

1931 was the last census to record every caste. [8] From 1951, the first census of independent India, the government stopped counting caste in general. The reason it gave was that counting caste would keep social divisions alive. [19] Only Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes were still counted, because the Constitution reserves seats for them and that needs numbers. [19]

In 2011, a separate Socio-Economic and Caste Census (SECC) was run alongside the main census. Its economic findings were published, but its caste data never was. [20] The Centre later told the Supreme Court the caste data was “unusable” because of technical flaws; others have disputed that. [21] Meanwhile, several states, including Bihar and Karnataka, ran caste surveys of their own. [20]

On 30 April 2025, the Union Cabinet’s political affairs committee decided to include caste in the 2027 census. It will be recorded in Phase 2, in February 2027. [4]

07

What these numbers decide

Census numbers aren’t just statistics. They feed into how many MPs a state gets, how central tax money is shared out, and which areas get reserved seats. [22]

Here’s a fact most people don’t know. The number of Lok Sabha seats each state gets is still based on the 1971 census. Parliament froze it in 1976 and extended the freeze in 2001, partly so that states which brought their population growth down would not lose seats for doing so. The freeze lasts until the figures of the first census taken after 2026 are published. [23] That census is this one.

The census also matters for women’s reservation. The 2023 law reserving one-third of Lok Sabha and Assembly seats for women takes effect only after a fresh delimitation (a redrawing of seats) based on the new census. [23] In April 2026, a bill to redraw seats using the old 2011 figures instead got 298 votes in favour and 230 against. That was short of the two-thirds majority it needed, so it failed, and the related bills were withdrawn. [24]

So the 2027 count is, for now, the trigger for both. States in the south and the north see the possible effects very differently, which is why the debate is intense. [25]

This episode explains the rules as they stand in November 2026. Parliament can change them, and these rules have been changed before.
08

Where your answers go

Under the Census Act, 1948, what you tell the census is confidential. [12] The government publishes totals, not individual answers, and this time it promises data down to the village and ward level. [26]

Going digital should also make results faster. In 2011, paper forms had to be scanned and read by machine before the numbers could be added up. [27] This time the data goes straight from a phone to a central system, and the first figures are expected by late 2027. [25]

Go back to where we started: one frozen second at midnight on 1 March 2027. Behind that second are 30 lakh people, a map of every house in India, a night count of the people with no house, and questions that will shape politics for decades. [4] When the enumerator comes to your door next February, you’ll know what the visit is part of.
