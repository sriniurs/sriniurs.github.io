MYSURU · MONEY · EVERYDAY LIFE

The Mysuru Squeeze:
Peaceful City,
Busy Budget

Why does a familiar city suddenly feel expensive? Follow the coffee, the commute and the house keys.

Local life, larger forces

ABOUT 6 MINUTES

A researched explainer inspired by the video. Dated price changes are historical examples; sample budgets are illustrative.

01

The coffee is only the opening clue

Has Mysuru become expensive—or has the bill changed in more than one way?

Imagine Zappy looking at a ₹20 filter-coffee bill and wondering whether the café has borrowed its ingredients from the palace treasury. It is an invented scene, but the question behind it is useful.

A café sells more than milk and coffee powder. Its price must also cover staff, premises, electricity, equipment and the service of making the drink. A change in one ingredient cannot, by itself, explain the whole menu.

There are several questions hidden inside “everything costs more”. Has the same product become dearer? Are we buying a different version? Or are we buying it more often? The answers can overlap, but separating them helps us understand the squeeze.

02

Small increases have a monthly life

Some everyday increases are documented. Reporting on the Karnataka government’s March 2025 decision described a ₹4-per-litre increase in Nandini milk and curd prices, effective 1 April 2025. The report gave toned-milk prices of ₹42 before and ₹46 after the revision. [1]

These are dated examples, not a current Mysuru price list. The important mechanism is repetition: a small extra amount can recur every day.

For a household buying one litre daily, an extra ₹4 means ₹120 over thirty days. That is our arithmetic using the reported increase. A household buying half as much has a different exposure.

A memorable price on one packet tells part of the story. Quantity and frequency tell us what happens to the month’s budget.

03

A transport price has a route attached

The bus story also needs its date. The Karnataka Cabinet approved a 15% revision in state-run bus fares beginning 5 January 2025, according to contemporary reporting. [2] That historical decision should not be presented as a fresh announcement.

To understand a household’s travel bill, we need more than the headline percentage. Which service does the person use? How many journeys do they make? Are there transfers? What ticket or pass applies?

The same city can therefore produce different transport experiences for two neighbours. One may walk to work; another may pay for several daily trips. A household budget follows the journeys someone actually makes, rather than the average imagined commuter.

A cheaper ticket on an inconvenient route may also come with a longer journey. Money and time belong in the same conversation.

04

The expressway brings possibilities, not one verdict

Mysuru’s connection with Bengaluru is part of the backdrop. The Union transport ministry’s review records the expressway’s inauguration in March 2023 and described a reduction in travel time to about 75 minutes. That was a project description, not a guaranteed door-to-door commute. [3]

The Karnataka Digital Economy Mission also includes Mysuru among its Beyond Bengaluru clusters, supporting technology activity outside the capital. [4]

Better access and more employment can make a place attractive to additional households and businesses. If demand for homes in a particular neighbourhood grows faster than available housing, rents can face pressure. That is an economic mechanism, not proof of how much the expressway has raised Mysuru rents.

Growth can create opportunities and new competition for space at the same time. Neither effect fits neatly on a café receipt.

Prof. BoltYes. It did not issue one identical bill to everyone.

05

The house has two price tags

A rental discussion often mixes the monthly payment with the cash needed to move in. They answer different questions.

Consider an invented offer: ₹18,000 monthly rent and a deposit equal to six months’ rent. The deposit is ₹1,08,000. Adding the first month’s rent brings the initial cash requirement to ₹1,26,000, before moving expenses.

A refundable deposit is not the same as rent spent every month. It still ties up money that the tenant cannot use elsewhere. Its refund terms also matter. This example describes no actual listing or standard Mysuru deposit requirement.

The comparison changes again when location enters. A home saving ₹2,000 in monthly rent but adding ₹2,500 in travel costs is ₹500 more expensive on those two items combined. The lower headline rent has not produced a lower total.

06

Avocado toast is not the whole food basket

A premium café menu is noticeable. It is not a survey of what an entire city pays to eat.

We can change our food spending without any price rising: ordering delivery more often, switching brands, buying ready-cut ingredients or choosing a larger pack that goes partly unused. Equally, an unchanged shopping routine can cost more when the same goods become dearer.

A useful comparison holds the basket reasonably steady. Compare the same quantities and types of food across two periods, then separately identify changes in what was bought. Otherwise a different shopping trip can look like a pure price increase.

This is why a single “family grocery bill” needs assumptions: household size, meals eaten at home and what the basket includes. A dramatic number without those details describes a claim, not a typical Mysuru household.

07

Your inflation has its own shopping list

A broad inflation measure combines many prices using spending weights. The Reserve Bank’s explanation of the revised Consumer Price Index discusses how household expenditure data shape those weights and the consumption basket. [5]

An individual household has its own pattern. Someone spending heavily on rent can feel a rental increase more sharply than a homeowner whose largest recurring expense is elsewhere. Neither experience alone describes everyone.

For a simple illustrative example, take a monthly budget of ₹30,000. If rent rises by ₹1,000 and groceries by ₹500 while everything else stays unchanged, the total becomes ₹31,500: a 5% increase. No claim about Mysuru’s measured inflation follows from that calculation.

The same care applies to comparing cities. “Cheaper than Bengaluru” needs comparable homes, journeys and lifestyles. Even a lower bill can feel difficult if the household’s income is lower too.

₹1,500 extra ÷ ₹30,000 original budget × 100 = 5%Illustrative arithmetic; not a city inflation estimate.
08

Peace of mind needs a fuller picture

Put the pieces together and the city becomes easier to read. Some changes come from prices. Others come from where we live, how we travel and which version of a service we choose.

That does not make a stretched budget imaginary. It explains why two people can sincerely describe very different Mysurus. They may have different housing arrangements, incomes, routines and responsibilities.

Zappy’s coffee was a good opening question. The more revealing discovery lies behind it: a city’s reputation and a household’s affordability are different things. The first can be shared; the second has to be worked out from the life being lived.

There is still room to enjoy a familiar city. Understanding the bill simply helps us see which parts of that enjoyment cost money, which take time, and which remain hard to price.
