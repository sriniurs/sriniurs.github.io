HOW THIS WORKS: The mantra first, as it is chanted. Then the sage behind it, and two pages for each of its four parts: the words, a picture, what they say and the key words; then a deeper look, background facts, the inner teaching and the one thing to remember.

FOREWORD

If there is one mantra almost every Hindu home knows, it is this one. "Om bhūr bhuvaḥ svaḥ, tat savitur vareṇyaṃ…" is chanted at sunrise, at the sacred-thread ceremony, before study, and quietly by millions every day. Yet many of us have never been told, word by word, what we are saying.

This small book opens up the Gāyatrī in simple English: where it comes from, who gave it to us, what each of its words means, and why a prayer that asks for nothing but a clear mind has lasted more than three thousand years. It is for anyone who has chanted it since childhood, and for anyone hearing it for the first time.

May every reader begin each day as the mantra does: facing the light, and asking for a mind that sees clearly, for all of us.

THE STORY · THE KING WHO BECAME A SAGE

Viśvāmitra, "friend of the world"
The traditional account in 5 frames
A king. Viśvāmitra begins life as Kauśika, a powerful warrior-king.
A wish-cow. At sage Vasiṣṭha's hermitage he sees the wish-fulfilling cow and tries to take it. Vasiṣṭha's spiritual power defeats his whole army.
A turn. Realising that inner power is greater than armies, the king gives up his throne and begins fierce austerities (tapas).
Brahmarṣi. After many trials and long years, he becomes a brahmarṣi, the highest kind of sage, recognised even by Vasiṣṭha.
The seer of the mantra. Tradition honours him as the ṛṣi to whom the Gāyatrī was revealed, in the third book of the Ṛg Veda.

The seer

Viśvāmitra, credited with most of book 3 of the Ṛg Veda.

The deity

Savitṛ, the sun as the power that wakes the world.

The metre

Gāyatrī: 3 lines × 8 syllables = 24.

ZAPPY: A king giving up his kingdom for a mantra?

PROF. BOLT: For what the mantra points to. He learnt the hard way that a clear mind beats an army.

The Gāyatrī comes from a man who changed himself completely. Fitting, for a prayer about transforming the mind.

ABOUT THE MANTRA

Three thousand years old, chanted every day

📜 Where it's found

Ṛg Veda 3.62.10, and repeated in the Yajur Veda and Sāma Veda. The Upaniṣads, the Bhagavad Gītā and the Manusmṛti all refer to it.

🌅 When it's chanted

In sandhyāvandana at dawn, noon and dusk; at the upanayana (sacred-thread ceremony), where it is first taught; and before study and prayer.

Gāyatrī or Sāvitrī? "Gāyatrī" is strictly the name of the metre: three lines of eight syllables. Because this verse is the most famous one in that metre, the name stuck to it. Its older name is Sāvitrī, after Savitṛ, the deity it addresses. Later tradition also honours Gāyatrī Devī, the mantra pictured as a Goddess and called Vedamātā, Mother of the Vedas.

The name explained. The Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad, which discusses this mantra at length, explains gāyatrī as that which protects (trāyate) the life-breaths (gayas). It's often put more simply: the mantra protects the one who chants it.

For everyone. For centuries it was taught mainly within the sacred-thread ceremony. From the 19th century, reform movements spread its chanting to women and people of every background, and today it is one of the most widely chanted mantras in the world.

OM BHŪR BHUVAḤ SVAḤ · GOING DEEPER

OM AND THE THREE WORLDS

The doorway. The Ṛg Vedic verse begins at tat. In daily practice it is preceded by oṃ and three words called the vyāhṛtis, "utterances": bhūḥ, bhuvaḥ, svaḥ. The Taittirīya Āraṇyaka describes chanting it this way: Om, then the three vyāhṛtis, then the verse.

Three worlds. Bhūḥ is the earth, the solid world of bodies and things. Bhuvaḥ is the space between: atmosphere, wind, the realm of life-breath. Svaḥ is heaven, the realm of light. In three syllables the chanter names the whole universe, from the ground to the sky.

Inner readings. Teachers also read the three worlds inside us: body (bhūḥ), breath and life-force (bhuvaḥ), and mind and awareness (svaḥ). Either way, the meaning is the same: the light we are about to praise fills everything, outside and in.

💡 Mahāvyāhṛti

Together, Om and bhūr bhuvaḥ svaḥ are called the mahāvyāhṛti, the "great utterance". The full set lists seven worlds; the first three are used here.

💡 Om

Om is said at the start of every Vedic recitation. The Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad is entirely about it.

Starting with "earth, sky, heaven" widens the mind before the prayer begins. You stop being one person with a request and become part of the whole universe, greeting its source.

ZAPPY: Is 'bhūr bhuvaḥ svaḥ' part of the Ṛg Veda verse?

PROF. BOLT: No. It's the doorway added in front. The verse itself starts at 'tat'.

Before asking for anything, the chanter first names the whole universe: earth, sky, heaven.

TAT SAVITUR VAREṆYAṂ · GOING DEEPER

THAT MOST EXCELLENT LIGHT OF SAVITṚ

Tat: "that". The mantra doesn't begin with a name but with a pointer: tat, "that". It's the same word Vedānta uses in tat tvam asi, "that you are". Something too great to name is simply pointed at.

Savitṛ. From the root sū, to impel or set in motion. Savitṛ is the sun as the power that rouses everything: the force that wakes birds, opens flowers, and gets people out of bed. Vedic hymns picture Savitṛ especially at dawn and dusk, the moments of change. That's why the mantra is chanted at those times.

Vareṇyam. "To be chosen", most excellent, most desirable. Of all the things the world offers, the mantra says, choose this light. A small technical note: in the verse as preserved, vareṇyaṃ has seven syllables, not eight; reciters often say vareṇiyaṃ to restore the metre's count.

💡 Where it comes from

Ṛg Veda, book 3, hymn 62, verse 10. Book 3 is traditionally the family book of Viśvāmitra and his descendants.

💡 Sāvitrī

Because it's addressed to Savitṛ, the mantra is also called the Sāvitrī. "Gāyatrī" is actually the name of its metre.

Every day we choose what to fill our minds with. Vareṇyam is a quiet reminder: of everything on offer, choose what brings light.

ZAPPY: Isn't Savitṛ just another name for the sun?

PROF. BOLT: Close. Sūrya is the sun you see. Savitṛ is the power behind it that wakes the world up.

Of everything in the world, choose the light. That's what vareṇyam says.

BHARGO DEVASYA DHĪMAHI · GOING DEEPER

ON THAT DIVINE RADIANCE WE MEDITATE

Bhargaḥ. Radiance, splendour. Traditional teachers connect it to the root bhṛj, to roast or burn: the light that burns away impurity and ignorance, as the morning sun burns away mist. It's not a soft glow but a cleansing fire.

Devasya. Of the divine, the shining one. Deva comes from a root meaning "to shine". The radiance belongs to the Divine, not to a ball of gas; the sun is its most visible sign.

Dhīmahi. "We meditate", or "may we hold in mind". This is the heart of the mantra. We aren't just admiring the light; we take it in and hold it steadily in thought. Notice the plural: it is something we do together.

💡 Same root, three times

Dhīmahi (we meditate) and dhiyaḥ (minds) in the next line come from the same family of words. The mantra links meditation and the mind deliberately.

💡 Gāyatrī elsewhere

Many deities have their own Gāyatrīs in the same pattern, like the Rudra Gāyatrī: "tat puruṣāya vidmahe, mahādevāya dhīmahi…"

Meditation here is simple: hold one bright thought steadily. A minute of that each morning does what this line asks: lets the light do its cleaning.

ZAPPY: Why 'we' meditate, not 'I'?

PROF. BOLT: Because the whole mantra is in the plural. Even meditation here is something we do together.

Not just look at the light: hold it in the mind until it starts to burn away the darkness.

DHIYO YO NAḤ PRACODAYĀT · GOING DEEPER

MAY IT INSPIRE OUR MINDS

Dhiyaḥ. Minds, thoughts, intelligence, insight. In the Veda the word also means "prayers" or "inspired thoughts". So the line can be read as "may He inspire our thinking" and "may He set our prayers in motion": both work.

Naḥ: "our". Not "my mind" but "our minds". The most famous prayer in Hinduism is a group prayer. Even chanted alone, it asks for everyone's understanding to be lit up.

Pracodayāt. "May He impel, urge on, inspire." It's the same root idea as Savitṛ, the Impeller. The sun sets the world in motion each morning; the prayer asks that the same power set our minds in the right direction. Swami Vivekananda translated the whole: "We meditate on the glory of that Being who has produced this universe; may She enlighten our minds."

💡 Nothing else asked

The Gāyatrī asks for no wealth, health or victory. Tradition holds that a well-guided mind takes care of the rest.

💡 The Gītā

In Bhagavad Gītā 10.35, Kṛṣṇa says: "Among metres, I am the Gāyatrī."

A clear mind is the root of every good decision. Asking each day for that, for yourself and for everyone, is a prayer that never goes out of date.

ZAPPY: So the most famous prayer in India doesn't ask for anything?

PROF. BOLT: It asks for the one thing that makes everything else go right: a well-guided mind.

The whole request: light up our minds. Not wealth, not success. Just clear thinking, for all of us.
