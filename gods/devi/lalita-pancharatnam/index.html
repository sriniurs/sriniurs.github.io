FOREWORD

Some hymns are long and grand. The Lalitā Pañcaratnam is five small gems, meant to be held in the palm at sunrise. Each verse begins with the same word, prātaḥ, "at dawn", and each does one simple thing: remember Her face, worship Her arms, bow at Her feet, praise Her true nature, speak Her names.

This small book opens up those five gems in simple English: what each verse says, what its key words mean, and the teaching hidden inside. It is for anyone who has heard this hymn in the morning without knowing its meaning, and for anyone who would like a five-minute way to begin the day well.

May every reader find in these verses a gentle start to each day: a face to remember, a hand to hold, feet to bow to, a truth to wonder at, and a name to call. Śrī Lalitāmbikāyai namaḥ.

THE STORY · WHO IS LALITĀ?

The Goddess who rose from the fire of consciousness
The Lalitopākhyāna in 5 frames
Kāma burnt. Kāma, the god of love, shoots his flower arrows at Śiva, who opens his third eye and burns him to ashes.
A demon from the ashes. From those ashes rises Bhaṇḍāsura, a powerful demon who torments the worlds.
The fire of consciousness. The gods perform a great offering, and from the sacrificial fire, the cidagni-kuṇḍa, rises Lalitā Tripurasundarī, radiant and red like the hibiscus.
Her weapons. She holds the noose, goad, sugarcane bow and five flower arrows: love's own weapons, now in divine hands.
Victory. She defeats Bhaṇḍa, restores the gods, and rules from Her city, Śrīnagara, with Kāmeśvara (Śiva) as Her consort.

The source

The Lalitopākhyāna, the story of Lalitā in the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa.

The meaning

Desire, burnt and misused, becomes a demon. In Her hands, the same power becomes grace.

The hymn

Śaṅkara's five verses picture this Goddess at dawn, from Her face to Her names.

ZAPPY: Love's weapons in the Goddess's hands? Isn't that strange?

PROF. BOLT: It's the whole point. Desire burnt in anger made a demon. Held by the Mother, the same power becomes sweetness.

Lalitā means "She who plays": the whole universe is Her graceful play.

ABOUT THE HYMN

Five gems, five verbs, one sunrise

✍️ Who wrote it

Traditionally Ādi Śaṅkarācārya; printed copies end with his traditional signature as disciple of Govinda Bhagavatpāda. Also known as Lalitā Pañcakam and Devī Prātaḥsmaraṇam.

🌅 When to chant

At daybreak. Every verse begins with prātaḥ, "at dawn", and the phalaśruti asks for it to be read prabhāte, at sunrise.

Five verbs. The hymn is built on five actions, one per verse: smarāmi (I remember), bhajāmi (I worship), namāmi (I bow), stuve (I praise), vadāmi (I speak). Mind, hands, head, heart and voice: the whole person, offered in five verses.

From form to beyond. Verses 1–3 travel over Her form: face, arms, feet. Verse 4 turns to what She truly is and admits She is beyond speech and mind. Verse 5 answers that with Her names: what can't be thought can still be called.

Lalitā and Śrī Vidyā. Lalitā Tripurasundarī is the Goddess at the centre of Śrī Vidyā, worshipped through the Śrī Yantra and the Lalitā Sahasranāma. This little hymn is often the morning doorway into that larger world.

VERSE 1 · GOING DEEPER

AT DAWN I REMEMBER HER FACE

A morning hymn. Every verse of this little hymn begins with prātaḥ, "at dawn". It's meant to be the first thing on your mind when you wake. Śaṅkara doesn't open with philosophy or requests. He opens with a face, the way a child waking up looks first for its mother's face.

The details. Her lips are bimba-red, the colour of the ripe ivy-gourd fruit, a favourite comparison in Sanskrit poetry. A large pearl shines on Her nose. Her eyes are ākarṇa-dīrgha, so long they seem to reach Her ears, a classic mark of beauty. She wears jewelled earrings, smiles gently (manda-smita), and Her forehead glows with mṛgamada, musk.

Remembering is the first step. The verb is smarāmi, "I remember". The whole hymn moves through five actions, one per verse: remember, worship, bow, praise, speak. It starts with the inner act. Before hands or voice do anything, the mind brings Her face to light.

💡 Who is Lalitā?

Lalitā Tripurasundarī, "She who plays" and "the most beautiful in the three worlds", is the central Goddess of the Śrī Vidyā tradition and of the Lalitā Sahasranāma.

💡 Mṛgamada

Literally "the deer's intoxicant": musk, used since ancient times for the forehead mark (tilaka) and in perfume.

Starting the day with a beautiful, loving image shapes how the whole day feels. Before the phone, before the news, Śaṅkara suggests one minute of seeing the Mother's smiling face.

ZAPPY: Five verses and he starts with her nose-ring?

PROF. BOLT: He starts with her face because that's where you look first when you wake up and see your mother.

Begin the day by remembering Her face: the first gem is simply to see Her.

VERSE 2 · GOING DEEPER

AT DAWN I WORSHIP HER ARMS

Arms that give. Bhuja-kalpavallī: Her arms are like the kalpavallī, the wish-fulfilling creeper of heaven. Her fingers are soft as new shoots (pallava), shining with red-gemmed rings; ruby and gold bangles and armlets adorn Her. These are arms made for giving.

What the hands hold. Lalitā's classic weapons are the noose (pāśa), the goad (aṅkuśa or sṛṇi), the sugarcane bow (ikṣu-cāpa) and five flower arrows. This verse names three of them: puṇḍrekṣu-cāpa, a bow of fine sugarcane; kusumeṣu, the flower arrows; and sṛṇi, the goad.

What they mean. The Lalitā Sahasranāma calls Her manorūpekṣu-kodaṇḍā, She whose sugarcane bow is the mind itself, and says the five arrows are the five subtle senses. The goad steers, as a mahout steers an elephant. Put together: She takes the mind and the senses, which usually pull us around, and makes them sweet, gentle and directed.

💡 Kalpavallī

The creeper of the heavenly garden that grants any wish, the climbing cousin of the famous kalpavṛkṣa, the wish-fulfilling tree.

💡 Kāma's weapons

Kāma, the god of love, also carries a sugarcane bow and flower arrows. Lalitā holds the same weapons: the power of desire, but in the hands of the divine.

Desire isn't the enemy here; it's a bow in the right hands. Worshipping Her arms is asking that our wants and senses be held by grace, turned sweet and pointed in a good direction.

ZAPPY: A bow made of sugarcane? That can't shoot anything.

PROF. BOLT: It shoots the mind. Her bow is sweet because the mind She tames is meant to become sweet.

Her arms give like a wish-creeper and guide with bow, arrows and goad.

VERSE 3 · GOING DEEPER

AT DAWN I BOW TO HER FEET

From arms to feet. The hymn moves down: face, arms, now feet. In Indian devotion the feet are the most humble and the most precious part of the divine. To bow at someone's feet is to give up pride completely. The verb fits: namāmi, "I bow".

Always giving, always carrying. Her feet are bhakteṣṭa-dāna-nirata, ever intent on granting what devotees wish. And they are bhava-sindhu-pota, the boat across the ocean of worldly life, with its waves of birth, death, worry and loss. You don't have to swim across; you hold on to Her feet and are carried.

Worshipped by the gods, marked with signs. Padmāsanādi-sura-nāyaka-pūjanīya: Brahmā (the lotus-seated) and the lords of the gods worship these feet. Their soles bear auspicious marks (lāñchana): the lotus, the goad, the banner and the discus (sudarśana). Such signs on the soles are a traditional mark of divinity.

💡 Feet first

Many hymns describe the deity from feet to head (pādādi-keśānta) or head to feet (keśādi-pādānta). Here Śaṅkara goes face → arms → feet, then turns inward.

💡 Sudarśana

"Beautiful to see": the name of Viṣṇu's discus. On Lalitā's feet it appears as one of the auspicious marks.

Bowing at the feet is the oldest cure for an inflated ego. Every morning, one honest bow: "I can't cross this ocean alone." That admission is where real strength begins.

ZAPPY: Marks on the soles of Her feet?

PROF. BOLT: Lotus, goad, banner, discus. Sacred feet carry signs, like a seal on a royal letter.

Her feet are the boat. Hold on to them and the ocean of worries becomes crossable.

VERSE 4 · GOING DEEPER

AT DAWN I PRAISE HER TRUE NATURE

From form to essence. Three verses described Her form. Now Śaṅkara, the great teacher of Vedānta, praises what She truly is. Para-śivā, the supreme auspicious one; Bhavānī, a name of Pārvatī. Her glory is trayyanta-vedya, known through the end (anta) of the three Vedas (trayī): the Upaniṣads.

Compassion and cause. She is karuṇānavadyā, flawless in compassion, and viśvasya sṛṣṭi-vilaya-sthiti-hetu-bhūtā, the cause behind the creation, dissolution and preservation of the universe. She is also vidyeśvarī, Queen of Knowledge, a fitting title for the Goddess of Śrī Vidyā.

The humble ending. After all that, the verse ends: nigama-vāṅ-manasāti-dūrā, far beyond scripture, speech and mind. Śaṅkara, the master of words, admits that words can't capture Her. That's not defeat; it's honesty. The praise is offered knowing it can only point.

💡 Trayī

"The three": the Ṛg, Yajus and Sāma Vedas. Trayyanta = "the end of the three", another way of saying Vedānta, the Upaniṣads.

💡 Beyond speech and mind

The phrase echoes the Taittirīya Upaniṣad, which says words and mind turn back without reaching the Absolute.

Some things are bigger than our understanding, and that's all right. Praise doesn't have to explain. A morning of wonder, even about what we can't grasp, keeps the mind humble and open.

ZAPPY: So after the face, arms and feet, now the philosophy?

PROF. BOLT: Now the truth behind the form. And then he admits: even this can't fully reach Her.

She is the cause of everything, and still beyond words and thought. Praise Her anyway.

VERSE 5 · GOING DEEPER

AT DAWN I SPEAK HER NAMES

When the mind falls silent, the tongue speaks. Verse 4 ended with "beyond speech and mind". Verse 5 answers beautifully: prātar vadāmi, "at dawn I say" Your holy names. If She can't be fully thought, She can still be called. Names are how love reaches what understanding can't.

The names. Kāmeśvarī, consort of Kāmeśvara (Śiva in the Śrī Vidyā tradition). Kamalā, the lotus one: a name of Lakṣmī. Maheśvarī, great ruler. Śrī Śāmbhavī, the power of Śambhu (Śiva). Jagatāṃ jananī, Mother of the worlds. Parā, the Supreme. Vāg-devatā, Goddess of Speech: Sarasvatī's role. Tripureśvarī, ruler of the three cities.

One Mother. In a single verse we hear Lakṣmī (Kamalā), Sarasvatī (Vāgdevatā) and Pārvatī (Śāmbhavī, Kāmeśvarī), all as names of Lalitā. It's the same vision as verse 10 of Kanakadhārā: different names, one Goddess.

💡 Tripura

"Three cities": read as the three worlds, the three states of waking, dream and deep sleep, or the three bodies. Tripurasundarī is the beauty who rules all three.

💡 Lalitā Sahasranāma

The famous "thousand names of Lalitā" takes this idea to its fullest: a thousand names, chanted daily by millions.

Saying a sacred name out loud at the start of the day is a small act with a big effect. It sets the tone of the voice for everything else we'll say that day.

ZAPPY: Kamalā is Lakṣmī. Vāgdevatā is Sarasvatī. In one verse?

PROF. BOLT: Same as Kanakadhārā verse 10. Śaṅkara keeps saying it: one Mother, many names.

When thinking runs out, say Her names. The tongue can reach where the mind can't.

PHALAŚRUTI · GOING DEEPER

THE FRUIT: WHAT THE MORNING GIVES

Why "five gems". Pañca means five and ratna means gem. The hymn calls itself a śloka-pañcaka, a set of five verses. This sixth verse, the phalaśruti, describes the fruit of reciting them. It's also known as Lalitā Pañcakam.

A playful word. Śaṅkara calls the five verses sulalitam, very graceful, a pun on the Goddess's own name, Lalitā. The hymn is as charming as the one it praises. It is saubhāgya-da, giver of good fortune, and is to be recited prabhāte, at daybreak.

Four gifts. She is jhaṭiti prasannā, quickly pleased, and gives four things: vidyā (knowledge), śrī (prosperity), vimala-saukhya (pure, untainted happiness) and ananta-kīrti (endless renown). Notice "vimala": not just happiness, but happiness without stain, the kind that doesn't come at someone else's cost.

💡 Five verbs, five steps

Remember (1) → worship (2) → bow (3) → praise (4) → speak (5): mind, hands, head, heart, voice. A complete morning offering in five minutes.

💡 Śaṅkara's signature

Printed copies end by naming the author as Śaṅkara, "disciple of Śrī Govinda Bhagavatpāda", the traditional colophon of his works.

Five verses, once a day, at dawn. The promise isn't magic; it's what a mind that begins every day with beauty, humility, wonder and gratitude naturally grows into: wiser, richer in spirit, happier and well regarded.

ZAPPY: What does 'quickly pleased' mean?

PROF. BOLT: Jhaṭiti: straight away. Śaṅkara is saying She doesn't keep a devotee waiting.

Four gifts for five minutes at dawn: knowledge, prosperity, pure happiness, lasting good name.
