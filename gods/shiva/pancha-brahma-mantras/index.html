FOREWORD

Anyone who has stood near a Śiva temple during abhiṣeka has heard them: "Sadyojātaṃ prapadyāmi… Vāmadevāya namaḥ…" These are the Pañca-Brahma mantras, five short passages from the Veda, each addressed to one face of Śiva. Together they describe a God who creates, sustains, dissolves, conceals and, finally, blesses.

This small book opens up the five mantras in simple English: what each one says, what its key words mean, which face, direction and element it belongs to, and the teaching inside. It is for anyone who has heard these mantras chanted for years without knowing their meaning, and for anyone meeting them for the first time.

May every reader come away seeing the many faces of life, gentle and fierce, beginning and ending, as faces of one auspicious Reality. Om Namaḥ Śivāya.

ABOUT THE MANTRAS

Vedic, ancient, and still chanted daily

📜 Where they come from

The Taittirīya Āraṇyaka of the Kṛṣṇa Yajur Veda, in the part known as the Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad. Unlike Śaṅkara's hymns, these are Vedic: far older, and not credited to any one poet.

🛕 How they're used

Chanted during abhiṣeka (bathing the liṅga), in Rudra worship and at the start of Śiva pūjā, often as part of a longer recitation with Śrī Rudram.

Why "Brahma"? In Pañca-Brahma, brahma doesn't mean the creator god Brahmā. It means the Supreme Reality. The five mantras treat each face as a full expression of that one Reality.

A journey in five steps. Read in order, the mantras move through a whole cycle: birth and refuge (Sadyojāta), time and strength (Vāmadeva), the fierce fire that ends things (Aghora), meditation and inner guidance (Tatpuruṣa), and finally grace from above (Īśāna).

The great Sadāśiva at Elephanta. India's most famous image of these faces is the colossal three-faced Sadāśiva in the Elephanta caves near Mumbai: calm Tatpuruṣa at the centre, fierce Aghora on one side, gentle Vāmadeva on the other. The other two faces are understood to be behind and above.

SADYOJĀTA · GOING DEEPER

THE FACE OF BIRTH · WEST · EARTH

The face of beginnings. Sadyojāta means "born at once", "instantly manifest". In the widely followed scheme, it is Śiva's west face, linked with the element earth and with the act of creation. It is often pictured as white and gentle, like a new-born.

Refuge first. The mantra opens with prapadyāmi, "I take refuge, I surrender". Before any request, the worshipper places himself in Śiva's hands. Then the salutation, doubled for emphasis: namo namaḥ.

Beyond the cycle. Bhave bhave nātibhave bhajasva mām: in birth after birth, not into endless birth, be gracious to me. The prayer to the Lord of birth is, beautifully, to be freed from birth. It ends by saluting bhavodbhava, He from whom all becoming arises.

💡 Brahma ≠ Brahmā

In "Pañca-Brahma", brahma means the Supreme Reality, not Brahmā the four-faced creator. The five mantras are the five faces of that one Reality.

💡 Five elements, five faces

In the common scheme: Sadyojāta earth, Vāmadeva water, Aghora fire, Tatpuruṣa air, Īśāna space. Texts differ in some details.

Every day is a small birth: we wake into a new morning. Beginning it with "I take refuge" sets the tone. Whatever we create that day, we create from a place of trust, not fear.

ZAPPY: Why start with a face called 'born at once'?

PROF. BOLT: Because everything begins somewhere. The first face is the beginning of every beginning.

The first prayer is the oldest one: "I take refuge." Then: lead me beyond the cycle of births.

VĀMADEVA · GOING DEEPER

THE GRACEFUL FACE · NORTH · WATER

The gracious face. Vāma means lovely, pleasing (and also "left"). Vāmadeva is the north face, linked with water and with preservation: the gentle, sustaining aspect. At Elephanta, the face identified as Vāmadeva is serene and soft-featured.

Time and the parts. He is jyeṣṭha (eldest), śreṣṭha (best), rudra and kāla, Time itself. Kala-vikaraṇa: He who differentiates the kalās, the parts and phases of creation. Everything that changes, changes through Him.

Strength and the mind. Bala-vikaraṇa, bala, bala-pramathana: He varies strength, is strength, and crushes strength when it becomes arrogance. Sarva-bhūta-damana: He tames all beings. And finally manonmana: He who raises the mind beyond the mind, the state of stillness yogis aim for.

💡 The longest mantra

Vāmadeva's is the longest of the five, built as a chain of namaḥ salutations, each naming a power.

💡 Manonmanī

Yoga texts use unmanī, "beyond the mind", for deep meditative stillness. Manonmana names Śiva as the One who leads there.

Strength, time and the restless mind are all in His hands. When strength turns into pride, or time feels short, or the mind won't settle, this mantra is a reminder of who holds all three.

ZAPPY: That's a long list of 'namaḥ'!

PROF. BOLT: Eleven bows in one breath. Each one names a power. The last one is the best: the mind lifted beyond itself.

Śiva as Time and Strength, and the One who tames both, until the mind goes beyond itself.

AGHORA · GOING DEEPER

THE FIERCE FACE · SOUTH · FIRE

A puzzling name. Aghora literally means "not terrible", yet the south face is the fierce one, linked with fire and with dissolution. Tradition explains: His fierceness is terrible only to what must end, to ignorance, ego and evil. To the devotee, it is protection.

Gentle, terrible, more terrible. The mantra salutes all three: aghora (gentle), ghora (terrible) and ghora-ghoratara (more terrible than the terrible). It refuses to divide Śiva into comfortable and uncomfortable parts. All of them are bowed to.

Everywhere. Sarvebhyaḥ sarva-śarvebhyaḥ: to all of them, O Śarva, in every form. Namas te astu rudra-rūpebhyaḥ: salutations to Your forms as Rudra. Fire burns, but it also cooks, warms and purifies. The fierce face is that fire.

💡 Elephanta

The great Sadāśiva at Elephanta shows three faces: Tatpuruṣa at the centre, the fierce Aghora (Bhairava) on one side and the gentle Vāmadeva on the other.

💡 Two readings

Some versions read the second line as aghora-ghoretarebhyaḥ and the third as sarvataḥ śarva sarvebhyo. The meaning stays the same.

Life brings both gentle and hard days. Bowing to the fierce face is accepting that endings, losses and hard lessons can also be grace, the fire that clears space for something new.

ZAPPY: A face called 'not terrible' that's actually fierce?

PROF. BOLT: That's the trick. Aghora means 'not terrible'. His fierceness only looks terrible to what needs to end.

The gentle and the terrible are one Śiva. Bow to all His forms, not just the comfortable ones.

TATPURUṢA · GOING DEEPER

THE MEDITATIVE FACE · EAST · AIR

The central face. Tatpuruṣa, "that Person", the Supreme, faces east, toward the rising sun. It is linked with air and, in the five-act scheme, with concealment (tirobhāva): the power that veils the infinite so the finite world can exist. It is usually shown calm and meditative.

The Gāyatrī shape. This mantra follows the pattern of the famous Gāyatrī: vidmahe (may we know), dhīmahi (may we meditate), pracodayāt (may He inspire). It's known as the Rudra Gāyatrī. Many deities have Gāyatrīs in this form.

Know, meditate, be moved. Three steps: first understand who Śiva is, then hold Him steadily in meditation, then let Him set the mind in motion. Pracodayāt is the key word: the prayer is not for things but for a mind guided from within.

💡 Facing east

Śiva-liṅga worship traditionally places Tatpuruṣa facing east; Elephanta is unusual in turning it north.

💡 Gāyatrī metre

24 syllables in three lines of eight. The Rudra Gāyatrī borrows the shape of the original Sāvitrī Gāyatrī of the Ṛg Veda.

A guided mind is worth more than any single answer. Asking each morning to be "set in motion" by the highest is a simple way to begin the day facing east, toward the light.

ZAPPY: This one sounds like the Gāyatrī!

PROF. BOLT: It is a Gāyatrī: the Rudra Gāyatrī. Same shape: know, meditate, may He inspire us.

Know Him, meditate on Him, and let Him move your mind: the Rudra Gāyatrī.

ĪŚĀNA · GOING DEEPER

THE UPWARD FACE · SKY · SPACE

The face that looks up. Īśāna, the Ruler, is the fifth face. The other four look out to the four directions; Īśāna looks upward, beyond them all. It is linked with space (ākāśa), the subtlest element, and with grace (anugraha), the act that frees.

Lord of everything. Īśānaḥ sarva-vidyānām: Ruler of all knowledge. Īśvaraḥ sarva-bhūtānām: Lord of all beings. Brahmādhipatir brahmaṇo'dhipatiḥ: master of brahman (the Veda) and master of Brahmā himself. Tradition says four faces revealed the Vedas and Īśāna the Āgamas.

A simple wish. After all that grandeur, the request is small and personal: śivo me astu, may He be auspicious, kind, to me. And then the closing name, sadāśivom: He is Sadāśiva, the ever-auspicious, sealed with Om.

💡 In the Veda

Wikipedia notes an early mention of Īśāna in this very mantra, found in the Taittirīya Āraṇyaka (10.21.1).

💡 Five acts

Creation, preservation, dissolution, concealment and grace: the five acts (pañca-kṛtya) of Śiva, one for each face in the common scheme.

The last word of all five mantras is grace. However the day goes, the closing wish is the same: may the Highest be kind to me. Ending with that thought is ending in peace.

ZAPPY: Why does this face look up instead of out?

PROF. BOLT: The other four look after the world in four directions. This one looks beyond it.

The highest face is grace: the One beyond all directions, asked simply to be kind to me.
