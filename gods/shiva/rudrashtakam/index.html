Tulasīdāsa's eight verses to Śiva · explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: First the whole hymn for chanting in one go. Then the story and background, and two pages for each verse: the chant, a picture, what it says and the key words; then a deeper look, background facts, the inner teaching and the one thing to remember.

FOREWORD

"Namāmīśam īśāna nirvāṇarūpam…" In North India especially, few Śiva hymns are sung as often or with as much feeling. Its rolling rhythm stays in the ear; its words go from the highest philosophy to the most personal cry for help.

The surprise is where it comes from. The Rudrāṣṭakam was written by Gosvāmī Tulasīdāsa, the great poet of Lord Rāma, and placed inside his Rāmacaritamānas. And in that book, it is sung by a guru, for a proud student who had insulted him. It is a prayer of forgiveness as much as praise.

Its eighth verse may be the most comforting line in all devotional poetry: "I know no yoga, no japa, no pūjā; I only bow to You." May every reader find that bowing is enough.

THE STORY · THE GURU'S PRAYER

From the Rāmacaritamānas, as Kākabhuśuṇḍi tells it
The story in 5 frames
A proud devotee. In Ujjain long ago, a young devotee of Śiva studied under a kind, learned guru. But he was arrogant, and looked down on others. His guru gently warned him, again and again.
The insult. One day, as the young man sat chanting in Śiva's temple, his guru walked in. Out of pride, he did not rise or even greet him. The gentle guru said nothing.
The curse. But Śiva could not bear it. A voice thundered from the shrine: for this arrogance, become a serpent, and suffer a thousand births.
The guru prays. Horrified, the guru, the very man who had been insulted, folded his hands and sang this hymn, the Rudrāṣṭakam, begging Śiva to show mercy to his student.
Grace. Śiva was pleased. The curse ran its course, but without suffering and with knowledge intact. In a later life the student became Kākabhuśuṇḍi, the crow-sage devoted to Rāma.

The place

Ujjain, city of Mahākāla.

The fault

Pride before one's guru.

The cure

A guru's forgiveness, sung.

ZAPPY: The guru prayed for the student who insulted him?

PROF. BOLT: That's why Śiva was so moved. The hymn is beautiful, but the forgiveness behind it is what was heard.

The Rudrāṣṭakam was born as a prayer of forgiveness, sung for someone else.

ABOUT THE RUDRĀṢṬAKAM

A Sanskrit jewel inside a Hindi epic

✍️ The poet

Gosvāmī Tulasīdāsa (16th century), who wrote the Rāmacaritamānas in Avadhī Hindi, mostly at Vārāṇasī.

📜 Where it's found

Rāmacaritamānas, Uttara Kāṇḍa, in Kākabhuśuṇḍi's account of his past life. One of the few passages in the epic written in Sanskrit.

The shape. Eight verses (an aṣṭakam) and a closing verse. Verses 1–2 describe the formless Absolute; 3–5 paint Śiva's form; 6–8 turn into personal prayer: prasīda, "be gracious", and pāhi, "protect me".

The music. It is in bhujaṅgaprayāta, "the serpent's movement", a rolling twelve-syllable metre, which is why it is so easy to sing and so often set to music.

Where you'll hear it. Sung at Śiva temples, especially in North India and at Kāśī, on Mondays, in Śrāvaṇa and on Mahāśivarātri, and at the close of Rāmāyaṇa recitals.

VERSE 1 · GOING DEEPER

THE FORMLESS LORD OF ALL

Starting with the Absolute. Count the "beyond" words: nirvāṇa, nirguṇa, nirvikalpa, nirīha. The hymn begins by saying what Śiva is not: not limited by form, quality, thought or desire. This is pure Vedānta, the same Śiva as our Nirvāṇa Ṣaṭkam.

The sky. Cidākāśam ākāśavāsam: the sky of consciousness, who dwells in the sky, or who wears the sky as his clothes (digambara). Space contains everything and is stained by nothing; so is Śiva.

A Rāma devotee sings Śiva. Tulasīdāsa is the great poet of Rāma. Yet in the middle of his Rāma epic he places this Sanskrit hymn to Śiva, in the deepest philosophical language. For him, there was no rivalry: Śiva is Rāma's own beloved devotee and Lord.

💡 Bhujaṅgaprayāta

The metre, "the movement of a serpent": twelve syllables a line in a rolling da-DUM-DUM rhythm. That's why it is so easy to sing.

💡 Īśāna

"The Ruler": also the upward, fifth face of Śiva in our Pañca-Brahma Mantras.

Before picturing God, remember He is bigger than any picture. The sky holds every cloud and is stained by none.

ZAPPY: A god with no form, no qualities, no desire... how do I picture that?

PROF. BOLT: Like the sky: everywhere, holding everything, touched by nothing. Tulasīdāsa starts there before he paints any picture.

Before the snake and the moon: Śiva as the sky of consciousness.

Śiva is the root of Oṃ, beyond words and senses, the death of Time itself: terrible, yet compassionate.

VERSE 2 · GOING DEEPER

OṂ, TIME, AND THE FAR SHORE

Oṃ and the Fourth. The Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad teaches that the three sounds of Oṃ (A-U-M) are waking, dreaming and deep sleep, and the silence after them is turīya, "the Fourth", pure awareness. Tulasīdāsa says Śiva is the root of Oṃ and that Fourth itself.

Mahākāla. Mahākāla-kāla: the death even of Time. It's also a pointer to Ujjain's Mahākāla temple, where this hymn's story takes place.

Karāla, kṛpāla. "Terrible" and "compassionate" rhyme in the same line. Tulasīdāsa loves such sound-pairs. The meaning is serious: the same Śiva who destroys is the one who cares.

💡 Mahākāleśvara

The jyotirliṅga at Ujjain, famous for its dawn bhasma-ārati with sacred ash.

💡 Māṇḍūkya

The shortest major Upaniṣad, entirely about Oṃ and the Fourth state.

What frightens us in life is often what frees us. The power that ends things is also the one that cares.

ZAPPY: Terrible AND compassionate?

PROF. BOLT: Terrible to our ego and illusions, kind to us. Like a surgeon: the knife looks frightening, the purpose is healing.

Beyond words, senses and Time itself: terrible to ignorance, kind to us.

VERSE 3 · GOING DEEPER

FAIR AS THE SNOWY MOUNTAIN

From formless to form. After two verses on the formless (nirguṇa), Tulasīdāsa paints the Śiva with form (saguṇa) that devotees love. Both are true; the form is the formless made approachable.

The Gaṅgā. When King Bhagīratha brought the heavenly Gaṅgā down to earth, her fall would have shattered the world. Śiva caught her in his matted locks and let her flow gently out. Hence Gaṅgādhara, "Bearer of the Gaṅgā".

Moon and serpent. The moon god, cursed to waste away, took shelter on Śiva's head, and still waxes and wanes there. The serpent Vāsuki coils at his throat: danger worn as an ornament.

💡 Gaṅgādhara

"Bearer of the Gaṅgā": one of Śiva's best-loved names, and a favourite theme of temple sculpture.

💡 Candraśekhara

"Moon-crested": the crescent's shelter on Śiva's head is linked to the Somnāth jyotirliṅga.

The infinite takes a form so we can love it. Use the form, but remember what it points to.

ZAPPY: Now we get the picture!

PROF. BOLT: Verses 1 and 2 said He has no form. Verse 3 paints one anyway, for those of us who need a face to love.

Gaṅgā in His hair, the moon on His brow, a snake at His throat: a form for the heart.

VERSE 4 · GOING DEEPER

THE KIND FACE OF THE LORD OF ALL

The blue throat. At the churning of the ocean, the first thing to rise was hālāhala, a poison that could destroy the world. Śiva drank it to save everyone; Pārvatī held his throat so it wouldn't go down, and it stayed there, blue. Nīlakaṇṭha is compassion in one word.

Fierce dress, gentle face. Skull garland, animal skin, serpents: frightening ornaments. But the face is prasanna, cheerful, and the heart dayāla, kind. The ornaments show mastery over death and over our animal nature; the face shows how He feels about us.

Priya. "Dear". After all the grand titles, Tulasīdāsa calls Śiva simply dear: the Lord of all, and also my own.

💡 Hālāhala

The world-killing poison from the churning of the ocean, which Śiva drank to save creation.

💡 Śaṅkara

"Maker of peace" (śam-kara): the same name we met in the Rudram's eighth anuvāka.

Take on hardship so others don't have to, and keep smiling. That's what the blue throat teaches.

ZAPPY: A skull garland and a happy face?

PROF. BOLT: Death hangs around His neck, and He's smiling. He has made friends with mortality.

Skull garland, smiling face: He wears death lightly.

VERSE 5 · GOING DEEPER

THE TRIDENT THAT ENDS THREE PAINS

A pun on śūla. Śūlapāṇi means "trident in hand". Trayaḥśūla means the three sufferings, since śūla also means sharp pain. The weapon that pierces is the one that ends our piercing pain.

The three sufferings. Tradition counts three: ādhyātmika (from our own body and mind), ādhibhautika (from other beings) and ādhidaivika (from nature and fate). It's why "śāntiḥ" is said three times at the end of prayers.

Bhāvagamya. "Reached through feeling". Not by learning, wealth or elaborate ritual, but by bhāva, heartfelt devotion. This is the heart of Tulasīdāsa's bhakti.

💡 Tāpatraya

The three sufferings, the reason for the triple oṃ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ.

💡 Bhavānī

A name of Pārvatī, "wife of Bhava". Her temple at Tuljāpur was dear to Śivājī.

Feeling, not cleverness, is the road to the divine. Bring your heart, not just your head.

ZAPPY: Why a trident?

PROF. BOLT: Three prongs for three kinds of pain: from our own body and mind, from other beings, and from nature and fate. One weapon uproots all three.

Three prongs for three kinds of pain, and the road to Him is love.

VERSE 6 · GOING DEEPER

PRASĪDA, PRASĪDA

The ka-sound. Kalātīta kalyāṇa kalpāntakārī: three "ka" words in a row. Tulasīdāsa's Sanskrit rings like a bell; the sound itself is part of the worship.

Purārī. The three flying cities of the demons, of gold, silver and iron, could be destroyed only when they lined up, once in a thousand years. At that moment Śiva shot one arrow and destroyed all three: Tripurāntaka.

Manmathārī. When Kāma, the god of desire, shot his flower-arrow to break Śiva's meditation, Śiva opened his third eye and burnt him to ash. Desire burnt, delusion (moha) removed: and then, at last, the poet pleads: prasīda, prasīda.

💡 Tripura

The three cities were built by the demon architect Maya; their destruction is one of Śiva's great deeds in the Purāṇas.

💡 Anaṅga

After being burnt, Kāma lived on without a body, "Anaṅga": desire that works invisibly.

There's a time to describe and a time to simply ask. Real prayer ends in "please".

ZAPPY: Why say 'prasīda' twice?

PROF. BOLT: That's the turning point of the hymn. The poet stops describing and starts pleading: be pleased, be pleased.

From describing to pleading: prasīda, prasīda, be gracious to me.

VERSE 7 · GOING DEEPER

NO PEACE WITHOUT HIS FEET

As long as… so long. Na yāvat … na tāvat: "as long as not… so long not". A firm, logical shape: without this, no that. It's the strongest claim in the hymn.

The feet. In Indian tradition, bowing at someone's feet is total humility and trust. The feet are also the part we can reach: we may not understand the Absolute, but we can hold the feet.

In all beings. The verse ends sarvabhūtādhivāsa, "dwelling in all beings". So worshipping His feet includes respecting every living being, the same lesson as the Śrī Rudram, which finds Rudra in everyone.

💡 Pādāravinda

"Lotus feet": bowing at the feet is the gesture of complete surrender.

💡 Umānātha

Umā is Pārvatī. Her penance to win Śiva is told in Kālidāsa's Kumārasambhava.

If peace keeps slipping away, check what you're anchored to. Anchor deeper.

ZAPPY: No peace at all without Him? That's strong.

PROF. BOLT: Peace that depends on circumstances comes and goes. Lasting peace needs an anchor deeper than circumstances.

Happiness and peace need an anchor deeper than circumstances.

VERSE 8 · GOING DEEPER

I KNOW NO YOGA, NO JAPA, NO PŪJĀ

The most quoted verse. Na jānāmi yogaṃ japaṃ naiva pūjām: "I know no yoga, no japa, not even pūjā". In the story, it's sung by a learned guru, who certainly knew all three. Before God, even the expert is a beginner.

Burning again and again. Tātapyamāna is an intensive verb: scorched over and over by the flood of sorrows, birth after birth, old age after old age. It's the cry of every person who has been through hard times.

Refuge. Āpanna: one in distress who comes for shelter. This is śaraṇāgati, surrender, the simplest and most complete form of devotion. When every method fails, taking refuge is still possible.

💡 Gītā 18.66

"Give up all methods and take refuge in Me alone": the same surrender.

💡 Āmīśa

"My Lord": the hymn ends on a personal note, not a title.

You don't need to be good at religion to be heard. Bow, ask for help, and that is prayer.

ZAPPY: He admits he doesn't know how to pray, in the middle of a prayer?

PROF. BOLT: That's the most honest line in the hymn. When you have no method left, bowing is enough.

No yoga, no japa, no pūjā: just bowing is enough.

PHALAŚRUTI · GOING DEEPER

THE GURU'S HYMN

The Brahmin. Vipreṇa, "by a Brahmin": in the Rāmacaritamānas, this is the guru of our story, who sang the hymn to save his proud student from Śiva's curse.

Prasīdati. "He is pleased". Verses 6 and 7 pleaded prasīda, "be pleased"; the phalaśruti answers: prasīdati, He is pleased. The prayer is heard.

Inside a Rāma epic. The story is told by Kākabhuśuṇḍi, the crow-sage, to Garuḍa, near the end of the Rāmacaritamānas. The proud student of the story is Kākabhuśuṇḍi himself, in an earlier life.

💡 Kākabhuśuṇḍi

The crow-sage of the Rāmacaritamānas, an immortal devotee of Rāma who narrates the epic to Garuḍa.

💡 Hara

"The one who takes away": Śiva removes sin, sorrow and delusion.

The most powerful prayers are often said for others, even for those who have hurt us.

ZAPPY: Who's the Brahmin?

PROF. BOLT: The guru in our story, praying for the rude student who had insulted him. That's why Śiva listened.

A hymn sung for someone else: the guru's prayer for his student.

THE WHOLE HYMN ON ONE PAGE

From the formless sky to "I just bow"
Verses 1–2: the formless. Liberation itself, beyond qualities and thought; root of Oṃ, the Fourth, the death of Time; terrible, yet compassionate.
Verses 3–5: the form. Snow-white, Gaṅgā in His hair, moon and serpents; a smiling face and blue throat; the trident that ends three pains; reached through love.
Verses 6–8: the prayer. Be gracious, be gracious; no peace without Your feet; I know no yoga, japa or pūjā, I only bow: protect me.
Phalaśruti. Sung by a guru for his student; whoever recites it with devotion, Śambhu is pleased.
