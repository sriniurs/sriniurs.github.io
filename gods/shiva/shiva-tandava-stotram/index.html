ŚIVA TĀṆḌAVA
STOTRAM

Rāvaṇa's thundering hymn to the dancing Śiva · explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: First the whole hymn for chanting in one go. Then the story and background, and two pages for each verse: the chant, a picture, what it says and the key words; then a deeper look, background facts, the inner teaching and the one thing to remember.

FOREWORD

"Jaṭāṭavīgalajjala…" Even people who know no Sanskrit recognise it: the pounding rhythm, the drum-sounds ḍamaḍ-ḍamaḍ, the crackle of fire dhagad-dhagad, the beat of the mṛdaṅga dhimid-dhimid. The Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotram doesn't just describe Śiva's dance; it is the dance, in sound.

Tradition says it was sung by Rāvaṇa, the ten-headed king of Laṅkā, pinned under Mount Kailāsa by Śiva's toe. A proud king, a great scholar and musician, and one of Śiva's greatest devotees. And the surprise is how the hymn ends: after all the thunder, he longs for a still mind, an equal eye toward friend and foe, and a quiet life by the Gaṅgā, chanting "Śiva".

May every reader feel the dance, and find the stillness at its heart.

THE STORY · RĀVAṆA UNDER KAILĀSA

From the Uttara Kāṇḍa of the Rāmāyaṇa, and tradition
The story in 5 frames
A chariot stops. Flying home in his aerial chariot, the Puṣpaka, Rāvaṇa finds it can't pass over a mountain. It is Kailāsa, Śiva's home. Nandi, Śiva's attendant, tells him to turn back.
A careless insult. Rāvaṇa laughs at Nandi's monkey-like face. Nandi warns him: one day monkeys will bring about your ruin. (Hanumān and the monkey army would.)
The lift. Furious, Rāvaṇa thrusts his twenty arms under the mountain and begins to lift Kailāsa itself. The mountain shakes; Pārvatī is startled.
One toe. Śiva, unbothered, presses down with one toe. The mountain settles, crushing Rāvaṇa's arms beneath it. He roars in pain so loudly the worlds tremble; that roar, says the Rāmāyaṇa, gave him the name Rāvaṇa, "the one who roars".
The song. Trapped, Rāvaṇa praises Śiva, and tradition says this is the hymn he sang. Pleased, Śiva frees him and gives him the sword Candrahāsa.

The singer

Rāvaṇa, the ten-headed king.

The lesson

Pride crushed by one toe.

The gift

The sword Candrahāsa.

ZAPPY: Śiva forgave Rāvaṇa?

PROF. BOLT: He crushed the pride and loved the devotee. Rāvaṇa's later choices were his own; but this song stayed pure.

Pinned by pride, freed by praise: the song came from a broken ego.

ABOUT THE ŚIVA TĀṆḌAVA STOTRAM

A hymn you can hear dancing

✍️ The singer

Attributed to Rāvaṇa, king of Laṅkā: a great scholar, musician and devotee of Śiva, remembered also as a master of the vīṇā.

🎵 The sound

The pañcacāmara metre, short-long, short-long, sixteen syllables a line; full of sound-words: ḍamaḍ, dhagad, dhimid.

The shape. Verses 1–2 open the dance; 3–8 paint Śiva: Pārvatī's love, the serpent-gems, the gods bowing, the fire that burnt desire, the dark throat; 9–10 list the seven enemies He conquered; 11 is the dance at full speed; 12–13 turn to stillness and longing; 14–15 are the closing verses.

Why it's loved. It's a hymn for the ear. Its relentless rhythm and alliteration make it thrilling to chant fast, and it's a favourite at Mahāśivarātri and in classical dance.

When it's chanted. Traditionally at pradoṣa, evening twilight, at the end of Śiva worship, as its own closing verse says.

VERSE 1 · GOING DEEPER

THE DANCE BEGINS

Sound you can hear. Ḍamaḍḍamaḍḍamaḍḍaman: the words copy the drum. The Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotram is famous for this. It is written in pañcacāmara, a metre of alternating short and long syllables that beats like a march. Read it aloud and you hear the dance.

The tāṇḍava. Śiva's cosmic dance, which destroys and renews the universe. As Naṭarāja, the Lord of Dance, he dances within a ring of fire, one foot on the demon of ignorance, his ḍamaru sounding creation and his fire dissolving it.

The blessing. The verse ends tanotu naḥ śivaḥ śivam, a play on words: "may Śiva spread śivam (auspiciousness) for us". The fierce dancer is the giver of blessings.

💡 Naṭarāja

Śiva as Lord of Dance: the famous bronze image from Tamil Nadu, and the presiding deity of Chidambaram.

💡 Pañcacāmara

The metre: sixteen syllables a line, short-long, short-long: a war-drum rhythm.

Even destruction can be a dance. When old things fall, something new is being made.

ZAPPY: Why does it sound like a drum?

PROF. BOLT: It's meant to! 'Ḍamaḍ-ḍamaḍ-ḍamaḍ' copies the ḍamaru. The whole hymn is written to be heard as much as understood.

Gaṅgā flowing, serpents swaying, drum sounding: the dance begins.

VERSE 2 · GOING DEEPER

FIRE ON THE FOREHEAD, MOON IN THE HAIR

A cauldron of hair. Jaṭā-kaṭāha: the matted hair is pictured as a huge cauldron in which the Gaṅgā whirls round and round (sambhrama-bhramat) before flowing out. It's a picture of the river's fall from heaven, caught and tamed.

Dhagad-dhagad. The sound of fire flaring: the third eye's fire on the broad plate of the forehead. In the same verse: cool water above, fire below, and the gentle young moon.

Love every moment. Ratiḥ pratikṣaṇaṃ mama: "my love, every moment". Rāvaṇa's hymn is full of awe, but it's also a love song.

💡 Bhagīratha

The king whose penance brought the Gaṅgā down; Śiva caught her in his hair to soften her fall.

💡 Kiśora-candra

"The young moon": the crescent of the second or third day.

Let wonder grow into love. Awe at something great becomes devotion when it turns personal.

ZAPPY: 'Dhagad-dhagad' again?

PROF. BOLT: Now it's the sound of fire crackling on His forehead. Rāvaṇa hears the dance as well as sees it.

Water in His hair, fire on His brow: my love for Him, every moment.

VERSE 3 · GOING DEEPER

THE KIND GLANCE

Pārvatī's grace. Dharādharendra-nandinī, "daughter of the king of mountains". Śiva's mind delights in her playful grace, and in the whole shining expanse of the universe.

The kind glance. Kṛpā-kaṭākṣa-dhoraṇī: a stream of compassionate sideways glances. Indian devotion often says one glance of grace is enough; here it stops troubles that seem impossible to bear.

Delight. Mano vinodam etu, "may my mind find delight". This refrain appears in verses 3 and 4: the hymn asks not only to worship but to enjoy the Lord.

💡 Kaṭākṣa

A sideways glance: in Indian poetry the look of grace, as in Śaṅkara's Kanakadhārā Stotram to Lakṣmī.

💡 Digambara

"Sky-clad": wearing only the directions.

Grace often comes as a glance: a small kindness at the right time. Give those glances freely.

ZAPPY: Can a glance really stop trouble?

PROF. BOLT: Rāvaṇa thinks so. One kind look from Śiva, he says, holds back even unbearable disasters.

One kind glance holds back even the hardest troubles.

VERSE 4 · GOING DEEPER

LORD OF ALL BEINGS

Painting the sky. The serpents in His tawny hair have glowing gems on their hoods. Their light paints the faces of the four directions, pictured as young women (dig-vadhū), with saffron, like the colours of dawn.

The elephant hide. Madāndha-sindhura: an elephant blind with rage. Śiva killed the elephant-demon Gajāsura and wore its hide, a story behind his name Gajāntaka, which returns in verses 9 and 10.

Bhūta-bhartṛ. "Lord, supporter of all beings": the one who carries everything that exists.

💡 Gajāsura

The elephant-demon Śiva slew and whose hide he wears: told at Kāśī's Kṛttivāseśvara shrine.

💡 Dig-vadhū

"Maidens of the directions": a favourite poetic picture of the sky.

Let wonder in. A mind that can still be amazed stays young and open.

ZAPPY: The directions have faces?

PROF. BOLT: Poets picture the four directions as young women. The glow from the serpent-gems paints their faces saffron, like dawn.

Serpent-gems light the sky: the whole world glows around Him.

VERSE 5 · GOING DEEPER

THE DUST ON HIS FEET

Pollen on the footstool. Indra and all the gods bow so often that the flowers on their crowns shed pollen (prasūna-dhūli), greying Śiva's footstool. It's a vivid way to say: everyone worships Him.

Serpent hair-tie. Bhujaṅga-rāja-mālayā nibaddha-jāṭa-jūṭakaḥ: His matted hair is tied up with the king of serpents. Danger as a hair band.

The cakora's friend. The cakora is a bird said in poetry to live on moonlight. Its "friend" is the moon, on Śiva's head.

💡 Cakora

The chukar partridge; poets say it drinks only moonbeams, a symbol of longing.

💡 Sahasrākṣa

"Thousand-eyed": Indra, given a thousand eyes in an old story.

Bowing doesn't diminish anyone. Even the king of the gods grows greater by honouring what is greater.

ZAPPY: Pollen on His feet?

PROF. BOLT: The gods bow so often and so low that the flowers in their crowns dust His footstool with pollen. That's how much they worship Him.

The gods bow so low their flowers dust His feet.

VERSE 6 · GOING DEEPER

THE FIRE THAT BURNT DESIRE

The courtyard of fire. Lalāṭa-catvara: the forehead is a wide courtyard where fire (dhanañjaya) blazes. Its sparks "drank up" (nipīta) Kāma, the five-arrowed god of desire.

Indra bows. Naman-nilimpa-nāyaka: the leader of the gods bowing. Śiva is the Lord even of the king of heaven.

Mahākapālin. The "great skull-bearer": Śiva as the ascetic who carries a skull bowl, as in our Kāla Bhairava story. Rāvaṇa asks this fierce ascetic for prosperity (sampad): the one who owns nothing gives everything.

💡 Dhanañjaya

"Winner of wealth": a name for fire, and also for Arjuna.

💡 Kāma's revival

After he was burnt, Kāma lived on bodiless as Anaṅga, and was later reborn as Pradyumna, son of Kṛṣṇa.

Desire burnt by awareness doesn't vanish; it's transformed. Let wisdom be the fire that refines what you want.

ZAPPY: A courtyard on the forehead?

PROF. BOLT: A wide, flat space where the fire of the third eye burns: so wide that Kāma, desire himself, was swallowed in its sparks.

Sparks from the third eye swallowed desire itself.

VERSE 7 · GOING DEEPER

THE THREE-EYED ARTIST

Desire as an offering. Āhutī-kṛta: made into an oblation. Kāma isn't just destroyed; he is offered, like ghee poured into a sacred fire.

The artist. Citra-patraka-prakalpanaika-śilpin: the only artist who can paint decorative designs on Pārvatī. In classical poetry, lovers paint patterns of sandal and musk on each other. The same Śiva who burnt desire is the tender husband.

Both at once. Ascetic and lover, fire and tenderness: Śiva holds what seem to be opposites. The verse ends simply: trilocane ratir mama, "my love is for the three-eyed One".

💡 Patra-racanā

The classical art of drawing leaf and flower designs with sandal paste or musk, still echoed in mehendi.

💡 Ardhanārīśvara

Śiva and Pārvatī as one body: the deepest image of ascetic and lover united.

Discipline and love aren't enemies. Master desire, and love becomes pure.

ZAPPY: Fierce fire and drawing designs, in one verse?

PROF. BOLT: That's the point: the eye that burnt desire is the same Lord who lovingly decorates Pārvatī. Ascetic and lover at once.

The eye that burnt desire, the hand that decorates love: both are Śiva.

VERSE 8 · GOING DEEPER

DARK AS MONSOON CLOUDS

A monsoon image. Navīna-megha-maṇḍalī: banks of fresh monsoon clouds on a moonless night (kuhū-niśīthinī). That is how dark Śiva's throat is, from the poison he drank at the churning of the ocean.

Carrier of the world. Jagad-dhuraṃdhara: the one who carries the world's burden, like an ox carrying a yoke. The dark throat is the proof: he took the poison so the world wouldn't have to.

Lovely. Kalā-nidhāna-bandhura: beautiful with the moon, the "treasury of digits". Dark and bright together.

💡 Hālāhala

The poison from the churning: Śiva drank it and held it in his throat, which turned dark blue.

💡 Kuhū

The new-moon night: the darkest night of the month.

Sometimes love means carrying what others can't. The dark throat is a badge of care.

ZAPPY: Why is His neck so dark?

PROF. BOLT: From the poison He drank to save the world. Rāvaṇa compares it to the darkest monsoon night.

Dark-throated from the poison: He carries the world's burden.

VERSE 9 · GOING DEEPER

THE ONE WHO CUT THEM ALL DOWN

Blue lotuses. The dark throat again, now compared to fully blooming blue lotuses: gentler than the monsoon night of verse 8.

Seven conquests. Smara (Kāma, burnt), pura (the three flying cities, destroyed with one arrow), bhava (the cycle of worldly life), makha (Dakṣa's arrogant sacrifice), gaja (the elephant-demon), andhaka (the blind demon who lusted after Pārvatī), antaka (Death, for Mārkaṇḍeya).

The chant. Smaracchidaṃ puracchidaṃ bhavacchidaṃ makhacchidam: the "-cchidam" ("who cut") repeats like strokes of an axe. Chanted fast, it's thrilling.

💡 Andhaka

A blind demon (born of Śiva's sweat in one story) who lusted after Pārvatī; Śiva impaled him on his trident, and he became a devotee.

💡 Tripura

The three cities of gold, silver and iron, destroyed when they lined up once in a thousand years.

Every obstacle we fear has already been met by the divine. Name your fears, then name the One greater than all of them.

ZAPPY: 'Chidam, chidam, chidam': what's being cut?

PROF. BOLT: Seven things: desire, the demon cities, the cycle of birth, Dakṣa's arrogant sacrifice, the elephant demon, the demon Andhaka, and Death. Each has its own story.

Desire, demons, bondage, pride, Death: He cut them all down.

VERSE 10 · GOING DEEPER

THE BEE AT PĀRVATĪ'S FLOWERS

The bee. Madhuvrata, "whose vow is honey": a bee. Śiva is pictured as a bee drinking the nectar of the blossoms of Pārvatī's arts and graces. Pārvatī is called Sarvamaṅgalā, "all-auspicious".

The same list, a new word. Verse 9 used -cchidam, "who cut"; verse 10 repeats the seven with -antakam, "who ended". It ends antakāntakam: "ender of the Ender", the conqueror of Death.

From thunder to sweetness. The hymn swings between the fierce and the sweet: the destroyer of seven enemies is a bee at Pārvatī's flowers.

💡 Sarvamaṅgalā

"All-auspicious": a name of Pārvatī, used in the famous verse "sarvamaṅgala-māṅgalye".

💡 Antakāntaka

"Ender of the Ender": Śiva as Kālāntaka, who struck down Yama for Mārkaṇḍeya.

The strongest are often the sweetest. Power and tenderness belong together.

ZAPPY: Śiva is a bee?

PROF. BOLT: A bee drinking the sweetness of Pārvatī's grace. Then the verse repeats the list of conquests with a new word: antaka, 'ender'.

The bee at Pārvatī's flowers is the ender of Death itself.

VERSE 11 · GOING DEEPER

JAYA! THE DRUM AND THE DANCE

The climax. Jayatu: "Victory!" The dance reaches full speed: serpents whirl through the sky, their hissing breath (śvasat) fanning the forehead fire (havyavāṭ, "carrier of offerings").

Dhimid-dhimid. Now it's the deep drum, the mṛdaṅga, sounding dhimid-dhimid-dhimid. The verse ends where the hymn began: the fierce tāṇḍava. The whole hymn is framed by the dance.

Auspicious rhythm. Tuṅga-maṅgala-dhvani: the lofty, auspicious sound. Even at its fiercest, Śiva's dance is maṅgala, blessing.

💡 Mṛdaṅga

The double-headed drum of classical South Indian music; the tāṇḍava is often shown with Nandi playing it.

💡 Havyavāṭ

"Carrier of offerings": Agni, fire, which carries what we offer to the gods.

When life moves fast and loud, look for the rhythm in it. There's a dance even in chaos.

ZAPPY: This one sounds like a festival!

PROF. BOLT: It's the climax of the dance: serpents hissing, fire flaring, the drum going dhimid-dhimid, and Śiva dancing. Shout 'jaya'!

Dhimid-dhimid! The drum, the fire and the dance at full speed.

VERSE 12 · GOING DEEPER

THE EQUAL MIND

The turn. After eleven thunderous verses, the hymn goes quiet. Rāvaṇa, the king of Laṅkā, asks not for victory but for samatā, evenness of mind.

Six pairs. A rock and a beautiful bed; a serpent and a pearl necklace; precious jewels and clods of earth; friend and enemy; a blade of grass and lotus-like eyes; ordinary people and an emperor. To see all of them equally is the mark of a sage.

The Gītā's ideal. The Bhagavad Gītā describes the wise one as sama-loṣṭāśma-kāñcana: one to whom a clod, a stone and gold are the same (6.8; 14.24). Rāvaṇa's wish is the yogi's goal.

💡 Gītā 6.8

"To whom a clod of earth, a stone and gold are the same": the description of the steady yogi.

💡 Samatā

Equanimity: not indifference, but a calm that isn't shaken by gain or loss.

The deepest power is not owning more; it's not being shaken by what you own or lack.

ZAPPY: Rāvaṇa wanted an equal mind?

PROF. BOLT: After all the thunder, the hymn turns quiet. The greatest wish isn't power; it's to see rock and bed, friend and foe, with the same calm.

Rock or bed, friend or foe: when will my mind see them the same?

VERSE 13 · GOING DEEPER

WHEN WILL I BE HAPPY?

A simple dream. Nilimpa-nirjharī-nikuñja-koṭare vasan: living in a little hollow among the bushes by the Gaṅgā. The mighty king of Laṅkā dreams of a hermit's life.

The practice. Free of bad thoughts (vimukta-durmati); hands joined above the head (añjali); restless eyes made still (vimukta-lola-locana); attention fixed on the sacred mark on Śiva's forehead; and one mantra: śiva.

Kadā? "When?" Verses 12 and 13 both ask kadā, when. It's a longing, not a boast: the honest cry of someone who has everything and still isn't at peace.

💡 Añjali

Joined palms: held at the chest in greeting, and above the head in the deepest reverence.

💡 Kadā

"When?": the word of longing that ends many devotional hymns.

You can have everything and still want one quiet place and one sacred word. Make room for that while you can.

ZAPPY: The demon king dreams of a hut by a river?

PROF. BOLT: Strange, isn't it? The most powerful king of his age, longing for a simple life, a still mind and one word: Śiva.

Not a palace but a riverbank: happiness is a still mind chanting 'Śiva'.

VERSES 14–15 · GOING DEEPER

THE FRUIT OF CHANTING

Three ways. Paṭhan smaran bruvan: reading, remembering, reciting. Even remembering the hymn counts. Its fruit: lasting purity (viśuddhi) and quick devotion to Hara, the true guru. Nānyathā gatiḥ: there is no other way.

Pradoṣa. Verse 15 sets the time: pradoṣe, at evening twilight, at the end of worship. Pradoṣa is Śiva's special hour, when he is said to dance on Kailāsa, and the twice-monthly Pradoṣa vrata is devoted to him.

Daśavaktra. "The Ten-headed One": the closing verse signs the hymn with Rāvaṇa's name. His gift: sthirā lakṣmī, steady, smiling prosperity, with chariots, elephants and horses.

💡 Pradoṣa vrata

A fast kept on the thirteenth lunar day, twice a month, with Śiva worship at twilight.

💡 Other verses

Some versions add one or two more verses; this is the commonly sung text.

Remembering is a form of prayer too. Carry a few lines of this hymn in your head, and let them play at twilight.

ZAPPY: Why chant it at twilight?

PROF. BOLT: Pradoṣa, the evening twilight, is Śiva's special time, when he is said to dance. It's the traditional hour for this hymn.

Chant it at twilight, after worship: purity, devotion and steady prosperity.
