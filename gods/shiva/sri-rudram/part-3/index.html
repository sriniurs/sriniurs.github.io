"And for me": everything we ask for, from breath to a good day · explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: First the whole Camakam for chanting in one go. Then the story and background, and two pages for each anuvāka: the chant, a picture, what it says and the key words; then a deeper look, background facts, the inner teaching and the one thing to remember.

FOREWORD

In the Namakam we bowed, again and again, to the One in everything. Now the Rudram does something many people find surprising: it asks. Ca me, ca me, "and for me, and for me": breath and strength, truth and faith, food and a home, a good bed and a good day, rice and wheat and lentils, the whole universe on our side.

It is not a greedy list. It is the prayer of someone who has understood that everything already belongs to the Lord, and so asks him, like a child asks a parent, for what life needs. And by the end, it gives everything back: "may my life, my breath, my very self become an offering". It closes with a promise: "sweetly will I speak".

With this third part, the Śrī Rudram is complete. May its blessings, ca me, be every reader's.

THE STORY · THE STREAM OF WEALTH

The Vasordhārā, where the Camakam began
The Vedic ritual in 5 frames
The great altar. In the Agnicayana, the grandest Vedic ritual, a huge fire-altar is built from thousands of bricks, often in the shape of a bird.
Rudra is calmed. When it is done, the Śatarudrīya (the Namakam) is offered to calm Rudra's fierce power, as in Part 1's story.
The stream begins. Then a priest pours ghee into the fire from a long ladle in one unbroken stream. It is called the Vasordhārā, "the stream of wealth".
Ca me, ca me. As the ghee flows, the Camakam is chanted: "and for me, and for me". The stream must not break until the asking is complete.
It flows back. The idea: what we offer to the divine returns to us as blessing, like rising smoke that comes back as rain, and rain that comes back as grain.

The Veda

Taittirīya Saṃhitā 4.7, Kṛṣṇa Yajur Veda.

The ritual

Vasordhārā, the stream of ghee.

The refrain

ca me, "and for me", over 300 times.

ZAPPY: Why must the stream not break?

PROF. BOLT: Because grace shouldn't break either. The unbroken pour is a picture of a relationship that never stops: we offer, He gives, again and again.

The Camakam rides on an unbroken stream: offering going up, blessing coming down.

ABOUT THE CAMAKAM

The second half of the Śrī Rudram

📜 Where it's found

Taittirīya Saṃhitā 4.7 of the Kṛṣṇa Yajur Veda, after the Namakam (4.5). It opens with a verse to Agni and Viṣṇu.

🔁 How it's used

In Rudra Ekādaśinī, the Namakam is chanted 11 times and after each, one anuvāka of the Camakam, so the 11 anuvākas match the 11 Namakams.

Namakam and Camakam. The Namakam says namaḥ: I bow, You are in everything. The Camakam says ca me: and for me. First we recognise the One everywhere; then we ask Him for what life needs. One is surrender, the other is trust.

What it asks for. The 11 anuvākas move from the body (breath, senses, bones) to character (truth, faith, joy), to well-being (health, sleep, a good day), to food and materials, to the gods, to the ritual itself, and finally to life offered back as worship.

How it ends. Not with a request but with a vow: madhu vadiṣyāmi, "sweetly will I speak", and peace, three times.

ANUVĀKA 1 · GOING DEEPER

BREATH, SENSES AND A STRONG BODY

"And for me". Every item ends ca me: "and (may it be) for me". After eleven anuvākas of bowing to the divine in everything (the Namakam), the Camakam turns round and asks. It's the most honest kind of prayer: having seen that everything belongs to the Lord, we ask him for what we need.

The body first. The list starts with the person: food and energy, the three breaths (prāṇa, apāna, vyāna), mind, speech, eyes, ears, strength, vigour. Then āyuḥ ca me jarā ca me: long life, and a good old age. It doesn't ask never to grow old; it asks to grow old well.

Down to the bones. The anuvāka ends with the body in detail: limbs (aṅgāni), bones (asthāni), joints (parūṃṣi) and bodies (śarīrāṇi). Three thousand years ago, healthy joints were already on the prayer list.

💡 Agnāviṣṇū

The Camakam opens with a verse to Agni and Viṣṇu together: "may these songs make you grow; come with splendour and strength".

💡 Jarā

"Old age", asked for as a blessing: to live long enough to grow old is itself a gift.

Start with the body: breath, senses, strength. Caring for them is not selfish; it's the base everything else stands on.

ZAPPY: Bones and joints in a Vedic prayer?

PROF. BOLT: Strong bones, healthy joints, long life and a good old age. The ṛṣis asked for exactly what a body needs.

ca me, "and for me". The Camakam begins with the basics: breath, senses, mind, and a sound body.

ANUVĀKA 2 · GOING DEEPER

STANDING, TRUTH AND JOY

Outer and inner. The list mixes the outer (seniority, leadership, wealth, growth) with the inner (truth, faith, understanding, good judgement). The Camakam doesn't separate worldly success from spiritual life. It asks for both together.

Play and delight. krīḍā ca me modaś ca me: play, and delight. Right after truth and faith comes the wish for fun. A life of duty without joy isn't what the ṛṣis were praying for.

Past and future. jātaṃ ca me janiṣyamāṇaṃ ca me, what is born and what is yet to be; bhūtaṃ ca me bhaviṣyac ca me, the past and the future. Even time is asked for: may my past be good and my future too.

💡 Manyu

The same word as in the very first line of the Namakam, Rudra's "anger". Here it's asked for as righteous indignation, the energy to stand against wrong.

💡 Sumati

"Good judgement": the last word of the anuvāka. Of all the qualities, it is the one that steers the rest.

Ask for truth and for fun in the same breath. A good life has room for both.

ZAPPY: Play and delight? I thought prayers were serious.

PROF. BOLT: The Veda disagrees. Truth and faith, yes, but also fun and joy. A good life, not just a dutiful one.

Truth and faith, play and delight: a good life, not just a long one.

ANUVĀKA 3 · GOING DEEPER

PEACE, HEALTH AND A GOOD DAY

Peace first. The anuvāka opens śaṃ ca me mayaś ca me: peace and joy. The same śam is in Śambhu and Śaṅkara from Part 2. Having bowed to the giver of peace, we ask for that peace.

Health. ayakṣmaṃ ca me'nāmayac ca me jīvātuś ca me dīrghāyutvaṃ ca me: free of illness, free of disease, good health, long life. Four ways of saying "keep me well", one after the other.

The best ending. anamitraṃ ca me'bhayaṃ ca me sugaṃ ca me śayanaṃ ca me sūṣā ca me sudinaṃ ca me: no enemies, no fear, an easy way, a good bed, a good dawn, a good day. After all the grand requests, the anuvāka ends with the simplest happiness there is.

💡 Ṛtam · Amṛtam

Right order and immortality: the cosmic law that keeps things in balance, and the deathless that Part 2 ended on.

💡 Sīra · Laya

The plough and the harvest: sowing and reaping, effort and its reward.

Big wishes are fine, but don't forget the small ones: sleep well, wake to a good morning, have a good day.

ZAPPY: A good bed? Really?

PROF. BOLT: Ask anyone who hasn't slept well. Good sleep, a good morning, a good day: the Veda knew what matters.

From a good bed to a good dawn to a good day: sudinaṃ ca me.

ANUVĀKA 4 · GOING DEEPER

FOOD, MILK AND THE GRAINS OF INDIA

Kind speech is food. The anuvāka starts ūrk ca me sūnṛtā ca me: nourishment, and pleasant, truthful speech. Words are put alongside food. The right word at the right time nourishes as well as a meal.

Eating together. sagdhiś ca me sapītiś ca me: eating together, drinking together. Not just food, but the shared meal. The Veda knew that a family or a community is held together at the table.

The grain list. 💡 Akṣut

The raw materials. Stone, soil, hills, sand, trees; gold, iron, lead, tin, bronze, copper. This is what a civilisation is built from. The Camakam asks for the physical world in all its variety.

ANUVĀKA 6 · GOING DEEPER

EVERY GOD, WITH INDRA

The ritual pairing. In the fire-altar ritual, a series of offerings goes to each god together with Indra. This anuvāka lists them: "Agni and Indra for me, Soma and Indra for me…". It's a roll-call of the whole Vedic pantheon.

Up to the highest. The list climbs from the gods to the earth, the sky, heaven, the directions, the very top (mūrdhā), and ends with Prajāpati, the Lord of creatures. From the fire on the altar to the Creator himself.

💡 Viṣṇu with Indra

Viṣṇu appears here as Indra's companion, as in the Ṛg Veda, where the two are close allies.

💡 Aśvinau

The twin horsemen, the doctors of the gods: health appears even in the list of gods.

ANUVĀKA 7 · GOING DEEPER

THE SOMA CUPS

A technical list. This anuvāka names the grahas, the draughts or cups of soma poured during the great soma sacrifice: upāṃśu, antaryāma, aindravāyava, maitrāvaruṇa, āśvina, śukra, manthin, āgrayaṇa and many more. Each is offered to particular gods at a particular moment.

Why ask for cups? To ask for every cup is to ask that the whole ritual be complete, with nothing missed and nothing wrong. The traditional sense: may I have the merit of every part of the sacrifice.

An inner reading. Teachers also read the soma as the joy within, and the cups as the senses and faculties that receive it. Each "cup" of life (seeing, hearing, thinking) is offered and filled.

💡 Soma

A sacred plant whose pressed juice was the central offering of Vedic ritual; also the moon, and later a symbol of inner bliss.

💡 Hāriyojana

The last cup, for Indra's bay horses, offered as the gods depart: the ritual ends with a send-off.

Whatever you do, do all of it properly, every part, every step. Completeness is a form of devotion.

ZAPPY: These are just names of cups?

PROF. BOLT: Names of the soma draughts, each poured for particular gods. Asking for each one means: may every part of the work be done, and done right.

Even the ritual cups are asked for: may every part of the work be complete.

ANUVĀKA 8 · GOING DEEPER

THE TOOLS OF THE FIRE RITUAL

The tool-kit. After the soma cups come the things a Vedic ritual uses: idhma (firewood), barhiḥ (sacred grass to sit the gods on), vedi (the altar), sruc (ladles), camasa (cups), grāvan (pressing stones), droṇakalaśa (the big wooden vessel), sheds and halls.

Start to finish. The list ends with avabhṛtha, the purifying bath taken at the very end of a soma sacrifice, and svagākāra, the final call. It asks for the work all the way through to its proper close.

The humble things matter. A great ritual cannot happen without firewood and grass. In life, too, big goals rest on small, ordinary tools: asking for them is a reminder not to look down on the basics.

💡 Barhiḥ

Kuśa or darbha grass, spread for the gods to sit on. Still used in rituals today.

💡 Avabhṛtha

The closing bath of a sacrifice. "Avabhṛtha snāna" is still taken at the end of great temple festivals.

Respect the tools and finish the job. The final bath is part of the ritual too.

ZAPPY: Firewood and ladles? What's the inner meaning?

ANUVĀKA 9 · GOING DEEPER

FIRE, SUN, VEDAS AND SEASONS

Cosmic and personal. Fire and the sun, breath and the horse sacrifice, earth and heaven: the list puts the cosmic (sun, sky) beside the personal (breath, fingers). The same forces are at work in the universe and in us.

The Vedas and discipline. ṛk ca me sāma ca me stomaś ca me yajuś ca me: the three Vedas and the hymns of praise. Then dīkṣā and tapas: initiation and austerity. Learning and self-discipline are asked for together.

Made whole by offering. Twice the anuvāka ends yajñena kalpantām: "may these be made perfect by the offering". This is the key idea of the last anuvākas. It's not "give me these", but "may these become whole through a life lived as an offering".

💡 Aditi · Diti

Aditi, the boundless mother of the gods; Diti, the mother of the demons. Both are asked for: even the dark side, rightly ordered.

💡 Bṛhat · Rathantara

Two of the most important Sāma Veda chants, sung at the great soma sacrifices.

Things come right when life is lived as an offering: learning, discipline and seasons all serve something larger than ourselves.

ZAPPY: What does 'perfected by the offering' mean?

PROF. BOLT: Things work out when life is lived as an offering, not a grab. That phrase is where the Camakam is heading.

Rites and Vedas, discipline and seasons: may it all be made whole by the offering.

ANUVĀKA 10 · GOING DEEPER

CATTLE, AND MY WHOLE LIFE AS OFFERING

The herd. The anuvāka first lists cattle by age: calves in the womb, young calves, animals of one and a half, two, two and a half, three, three and a half and four years, bulls, oxen and milch cows. For a Vedic household, cattle were wealth, food and the means of farming.

The turn. Then: āyur yajñena kalpatām, may my life be perfected by the offering; my breath, eye, ear, mind, speech, my very self. After asking for everything, the chanter offers everything back, starting with life itself.

The offering offers itself. The last line is the deepest in the Camakam: yajño yajñena kalpatām, "may the offering be perfected by offering". There's nothing left to give but the act of giving. All the asking was leading here: life itself becomes worship.

💡 Ages of cattle

Names like tryavi (eighteen months) and turyavāṭ (three years) show how closely the Vedic people knew their herds.

💡 Gītā echo

The Bhagavad Gītā (4.24) says the offering, the offerer and the fire are all Brahman. The Camakam reaches the same idea.

Take what life gives, then give it back. A life offered, not hoarded, is the one that is perfected.

ZAPPY: It goes from cattle to 'my whole self'?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. First the farm, then the body, then the self, and finally the offering itself. Everything becomes worship.

yajño yajñena kalpatām: may even the offering be perfected by offering. Life itself becomes worship.

ANUVĀKA 11 · THE CLOSING · GOING DEEPER

NUMBERS, AND A VOW OF SWEET SPEECH

The numbers. ekā ca me tisraś ca me…: one, three, five… up to thirty-three; then four, eight, twelve… up to forty-eight. The commentators give different readings: the 33 gods, the syllables of the Vedic metres, the stages of the altar's bricks, or the growth of the universe in steps. Whatever the reading, it asks for completeness, every number in its place.

The vow of sweetness. The closing goes beyond asking: madhu maniṣye madhu janiṣye madhu vakṣyāmi madhu vadiṣyāmi: sweetly will I think, sweetly will I act, sweetly will I bring, sweetly will I speak. The chanter ends the whole Rudram not with a request but a promise.

Mother Earth. pṛthivīmātar mā mā hiṃsīḥ: Mother Earth, do not harm me. After asking for stones, crops, metals and herds from the earth, the chanter turns to her directly, as a child to a mother. Then: may the gods protect me, and may the ancestors be glad.

💡 Thirty-three

The Veda counts thirty-three gods: 8 Vasus, 11 Rudras, 12 Ādityas, Indra and Prajāpati.

💡 Śāntiḥ ×3

Peace three times: for troubles from within, from the world around, and from forces beyond our control.

After receiving everything, give back sweetness: in thought, in action, and above all in speech.

ZAPPY: After all that asking, it ends with a promise?

PROF. BOLT: A promise to speak sweetly. After receiving everything, the least we can do is give back kind words.

The Rudram ends with a vow: madhu vadiṣyāmi, I will speak sweetly.
