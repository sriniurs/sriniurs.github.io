FOREWORD

Early in the morning, in Śiva temples across India, priests pour water, milk and honey over the liṅga while a deep, rolling chant fills the shrine: namaste rudra manyava… This is the Śrī Rudram, the great hymn of the Yajur Veda, and one of the oldest prayers still chanted every day anywhere in the world.

Many of us have heard it all our lives without knowing what it says. It is full of surprises. It begins by bowing to God's anger. It salutes thieves and potters, dogs and rivers, the sleeping and the running, and finds Śiva in every one of them.

This is the first of three parts. Here we open the first five anuvākas of the Namakam, line by line, in simple English. May the One who is in everything make us gentle, and our whole world free of fear.

THE STORY · HOW WRATH BECAME ŚIVA

As the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa tells it
The Vedic account in 5 frames
Creation tires out. After pouring out all of creation, Prajāpati, the Creator, falls apart, and the gods leave him.
Only Wrath stays. One power remains in him: Manyu, wrath. It rises up as Rudra, with a hundred heads, a thousand eyes and a hundred quivers, bow strung, fierce and hungry.
The gods are afraid. How can they calm such a power? They bring food, and they bow: namaḥ, to every one of his forms.
A hundred offerings. The offering is called the Śatarudrīya, "for the hundred Rudras". Form by form, the anger is soothed.
Fierce becomes gentle. Rudra is pacified and becomes Śiva, the kind one. The Śatarudrīya is still offered when the great Vedic fire-altar is completed.

The Veda

Kṛṣṇa Yajur Veda, Taittirīya Saṃhitā 4.5 (Namakam).

The deity

Rudra, the fierce one, who becomes Śiva.

The pattern

11 namaḥ sections, then 11 ca me sections.

ZAPPY: So the whole Rudram is like the gods calming Rudra down?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, and calming ourselves too. Every 'namaḥ' turns a little more fear into peace.

The Rudram is the gods' own way of turning wrath into grace, one salutation at a time.

ABOUT THE RUDRAM

The heart of the Yajur Veda

📜 Where it's found

Taittirīya Saṃhitā 4.5 (Namakam) and 4.7 (Camakam) of the Kṛṣṇa Yajur Veda. The Śukla Yajur Veda has a parallel version, the Rudrādhyāya (Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā 16).

🏷️ Its names

Śrī Rudram, Rudrapraśna, Rudrādhyāya and the oldest, Śatarudrīya, "of the hundred Rudras".

Two halves. The Namakam has 11 anuvākas (sections) built on namaḥ, "salutation": we bow to the divine in everything. The Camakam has 11 built on ca me, "and to me": we ask for everything we need. First we see the One everywhere; then we ask.

What the Upaniṣads say. In the Jābāla Upaniṣad, Yājñavalkya is asked what to chant to reach immortality. His answer: the Śatarudrīya. The Kaivalya Upaniṣad says one who studies it is purified as if by fire.

The heart of the heart. Tradition often says the Rudram is the heart of the Yajur Veda, and namaḥ śivāya, the Pañcākṣarī, is the heart of the Rudram. It first appears in anuvāka 8 (Part 2).

ANUVĀKA 1 · VERSES 1–5 · GOING DEEPER

SALUTATIONS TO YOUR ANGER

Starting with the anger. Most prayers begin by praising beauty or kindness. The Rudram begins by bowing to manyu, wrath, and to the weapons. It is honest: the Vedic Rudra is the storm, disease and the wild, the power that can strike. The hymn faces that power directly instead of pretending it isn't there.

From fierce to śiva. Count how often the word śiva appears in these five verses: śivatamā (most gentle), śivaṃ, śivā tanūḥ (gentle form), śivāṃ, śivena vacasā (with gentle words). The first anuvāka is a slow turning, from Rudra the fierce to Śiva the kind. This is one of the oldest places where "śiva" is used again and again for this god.

For the whole world. Verse 5 asks that "our whole world (sarvam jagat) be free of illness (ayakṣmam) and of good mind". The request isn't for one person. It has the same "all of us" spirit as the Gāyatrī.

💡 Same verse, two places

Verse 3, yā te rudra śivā tanūḥ, also appears in the Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad (3.5).

💡 Giriśa

"Dweller on the mountain": one of Śiva's oldest names. In the Veda it simply means the wild hills; Kailāsa comes later.

What we fear in life (illness, loss, sudden change) is also part of the One. Bowing to it, instead of running from it, is the first step to peace.

ZAPPY: Why start a prayer by bowing to God's anger?

PROF. BOLT: Because that's the part we're afraid of. Name it, bow to it, and fear starts turning into trust.

The Rudram starts where we are: afraid. It bows to the anger first, and asks it to become gentle.

ANUVĀKA 1 · VERSES 6–10 · GOING DEEPER

THE FIRST DIVINE HEALER

The healer. Daivyo bhiṣak, "the divine physician", is one of Rudra's oldest roles: the Ṛg Veda praises him as the best of healers, holding medicines in his hand. Here he is also adhivaktā, our advocate, the one who speaks on our behalf. The fierce one is on our side.

Seen by the simplest people. Verse 8 is one of the loveliest in the Veda. The blue-throated, red one moving across the sky (traditionally read as the sun) is seen by the cowherds and the women going to fetch water: ordinary people at their daily work. Not only priests see him, and he blesses all of them.

Lowering the bow. Verse 10 begins a run of requests: untie the bowstring from both ends, throw away the arrows. The commentators Bhaṭṭa Bhāskara and Sāyaṇa read this as asking Rudra to withdraw his power to harm and keep only his power to protect.

💡 Nīlagrīva

The blue throat here is older than the famous story of Śiva drinking the poison at the churning of the ocean, which is told in the epics and Purāṇas. Later tradition linked the two.

💡 Thousands of Rudras

Verse 7 speaks of Rudras in their thousands in every direction: the seed of the idea, filled out in the rest of the Namakam, that Rudra has countless forms.

The fierce and the healing come from the same source. Pain is often a teacher, and the healer is the one who understands its cause.

ZAPPY: Rudra the fierce is also the doctor?

PROF. BOLT: The same power that brings sickness knows the cure. The Veda calls him the first divine healer.

Rudra is seen by everyone: cowherds, water-carriers, all beings. Not only by priests.

ANUVĀKA 1 · VERSES 11–15 · GOING DEEPER

PUT DOWN YOUR BOW

Power turned into protection. Verse 13 is subtle. It doesn't ask Rudra to throw his weapon away; it asks him to use it "to protect us on every side". The power isn't removed, it's pointed the other way.

Kapardin. "The one with braided, matted hair": the ascetic look that becomes Śiva's signature. Later this jaṭā becomes the home of the Gaṅgā and the crescent moon.

The closing names. After verse 15 comes a prose litany: Viśveśvara (Lord of the universe), Mahādeva, Tryambaka, Tripurāntaka, Trikāgnikāla, Kālāgnirudra, Nīlakaṇṭha, Mṛtyuñjaya, Sarveśvara, Sadāśiva. The fierce archer of verse 1 has become, by the end of the first anuvāka, Śiva himself.

💡 Tryambaka

"Three-eyed". The same name opens the Mahāmṛtyuñjaya mantra: tryambakaṃ yajāmahe. It closes Part 2 of this series.

💡 Tripurāntaka

Destroyer of the three flying cities of the demons, of gold, silver and iron, with a single arrow: one of Śiva's great deeds in the Purāṇas.

Strength is not the problem; where it points is. The prayer asks that every power, our own included, be turned to protecting rather than harming.

ZAPPY: So the weapons aren't thrown away, just turned away from us?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. Keep your power, Lord, but use it to guard us, not to strike us.

The anger bowed to in verse 1 is now disarmed: bow unstrung, quiver set aside, and the fierce archer called Sadāśiva.

ANUVĀKA 2 · GOING DEEPER

LORD OF TREES, ANIMALS AND PATHS

The pattern. Almost every line ends "…ānāṃ pataye namaḥ": salutations to the lord of X. Directions, animals, paths, food, the well-fed, the world, fields, forests, trees, thickets, herbs, soldiers, the brave. Rudra is not a god of one corner of the world; he is the pati, the protector, of every corner.

Paśupati. "Lord of animals" (paśūnāṃ pati) is among Śiva's most loved names. In later Śaiva philosophy paśu means every soul bound by its limits, and Paśupati the Lord who frees it. Here it starts simply as the protector of cattle and wild creatures.

The wild and the settled. The list moves between the untamed (forests, thickets, herbs) and the human (paths, fields, counsellors, merchants). Rudra was the god of the wild who guarded those who had to cross it; this anuvāka puts both worlds in his care.

💡 Harikeśa

"Green-haired": the trees with their leaves are imagined as Rudra's own hair.

💡 Paśupatinātha

Nepal's great Śiva temple in Kathmandu takes its name from this title.

See a tree, a road, a field or a market as places where the divine is present and in charge. Nothing falls outside its care.

ZAPPY: Trees, fields, merchants, foot-soldiers... why such a mixed list?

PROF. BOLT: Because there's nowhere Rudra isn't. The list is long precisely to make that point.

Every tree, path, field and forest has a lord, and it's the same One.

ANUVĀKA 3 · GOING DEEPER

LORD OF THIEVES, AND OF EVERYONE

Lord of thieves. Stenānāṃ pati, taskarāṇāṃ pati: lord of thieves and robbers. The commentators give several readings. Rudra is in everything, so even crime isn't outside him. He is the master-thief who "steals" our ego. And practically: a traveller crossing a robbers' forest prays to their lord for safe passage.

From "lord of" to "you". Halfway through, the grammar changes. Instead of "to the lord of X", it says "to you who…, salutations": ca vo namaḥ. Rudra is no longer above these beings; he is the beings. The archers, the sitters and the sleepers are themselves his forms.

Every state. Sitting and lying, sleeping and waking, standing and running: these pairs cover every state a person can be in. Whatever you are doing as you hear this line, Rudra is in that too.

💡 The divine thief

A much-loved theme in later devotion: Kṛṣṇa steals butter, and poets say Śiva steals hearts.

💡 Śatarudrīya

"Of the hundred Rudras": the Namakam names well over a hundred forms. It's one of the hymn's oldest names.

No one is outside the divine, not even those we judge. Seeing the One in everyone softens condemnation without excusing wrong.

ZAPPY: Bowing to thieves? Isn't that crazy?

PROF. BOLT: Not to the stealing. To the One inside them as well. And even a thief needs grace to change.

Sitting, lying, sleeping, waking, standing, running: in every state, Rudra is there.

ANUVĀKA 4 · GOING DEEPER

THE CRAFTSMEN'S GOD

Work as worship. This anuvāka is a roll-call of working people: carpenters, chariot-builders, potters, blacksmiths, bow-makers, hunters, fishermen. In a hymn chanted by priests, ordinary trades are named one by one and saluted as divine. Work done well is worship.

Gaṇapati. gaṇebhyo gaṇapatibhyaś ca vo namaḥ: to groups and to the leaders of groups. Here gaṇapati means any leader of a band. Later, Gaṇeśa becomes Śiva's son and the leader of his gaṇas, his attendants, and this is the title he carries.

Every shape, every size. "To the misshapen and to those of every shape; to the great and to the small." Beauty and ugliness, big and little: nothing falls outside the One. The pairs quietly teach us to stop ranking.

💡 Dogs

śvabhyaḥ śvapatibhyaś ca: to dogs and their masters. Śiva's fierce form Bhairava is shown with a dog as his companion.

💡 Āyudha Pūjā

On the ninth day of Navarātri, craftsmen, farmers and workers across South India worship their tools: the same spirit.

Whatever your work (scalpel, hammer or keyboard), it is a place where the divine works through your hands.

ZAPPY: Potters, blacksmiths, even dogs?

PROF. BOLT: Every skilled hand and every living creature. The Rudram skips no one.

The potter at the wheel and the smith at the forge work as Rudra's own hands.

ANUVĀKA 5 · GOING DEEPER

BHAVA AND RUDRA: THE GOD OF PAIRS

The "ca" anuvāka. The style changes again. Now every line is "namo X-āya ca Y-āya ca", to X and to Y. Pairs of names, often opposites, joined by ca, "and". This is where the Rudram says most clearly that the divine holds both ends of every contradiction.

Bhava and Śarva. Bhava, "existence", the one from whom everything comes; Śarva, the archer who ends things. Creation and dissolution are saluted in one breath. Both are among Śiva's traditional eight names, with Rudra, Paśupati, Ugra, Bhīma, Īśāna and Mahādeva.

Rivers and islands. The anuvāka ends among waters: the rushing current and the calm pool, the waves and the still water, the river and the island in it. Rudra is both the flow and the resting place in the flow.

💡 Kapardin and Vyuptakeśa

Long matted hair and a shaven head: the two looks of Indian ascetics, then and now. Both are Rudra.

💡 Śipiviṣṭa

"Present in all beings", or "clothed in rays": a name the Ṛg Veda also gives to Viṣṇu.

Life comes in pairs: gain and loss, praise and blame, rush and rest. Seeing the One on both sides is what keeps us steady.

ZAPPY: Blue-throated and white-throated? Long-haired and shaven? Which is he?

PROF. BOLT: Both. For every opposite, the Rudram says 'ca … ca', 'and … and', and bows to both.

ca … ca: 'and … and'. Rudra is both sides of every pair.
