FOREWORD

In Vārāṇasī, before pilgrims go anywhere else, many go first to a small, crowded temple to pay their respects to the city's guardian: Kāla Bhairava, Śiva in his fiercest form, dark, sky-clad, garlanded with skulls, with a black dog at his side.

He looks terrifying. But Ādi Śaṅkara's Kālabhairavāṣṭakam shows who he really is: a mine of compassion, the giver of both a good life and liberation, the guardian of dharma, the one who cuts the noose of karma and breaks the pride of Death. Each verse ends with the same quiet promise: kāśikāpurādhinātha kālabhairavaṃ bhaje, "I worship Kāla Bhairava, Lord of Kāśī".

May every reader find in Him what devotees have always found: the fierce protector who is kindest of all.

THE STORY · BRAHMĀ'S FIFTH HEAD

How Bhairava became the guardian of Kāśī
The Purāṇic story in 5 frames
Brahmā's pride. Brahmā, the Creator, once had five heads. Growing proud, he declared himself the supreme god and spoke insultingly of Śiva.
Bhairava appears. From Śiva's anger arose a terrible form: Kāla Bhairava, dark, fierce and blazing.
The fifth head. Bhairava plucked off the fifth head, the one that had spoken with pride. Humbled, Brahmā has had four heads ever since.
The skull that would not fall. But cutting off Brahmā's head was a grave sin, and the skull stuck fast to Bhairava's hand. He wandered the worlds as a beggar, the skull his only bowl.
Kāśī. When he entered Kāśī, the skull dropped from his hand at last, at a place called Kapālamocana, "release of the skull". Śiva made Bhairava the guardian of Kāśī forever.

The pride

Brahmā's fifth head.

The penance

Wandering with the skull.

The release

Kapālamocana, Kāśī.

ZAPPY: Even Bhairava had to do penance?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. Even a god's justified act carried a cost. And Kāśī is where even that burden falls away.

Pride loses its head; in Kāśī, every burden falls away.

ABOUT THE KĀLA BHAIRAVĀṢṬAKAM

The guardian's hymn

✍️ The author

Traditionally Ādi Śaṅkara, who taught in Kāśī. Eight verses and a phalaśruti.

🎵 The rhythm

The pañcacāmara metre: short-long, short-long, sixteen syllables a line, the same marching beat as the Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotram.

The refrain. Every verse ends kāśikāpurādhinātha kālabhairavaṃ bhaje, "I worship Kāla Bhairava, supreme Lord of Kāśī".

The picture. Dark-bodied, sky-clad, three-eyed, blue-throated; serpents for a sacred thread and ornaments; trident, axe, noose and staff; a skull garland; golden bells at the waist; a black dog at his side.

Where he's worshipped. His temples in Vārāṇasī and Ujjain are famous; many Śiva temples have a Bhairava shrine guarding them. Devotees pray to him for protection, especially before journeys and in times of fear.

VERSE 1 · GOING DEEPER

THE LORD WHOSE FEET INDRA SERVES

Who is Kāla Bhairava? The fiercest form of Śiva, born from his wrath. Kāla means time and death; Bhairava is "the terrible one", and is also explained as the one who removes fear (bhaya) from devotees. Dark, sky-clad, garlanded with skulls, followed by a dog: frightening to evil, protective of the good.

The refrain. Every verse ends kāśikāpurādhinātha kālabhairavaṃ bhaje: "I worship Kāla Bhairava, supreme Lord of Kāśī". In Vārāṇasī he is the Kotwal, the city's guardian; pilgrims visit him first, and it's said no one stays in Kāśī without his leave.

Fierce, yet kind. Serpent for a sacred thread, nothing but the sky for clothing, yet kṛpākara, a mine of compassion. The first verse sets the tone: the terrible form exists to protect.

💡 Ādi Śaṅkara

Tradition attributes this aṣṭakam to Śaṅkara, who lived and taught in Kāśī.

💡 Digambara

"Sky-clad": owning nothing, not even clothes; total renunciation.

The fierce and the kind aren't opposites. A protector is fierce toward danger and gentle toward those he protects.

ZAPPY: Indra serves His feet?

PROF. BOLT: Bhairava is Śiva in his fiercest form. Even the king of the gods bows to Him.

Fierce and sky-clad, yet a mine of compassion: the Lord of Kāśī.

VERSE 2 · GOING DEEPER

BRIGHTER THAN A MILLION SUNS

Kāla. Time devours everything: people, kingdoms, mountains. Kāla Bhairava is kālakāla, "the Death of death", Time's own master. To worship Him is to step outside Time's fear.

The ferryman. Bhavābdhi-tāraka: the one who carries us across the ocean of worldly existence. It's the same image as the Śrī Rudram's "tāra" and "pāra", the boat and the far shore.

Akṣa and akṣara. Śaṅkara plays with sounds: akṣa (rudrākṣa beads), akṣaśūla (rosary and trident), akṣara (imperishable, also "syllable"). The fierce god counts beads and is the eternal syllable itself.

💡 Kālāṣṭamī

The eighth day of every waning moon is Bhairava's day; Kālabhairava Jayantī, his birthday, falls in Kārtika or Mārgaśīrṣa.

💡 Ambujākṣa

"Lotus-eyed", a name usually given to Viṣṇu: the fierce god has gentle eyes.

Time takes everything we hold. Holding on to what is beyond time is the only lasting safety.

ZAPPY: 'Death of death' again?

PROF. BOLT: Kāla means both time and death. Kāla Bhairava is the one Time itself fears.

Kāla is Time; Kāla Bhairava is the one Time fears.

VERSE 3 · GOING DEEPER

FOUR WEAPONS AND A WILD DANCE

The four weapons. The trident (śūla) pierces evil; the axe (ṭaṅka) cuts attachment; the noose (pāśa) binds wrongdoers; the staff (daṇḍa) corrects. Bhairava is the cosmic police: law and order, enforced.

Dark and healthy. Śyāmakāya, dark-bodied, the colour of night and of the deep. Nirāmaya, free of all disease, and so the giver of health to those who worship him.

The tāṇḍava. Vicitra-tāṇḍava-priya: the lover of the wondrous wild dance. Śiva's tāṇḍava destroys and renews the universe; Bhairava dances it in the cremation ground, where endings and beginnings meet.

💡 Daṇḍapāṇi

"Staff in hand": Bhairava's role as punisher of wrongdoers in Kāśī; Daṇḍapāṇi is also an attendant guarding the city.

💡 Ādideva

"The first god": Bhairava is here not a minor guardian but the original Lord.

Discipline is a form of care. Rules that bind and correct are there to protect.

ZAPPY: Why a noose and a staff?

PROF. BOLT: The noose binds what's wrong, the staff corrects it. Bhairava is the divine keeper of law and order.

Trident, axe, noose and staff: the tools of a cosmic guardian.

VERSE 4 · GOING DEEPER

GIVER OF JOY AND FREEDOM

Bhukti and mukti. Many paths say you must give up the world to be free. Bhairava gives both: bhukti, a good life in the world, and mukti, liberation. Tantric traditions especially honour this double gift.

Bhaktavatsala. Vatsala comes from vatsa, calf: the love of a cow for her calf. The fearsome Bhairava is as tender to his devotees as a mother cow.

The bells. A belt of golden bells (kiṅkiṇī) jingles at his waist. Like the bells on a temple door, the sound announces that the guardian is near: for a devotee, a comforting sound.

💡 Vatsala

From vatsa, calf: the classic image of divine tenderness.

💡 Viśvarūpa

"Whose body is all the worlds": like Kṛṣṇa's cosmic form in Gītā chapter 11.

Spiritual life doesn't mean rejecting the world. Live well, love well, and keep freedom as the goal.

ZAPPY: Enjoyment AND freedom? Don't you have to choose?

PROF. BOLT: Bhairava gives both: a good life here and freedom beyond. You don't have to give up one to have the other.

Bhukti and mukti: a good life here, and freedom beyond.

VERSE 5 · GOING DEEPER

GUARDIAN OF THE BRIDGE OF DHARMA

Setu. A setu is a bridge or embankment: the bund that keeps a river in its course. Dharma is that embankment for life; without it, everything floods into chaos. Bhairava is its guard.

The noose of karma. Karmapāśa-mocaka: the one who frees us from karma's noose. In Śaiva thought, the soul (paśu) is bound by the noose (pāśa) and freed by the Lord (pati). Bhairava cuts the rope.

Suśarma. "Good shelter, true happiness". Not passing pleasure, but the deep well-being that comes from living rightly and being free.

💡 Setu

Also the word for Rāma's bridge to Laṅkā, Rāma Setu.

💡 Paśu, pāśa, pati

Soul, bond and Lord: the three basic ideas of Śaiva Siddhānta.

Doing right keeps the river of life on course. And when past mistakes bind you, grace can still set you free.

ZAPPY: A bridge of dharma?

PROF. BOLT: Like an embankment that keeps a river on its course. Dharma keeps life from flooding into chaos, and Bhairava guards it.

He guards the bridge of dharma and cuts the noose of karma.

VERSE 6 · GOING DEEPER

THE SANDALS, AND DEATH'S BROKEN PRIDE

The sandals. In Indian devotion, the Lord's or the guru's sandals (pādukā) are worshipped as his presence. Bharata ruled Ayodhyā for fourteen years with Rāma's sandals on the throne.

Without a second. Advitīya, "one without a second", is a key Upaniṣadic phrase (ekam evādvitīyam). The fierce guardian of Kāśī is called the one Reality itself.

Death's fangs. Mṛtyudarpa-nāśana: destroyer of Death's pride. Like Śiva saving Mārkaṇḍeya, Bhairava pulls his devotees from Death's jaws.

💡 Pādukā

Sandals are worshipped as the presence of the Lord or guru: "guru-pādukā" worship is common in many traditions.

💡 Iṣṭa-devatā

The personal, chosen form of God that a devotee loves most.

The fear of death loosens when we know something deathless to hold on to.

ZAPPY: Death has pride?

PROF. BOLT: Death thinks it always wins. Bhairava is the one it can't beat.

Death's fangs can't hold those who hold Bhairava's feet.

VERSE 7 · GOING DEEPER

THE LAUGH THAT CRACKS WORLDS

The laugh. Aṭṭahāsa, the booming laugh of dissolution, cracks the shells of the cosmic eggs (brahmāṇḍa) that lotus-born Brahmā creates. Universes come and go; Bhairava laughs.

The glance. Dṛṣṭipāta-naṣṭa-pāpajāla: a single glance destroys whole nets of sin. Darśan, being seen by the divine, is itself purifying.

The skulls. Bhairava's skull garland recalls our story: he carried Brahmā's skull until Kāśī. The skulls also say: every ego, every lifetime, ends; only the Lord remains.

💡 Aṣṭa-siddhi

The eight yogic powers: becoming tiny, huge, light or heavy; reaching anything; having any wish; mastery; control.

💡 Brahmāṇḍa

"Brahmā's egg": the old Indian word for a universe.

False things can't survive a clear look. Let the truth see you, and the net of wrongs loosens.

ZAPPY: His laugh breaks universes?

PROF. BOLT: And His glance breaks sins. Whatever is false cracks in front of Him.

One laugh cracks worlds; one glance breaks the net of sin.

VERSE 8 · GOING DEEPER

PURIFIER OF ALL WHO LIVE IN KĀŚĪ

Lord of the spirits. Bhūtasaṅgha-nāyaka: leader of the hosts of bhūtas, the ghosts and wild beings of Śiva's retinue. Whatever is frightening in the unseen world answers to him, so his devotees need not fear it.

Merit and sin. Puṇya-pāpa-śodhaka: purifier of both. Vedānta says good deeds done with ego bind us as surely as bad ones, golden chains instead of iron. In Kāśī, tradition holds, Bhairava clears both, so that the soul can go free.

Kāśī. Tradition says that anyone who dies in Kāśī is liberated, because Śiva whispers the saving mantra into their ear. Bhairava, the guardian, first settles their karmic account.

💡 Kāla Bhairava Mandir

His temple in Vārāṇasī: pilgrims tie a black thread from there for protection.

💡 Kāśī

"The city of light", Vārāṇasī: one of the oldest living cities in the world.

Good deeds done for pride still bind. Do good, then let go of the pride in it too.

ZAPPY: He purifies merits too, not just sins?

PROF. BOLT: Even good deeds bind if they make us proud. In Kāśī, Bhairava clears both, so only freedom remains.

In Kāśī, Bhairava clears both merit and sin, so only freedom remains.

PHALAŚRUTI · GOING DEEPER

THE FRUIT OF CHANTING

Knowledge and freedom. Jñāna-mukti-sādhana: a means to knowledge and liberation. Śaṅkara's phalaśruti puts wisdom first.

Six troubles. Śoka (sorrow), moha (delusion), dainya (misery, helplessness), lobha (greed), kopa (anger), tāpa (burning pain). A precise list of what makes human life hard.

Surely. Dhruvam: certainly, without doubt. They reach the sannidhi, the nearness, of Bhairava's feet: the protector's own shelter.

💡 Bhairava's dog

The black dog is Bhairava's companion; feeding dogs is a traditional offering to him.

💡 When it's chanted

Especially on Kālāṣṭamī and on Kālabhairava Jayantī, and by many before travel or in times of fear.

Name your troubles honestly: sorrow, confusion, greed, anger. Naming them is the first step to letting them go.

ZAPPY: Six troubles destroyed?

PROF. BOLT: Sorrow, delusion, misery, greed, anger and burning pain. The usual suspects in any human life.

Sorrow, delusion, misery, greed, anger, pain: six troubles, one remedy.

THE WHOLE HYMN ON ONE PAGE

The fierce guardian who is kindest of all
Verses 1–2: who He is. Indra serves His feet; sky-clad, compassionate; bright as millions of suns; the ferryman across life's ocean; the Death of death.
Verses 3–5: what He does. Trident, axe, noose and staff; the wild dance; gives worldly joy and liberation; guards the bridge of dharma and cuts karma's noose.
Verses 6–8: His power. Breaks Death's pride; His laugh cracks worlds, His glance burns sin; leader of the spirits, purifier of Kāśī.
Phalaśruti. Knowledge and freedom; sorrow, delusion, misery, greed, anger and pain destroyed; refuge at His feet.

ZAPPY: Should I be scared of Bhairava?

PROF. BOLT: Only if you're on the wrong side. To His devotees, the fiercest guardian is the safest shelter.

Kāla Bhairavāṣṭakam: the terrible one who removes all terror. 🙏
