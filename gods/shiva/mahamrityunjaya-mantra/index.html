MṚTYUÑJAYA

The great death-conquering mantra · word by word, explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: The mantra first, as it is chanted. Then the story and background, and two pages for each of its four parts: the words, a picture, what they say and the key words; then a deeper look, background facts, the inner teaching and the one thing to remember.

FOREWORD

When someone falls ill, when a family waits outside an operation theatre, when an elder is nearing the end, one mantra is chanted more than any other: oṃ tryambakaṃ yajāmahe… The Mahāmṛtyuñjaya, "the great conqueror of death", has been a prayer for healing and courage for three thousand years.

It is only thirty-two syllables long. It asks Śiva, the Three-eyed, the Fragrant, the giver of health, for one thing, using one homely picture: a ripe cucumber slipping from its vine. Free us from death, it says, as gently as that, but never from the deathless.

May every reader, and everyone they pray for, find in it health, peace and freedom from fear.

Oṃ Namaḥ Śivāya,

THE STORY · THE SECRET OF LIFE AFTER DEATH

Kaca and the Sañjīvanī, from the Mahābhārata
The story in 5 frames
An unfair war. The gods and the asuras are at war, but the asuras have a secret: their guru Śukrācārya knows the Sañjīvanī, the knowledge that brings the dead back to life, gained, tradition says, by worshipping Śiva. Every fallen asura rises again.
A student. The gods send young Kaca, son of their own guru Bṛhaspati, to serve Śukra as a student. Śukra's daughter Devayānī grows fond of him.
Killed, again and again. The asuras kill Kaca again and again; each time Devayānī begs, and Śukra revives him. At last they burn him, mix his ashes into wine, and give it to Śukra to drink.
Taught from within. To save Kaca without dying himself, Śukra teaches him the Sañjīvanī while Kaca is inside his body. Kaca bursts out, Śukra falls, and the grateful student uses the knowledge to bring his teacher back to life.
Knowledge beats death. Śukra forbids wine to Brahmins from that day. Tradition calls the Mahāmṛtyuñjaya the Mṛtasañjīvanī mantra, the mantra of the life-restoring knowledge.

The teacher

Śukrācārya, guru of the asuras.

The student

Kaca, son of Bṛhaspati.

The knowledge

Sañjīvanī, which restores life.

ZAPPY: The student brought his own teacher back to life?

PROF. BOLT: With the very knowledge he'd just been taught. Knowledge passed on with love defeats even death.

The Mahāmṛtyuñjaya is remembered as the mantra that restores life.

ABOUT THE MANTRA

Thirty-two syllables against fear

📜 Where it's found

Ṛg Veda 7.59.12, seer Vasiṣṭha: the last verse of a hymn to the Maruts, addressed to Rudra. Also in the Yajur Veda, and at the close of the Śrī Rudram's Namakam.

🏷️ Its names

Mahāmṛtyuñjaya (great conqueror of death), Tryambaka mantra, and Mṛtasañjīvanī (restorer of life).

The metre. Anuṣṭubh: four lines of eight syllables, thirty-two in all, the same metre as most of the epics' verses.

Mārkaṇḍeya. The mantra is tied to the boy Mārkaṇḍeya, who clung to the liṅga when death came for him and was saved by Śiva as Kālāntaka. (We tell that story in our Śrī Rudram, Part 2.)

How it's used. Chanted for the sick and at times of danger; 108 times on a rudrākṣa mālā; in Mṛtyuñjaya homa for health and long life; and quietly, by many, every morning.

TRYAMBAKAṂ YAJĀMAHE · GOING DEEPER

WE WORSHIP THE THREE-EYED ONE

Tryambaka. Tri is three; ambaka is read as "eye", so Tryambaka is the Three-eyed. Other readings make it "father of the three worlds" or "of the three mothers" (earth, sky and heaven), from ambā, mother. Both readings are old, and both are honoured.

The third eye. Śiva's two eyes are the sun and the moon; the third, on his forehead, is fire. It is the eye that burnt Kāma, the god of desire, and it stands for the inner eye of wisdom (jñāna-cakṣus), which sees past appearances.

Yajāmahe. "We worship", "we offer", from the root yaj, the same root as yajña, sacrifice. And it's plural, like the Gāyatrī's dhīmahi: the great Vedic prayers are said as "we".

💡 Tryambakeśvara

The temple at Tryambakeshwar near Nashik, one of the twelve jyotirliṅgas, takes its name from this word.

💡 Om

The Ṛg Veda verse begins at tryambakam; Om is added at the start when it's chanted as a mantra.

Begin by giving, not asking: attention, gratitude, worship. The asking can wait.

ZAPPY: Three eyes, for what?

PROF. BOLT: Two to see the world, and a third to see through it. Sun, moon and fire, or past, present and future.

We worship, together: the mantra starts with an offering, not a request.

SUGANDHIṂ PUṢṬIVARDHANAM · GOING DEEPER

THE FRAGRANT ONE WHO MAKES ALL THINGS GROW

Sugandhi. A fragrance fills a room without being seen and without noise. That is how the mantra pictures the divine: everywhere, gentle, felt rather than shown. In the tradition, a good life is also said to have a fragrance: a good name that spreads by itself.

Puṣṭivardhana. "Who makes nourishment grow": health, strength, growth, prosperity. Śiva, called the Destroyer, is named here as the great Nourisher. Destruction and nourishment are two hands of the same power, as the Rudram showed.

Why it's chanted for health. This line is a big reason the Mahāmṛtyuñjaya is chanted for the sick. The Rudram calls Rudra the first divine healer; here he is the one who makes the body and life flourish.

💡 Gandha

Sandal paste, gandha, is applied to the liṅga in pūjā: fragrance offered to the Fragrant One.

💡 Bhiṣak

"Healer": the Śrī Rudram (anuvāka 1) calls Rudra prathamo daivyo bhiṣak, the first divine physician.

Be like a fragrance: present without noise, and leave everything you touch a little better nourished.

ZAPPY: God has a fragrance?

PROF. BOLT: Like a flower you can't see but can smell: present everywhere, felt gently, never forced on you.

Fragrant and nourishing: Śiva makes life grow.

URVĀRUKAM IVA BANDHANĀN · GOING DEEPER

LIKE A RIPE CUCUMBER FROM ITS VINE

The picture. Cucumber and melon vines trail along the ground. When the fruit is ripe, it comes away from its stalk with the lightest touch, or drops by itself. An unripe one clings, and has to be torn off, bruising both fruit and vine.

Not forced. The mantra doesn't ask to be torn away from life. It asks for ripening: for the soul to mature until letting go is natural. The prayer is as much about how we live as about how we die.

Many bonds. Bandhana is any bond: the stalk, but also illness, fear, attachment, habit. Whatever holds us too tightly, the mantra asks that we outgrow it, the way ripe fruit outgrows its stalk.

💡 Urvāruka

A kind of cucumber or melon (Cucumis). It's a humble image: a vegetable from the kitchen garden, not a jewel or a lotus.

💡 Ripe fruit

Many teachers use the same image for spiritual maturity: the fruit falls when it is ready.

Don't tear yourself away from life in a hurry. Ripen, and let go when the time is right, without pain.

ZAPPY: Why compare us to a cucumber?

PROF. BOLT: When it's ripe, it drops off the vine by itself. No tearing, no pain. That's the release we're asking for.

Ripen, and the bond lets go on its own.

MṚTYOR MUKṢĪYA MĀMṚTĀT · GOING DEEPER

FREE FROM DEATH, NOT FROM THE DEATHLESS

Two releases. Mukṣīya, "may I be released", comes from the root muc, the same root as mokṣa, liberation. First: mṛtyoḥ, from death, and from the fear of it.

Mā amṛtāt. Then the twist: mā amṛtāt, "not from the deathless". Commentators explain: may I never be separated from immortality. Let what is mortal fall away; keep me joined to what never dies. The whole spiritual path in four words.

A bride's version. The Yajur Veda also has a variant: sugandhiṃ pativedanam … ito mukṣīya māmutaḥ, "fragrant, who finds a good husband… free me from here, not from there". It was chanted for a bride leaving her parents' home: free from the old bond, never from the new one.

💡 Mokṣa

Mukṣīya and mokṣa share the root muc, "to release".

💡 Amṛta

"Deathless": the nectar from the churning of the ocean, and in the Upaniṣads the Self that never dies.

Fear of death eases when we know what in us does not die. Hold on to that; let everything else ripen and fall.

ZAPPY: Free from death but not from immortality? Isn't that the same thing?

PROF. BOLT: Not quite. Let go of what dies: the body, the fear. Never let go of what doesn't: the Self.

Free me from death, never from the deathless.

THE WHOLE MANTRA ON ONE PAGE

Worship, grow, ripen, let go
Oṃ tryambakaṃ yajāmahe. We worship the Three-eyed: sun, moon and the fire of wisdom.
Sugandhiṃ puṣṭivardhanam. Fragrant and gently present; the one who makes all life thrive.
Urvārukam iva bandhanān. Like a ripe cucumber that slips from its vine, without tearing.
Mṛtyor mukṣīya māmṛtāt. Free me from death and its fear, never from the deathless Self.
