Eight verses on the sign of the formless · explained and illustrated

FOREWORD

"Brahmamurāri surārcita liṅgam…" Few Śiva hymns are as easy to sing or as widely known. Children learn it; temples ring with it at evening ārati; on Mahāśivarātri it is sung through the night.

Each of its eight verses ends the same way, tat praṇamāmi sadāśiva liṅgam, "to that liṅga of Sadāśiva I bow", and each gives a new reason: the night Brahmā and Viṣṇu could not find its ends, Rāvaṇa's pride, Dakṣa's arrogant sacrifice, the kuṅkuma and sandal of daily pūjā, the light of a million suns, and finally the Supreme Self beyond everything.

The liṅga is the simplest of forms, the sign of the One beyond form. May every reader, in bowing to it, glimpse what it points to.

THE STORY · THE PILLAR WITH NO END

The Liṅgodbhava, from the Śiva and Liṅga Purāṇas
The story in 5 frames
A quarrel. Brahmā and Viṣṇu argue: which of them is the greatest?
The pillar. Suddenly a blazing column of fire appears between them, stretching endlessly up and down. A voice says: whoever finds its end is the greater.
The search. Brahmā becomes a swan and flies up; Viṣṇu becomes a boar and digs down. For ages they search. Neither finds an end.
A lie. Viṣṇu returns and admits he failed. Brahmā claims he reached the top, with a ketakī flower falling from above as his "witness". The pillar splits open and Śiva steps out.
The truth. Śiva blesses Viṣṇu for his honesty, and declares that Brahmā will hardly be worshipped, and the ketakī never offered to Śiva. Both bow to the pillar: the first liṅga, without beginning or end.

The pillar

Fire with no end: the first liṅga.

The lie

Brahmā and the ketakī flower.

The night

Remembered on Mahāśivarātri.

ZAPPY: So the liṅga is really a pillar of light?

PROF. BOLT: That's what it stands for: the infinite, which even the gods can't measure. The stone is just its sign.

The liṅga marks the infinite that no one can measure, not even the gods.

ABOUT THE LIṄGĀṢṬAKAM

The best-known song to the liṅga

🕉️ The liṅga

Liṅga means "sign" or "mark": the symbol of the formless Śiva. The base (pīṭha) represents Śakti; together they show Śiva and Śakti as one.

📜 The hymn

A traditional aṣṭakam of eight verses and a phalaśruti. Its author is uncertain; it's often attributed to Ādi Śaṅkara.

The refrain. Every verse ends tat praṇamāmi sadāśiva liṅgam. Each line before it is a compound ending in liṅgam, so the hymn rings with the word: twenty-four times in eight verses.

The shape. Stories (verses 1, 2 and 4: Brahmā and Viṣṇu, Rāvaṇa, Dakṣa); the pūjā itself (3 and 5: fragrance, kuṅkuma, sandal, lotuses); and the meaning (6–8: light, the source, the Supreme Self).

Where it's sung. At evening ārati, on Mondays, during abhiṣeka, and through the night of Mahāśivarātri.

VERSE 1 · GOING DEEPER

WORSHIPPED BY BRAHMĀ AND VIṢṆU

What is a liṅga? The word means "sign" or "mark": the visible sign of the invisible. The Liṅga Purāṇa calls it the symbol of the formless, unmanifest Śiva. Its rounded shape has no face and no features: a form that points beyond form.

Brahmā and Viṣṇu bow. Brahma-murāri-surārcita: worshipped by Brahmā, by Murāri (Viṣṇu, the slayer of the demon Mura) and by the gods. It refers straight to the Liṅgodbhava story: the night the endless column of light appeared.

The refrain. Every verse ends tat praṇamāmi sadāśiva liṅgam: "to that liṅga of Sadāśiva I bow". The verses keep adding reasons; the bow stays the same.

💡 Murāri

"Enemy of Mura": Kṛṣṇa slew the demon Mura, and the name is used for Viṣṇu.

💡 Twelve jyotirliṅgas

Twelve shrines across India honour Śiva as a "liṅga of light", recalling the pillar of fire.

The best symbols point beyond themselves. Bow to the sign, and let it carry you to what it stands for.

ZAPPY: Why do even Brahmā and Viṣṇu worship the liṅga?

PROF. BOLT: Because of the night they tried to find its top and bottom and couldn't. That's our story.

Even the Creator and the Preserver bow to it: the liṅga has no beginning and no end.

VERSE 2 · GOING DEEPER

THE ONE WHO HUMBLED RĀVAṆA

Kāmadaha. When Kāma shot his flower-arrow at the meditating Śiva, the third eye opened and burnt him to ash. Yet in the same line Śiva is karuṇākara, an ocean of compassion: He burns desire, not the one who desires.

Rāvaṇa and Kailāsa. Proud of his strength, Rāvaṇa tried to lift Mount Kailāsa. Śiva simply pressed down with one toe, trapping Rāvaṇa's arms beneath the mountain. Rāvaṇa cried out (his name is often explained as "the one who roared") and sang Śiva's praises, the Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotram, until Śiva freed and blessed him.

Pride, not the person. Darpa vināśana: destroyer of pride. Rāvaṇa remained a great Śiva devotee. Śiva broke his arrogance and kept his love.

💡 Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotram

The thunderous hymn tradition says Rāvaṇa sang under Kailāsa: "jaṭāṭavīgalajjala…".

💡 Candrahāsa

The sword Śiva is said to have given Rāvaṇa after forgiving him.

Pride is the one thing that blocks grace. Let it be broken, and what remains is welcomed.

ZAPPY: Rāvaṇa was a Śiva devotee, wasn't he?

PROF. BOLT: A great one, and a proud one. Śiva crushed the pride and kept the devotee. That's compassion.

Śiva breaks the pride and keeps the devotee.

VERSE 3 · GOING DEEPER

FRAGRANT, AND GIVER OF WISDOM

Fragrance. In pūjā the liṅga is anointed with sandal, perfumed oils and flowers. The verse turns that act into praise: sarva sugandhi sulepita, beautifully anointed with every fragrance.

Wisdom. Buddhi vivardhana kāraṇa: the cause of growing wisdom. Worship isn't only for wealth or protection; it sharpens the mind. Like the Gāyatrī, it asks for a better intelligence.

Both sides. Siddha-surāsura vandita: honoured by perfected beings, by gods and by asuras. Śiva is the one deity both sides worship. Rāvaṇa, Bāṇāsura and many others were his devotees.

💡 Bāṇāsura

A thousand-armed asura king and great Śiva devotee, whose story is told in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa.

💡 Siddhas

"Perfected ones": yogis who have reached spiritual mastery.

The divine doesn't take sides. Anyone who comes sincerely, friend or foe, is welcome.

ZAPPY: Gods AND asuras worship it?

PROF. BOLT: Śiva is the one god both sides pray to. Rāvaṇa, Bāṇa, many asuras were his devotees.

Friend and foe both bow here: the liṅga belongs to everyone.

VERSE 4 · GOING DEEPER

GOLD, SERPENT, AND DAKṢA'S FALL

Gold and serpent. Rich ornaments and a living cobra side by side. Wealth and danger both serve as Śiva's decorations; neither moves Him.

Dakṣa's sacrifice. Dakṣa, father of Satī, held a grand sacrifice and invited everyone except his son-in-law Śiva, then insulted him before all. Satī, unable to bear it, gave up her body in the sacrificial fire. In grief and fury Śiva sent Vīrabhadra, who destroyed the whole sacrifice.

The lesson. A ritual done with arrogance and disrespect has no value, however grand. The story ends with Dakṣa forgiven and restored (with a goat's head), and with Satī reborn as Pārvatī.

💡 Śakti Pīṭhas

Tradition says the places where parts of Satī's body fell became the Śakti Pīṭhas, the Goddess's great shrines.

💡 Vīrabhadra

The fierce form Śiva created from a lock of his hair to punish Dakṣa's arrogance.

Grandeur without respect falls apart. Honour the people around you, or the finest ritual is empty.

ZAPPY: Why destroy a sacrifice?

PROF. BOLT: Dakṣa held it to insult Śiva and humiliated his own daughter Satī. A ritual done with arrogance has no worth.

Gold and serpents both adorn Him, and a ritual without respect falls apart.

VERSE 5 · GOING DEEPER

KUMKUM, SANDAL AND LOTUS

Words that follow the hands. This verse describes the pūjā as it's done: kuṅkuma, sandal paste, a lotus garland. Chanted while decorating the liṅga, the act becomes a prayer and the prayer becomes an act.

Red and white. Red kuṅkuma is linked with Śakti, the Goddess; cool sandal with Śiva's calm. Together on the liṅga, they show Śiva and Śakti as one.

Accumulated sins. Sañcita pāpa: the load piled up over time, even over lifetimes. As layer after layer of paste and flowers goes on the liṅga, layer after layer of that old load comes off the heart.

💡 Sañcita karma

In the karma teaching, sañcita is the stored-up stock of past actions, prārabdha what is unfolding now, āgāmi what we create next.

💡 Paṅkaja

"Mud-born": the lotus grows from mud yet stays clean, a symbol of purity in the world.

Turn ordinary acts into prayer. Done with attention, decorating a shrine or cooking a meal can lighten the heart.

ZAPPY: Is this just describing how the liṅga is decorated?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, and that's the point: as you decorate the liṅga in pūjā, you chant this verse. The words follow the hands.

As you decorate the liṅga, the old load of sin falls away.

VERSE 6 · GOING DEEPER

LIGHT OF A MILLION SUNS

Bhāva and bhakti. Bhāvair bhaktibhir eva ca: "with feelings and with devotion". The word eva means "only" or "truly": real worship is made of feeling, not of materials.

A million suns. Dinakara koṭi prabhākara: shining like crores of suns. The same image appears in the Bhagavad Gītā (11.12), where Arjuna sees the cosmic form: "if a thousand suns rose at once in the sky…".

Light, not stone. The liṅga may be carved from stone, but the verse reminds us what it represents: the pillar of light from the Liṅgodbhava story, the light of awareness itself.

💡 Gītā 11.12

"If the light of a thousand suns blazed at once in the sky, it might resemble that great Being": famously quoted by J. Robert Oppenheimer.

💡 Jyoti

"Light": hence jyotirliṅga, "liṅga of light".

Bring feeling, not just offerings. A little sincere devotion shines brighter than any display.

ZAPPY: What makes worship real?

PROF. BOLT: This verse says it: bhāva and bhakti, feeling and devotion. Not how much you offer.

Feeling and devotion are the real offering to a light brighter than suns.

VERSE 7 · GOING DEEPER

THE EIGHT-PETALLED LOTUS

The eight petals. The liṅga is pictured on an eight-petalled lotus, a common base in temple iconography and in yantras. Eight directions, eight forms of Śiva (earth, water, fire, air, space, sun, moon and the self): the whole universe as the seat of the liṅga.

Cause of everything. Sarva samudbhava kāraṇa: the cause from which everything arises. The liṅga isn't one thing among many; it represents the source.

Eight kinds of poverty. Aṣṭa daridra. Lists vary, but they go well beyond money: poverty of health, knowledge, courage, family, food, friends, reputation and peace of mind. Like the eight Lakṣmīs of the Devi tradition, the point is that wealth comes in many forms.

💡 Aṣṭamūrti

Śiva's eight forms: earth, water, fire, air, space, sun, moon and the sacrificer (the self). Kālidāsa opens his play Śakuntalā praising them.

💡 Aṣṭalakṣmī

The eight forms of Lakṣmī: wealth of grain, courage, children, victory, knowledge and more.

Look at all the ways you might be poor, not just in money. Real abundance covers health, wisdom, courage and peace.

ZAPPY: Eight kinds of poverty?

PROF. BOLT: Not just money. Lack of health, knowledge, courage, children, food, friends, fame and peace: tradition lists many. The liṅga ends them all.

Set on an eight-petalled lotus, He ends every kind of lack.

VERSE 8 · GOING DEEPER

BEYOND THE BEYOND

The gods' own worship. Suraguru is Bṛhaspati, the gods' teacher; suravara is Indra, their king. Even they worship the liṅga, with flowers from suravana, the gods' garden (Nandana).

Parātpara. "Beyond the beyond", higher than the highest. After all the gold, sandal, lotuses and heavenly flowers, the hymn points past them to what the liṅga really is.

Paramātman. The Supreme Self: not a distant god, but the deepest Self in all. The last verse of the aṣṭakam lands in Vedānta, where our Nirvāṇa Ṣaṭkam lives.

💡 Nandana

Indra's heavenly garden, home of the wish-fulfilling pārijāta tree.

💡 Bṛhaspati

Guru of the gods, lord of the planet Jupiter (Guru) in Indian astronomy.

Enjoy the decorations of worship, but don't stop there. They point to the Self beyond all forms.

ZAPPY: After all the decorations, 'beyond the beyond'?

PROF. BOLT: The flowers and jewels are for us. The liṅga itself points to what's beyond all of it: the Supreme Self.

Under all the decoration: the Supreme Self, beyond the highest.

PHALAŚRUTI · GOING DEEPER

THE FRUIT OF CHANTING

The phalaśruti. As with most hymns, a closing verse names the fruit of chanting: an encouragement to keep at it.

Śivena saha modate. "Rejoices together with Śiva". Not just arriving somewhere, but sharing Śiva's own joy. The goal is companionship with the divine.

Where it's sung. The Liṅgāṣṭakam is sung at evening ārati in countless Śiva temples, on Mondays, and on Mahāśivarātri night, often as the abhiṣeka is completed.

💡 Author

The hymn's author is not known for certain; it's often attributed to Ādi Śaṅkara.

💡 Mahāśivarātri

The night the Liṅgodbhava is remembered: devotees keep vigil and worship the liṅga through the night.

Worship isn't only about where you'll end up. It's about whose company you keep, and finding joy in it now.

ZAPPY: Rejoices WITH Śiva?

PROF. BOLT: Not just reaching His world, but sharing His joy. Companionship, not just a destination.

Not just to reach Śiva, but to rejoice with Him.

ZAPPY: So what am I really bowing to?

PROF. BOLT: Not a stone. The infinite, which Brahmā and Viṣṇu couldn't measure, and which lives in you as the Self.

Liṅgāṣṭakam: tat praṇamāmi sadāśiva liṅgam, to that sign of the infinite, I bow. 🙏
