How Many Nuclear Weapons Exist, and How Does Anyone Count Them?

The number everyone quotes is about 12,000. No government announced it. It was worked out by researchers using satellite photos, budget documents, plutonium arithmetic and a great deal of patience.

ABOUT 8 MINUTES

01

A number nobody announces

Today, 19 November 2026, the leaders of the United States, China and Russia are due to sit at one table in Shenzhen, China, with nuclear arms cuts on the agenda.

It is the first meeting of its kind between the leaders of the world’s three largest nuclear powers. [1] Even the White House has played down expectations; a senior official said they did not expect any agreement. [2]

Here is the odd part. To cut something, you need to know how much there is. Yet the figure in every news report, about 12,187 nuclear warheads in the world as of January 2026, was not announced by any government. [3] It is an estimate, built by a small group of researchers from public clues.

Of the nine countries with nuclear weapons, only three (the US, the UK and France) have ever declared how many they hold. [4] The rest, including India, never have.

Prof. BoltAn estimate. A guess has no method. This one has a very good one.

02

One number is really three

“How many nuclear weapons?” has three honest answers, and news reports often mix them up.

The total inventory counts every warhead that still exists, including old ones that have been retired and are waiting in a queue to be taken apart. The military stockpile is the smaller group a country keeps ready for possible use. The deployed warheads are the ones actually sitting on missiles or at bomber bases. [3] [5]

About 2,100 of the deployed warheads, all American, Russian, British and French, are kept on high alert, ready to launch at short notice. [5] The usable stockpile alone carries the explosive force of more than 135,000 Hiroshima bombs. [6]

04

From 70,000 to 12,000, and turning again

The world’s count peaked at about 70,300 warheads in 1986, at the height of the Cold War. Most of the fall since then happened in the 1990s, after the Soviet Union broke up. [5] The US stockpile alone had peaked even earlier, at 31,225 in 1967. [7]

So the headline number is still shrinking, slowly, as old retired warheads are taken apart. But the number kept ready for use has started growing again. [5] SIPRI’s 2026 Yearbook describes countries leaning on nuclear weapons more, reversing decades of effort to reduce their numbers and role. [8]

The rules have thinned too. New START, the last treaty limiting American and Russian strategic weapons, expired on 5 February 2026. [9] For the first time since the early 1970s, there is no legal cap on either country’s strategic forces. [10]

05

The counter’s toolkit

The estimates come mainly from two researchers at FAS, Hans Kristensen and Matt Korda, who have explained their method publicly. [4] They piece the count together from several kinds of clue, each checking the others:

Where the clues come from
Government papers & budgets→Treaty data→Fissile-material limits→Satellite images→Leaks & conversations→Estimate

Budgets and documents describe weapon programmes, even when the totals are secret. [4] Treaty data was once the richest source: New START allowed each side up to 18 on-site inspections a year and about 2,000 notifications a year. [11] Inspections stopped during COVID and never restarted. [10]

Fissile material sets a ceiling. A warhead needs a few kilograms of plutonium or highly enriched uranium, so knowing how much a country has made tells you the most it could have built. The researchers do not simply divide, because countries keep material in reserve. [4]

Then come satellite photos of bases and construction, and, used carefully, leaks and off-the-record conversations. Social media is treated with suspicion, because reports often repeat each other. [4]

06

Counting from space, and checking the answer

In June 2021, researchers at the James Martin Center for Nonproliferation Studies in California were studying commercial satellite pictures of China’s western desert. They spotted about 120 missile silos under construction near Yumen. [12] Weeks later, Matt Korda at FAS used the same kind of images to find a second field of about 110 silos near Hami, some 380 km away. FAS called it the biggest expansion of China’s nuclear arsenal ever. [13]

The giveaway was almost comic: identical white shelter domes laid out in neat grids, so the builders could work out of sight. That repetition is what led the analysts from one field to the next. [14]

But how good are these estimates? There is one clean test. In February 2009, Kristensen and a colleague estimated the US stockpile at 5,200 warheads. [15] In May 2010, the US declassified the real figure for that date: 5,113. [7] By our arithmetic, the outside estimate was off by less than 2%.

That window has since closed again. In 2019 the Pentagon refused a request to release the current stockpile number. [16]

07

Where India fits, and a plant near Mysuru

India has never said how many nuclear weapons it has. So the estimate starts with plutonium. The International Panel on Fissile Materials estimates that India had made about 730 kg of weapons-grade plutonium by early 2026, give or take about 170 kg. At roughly 4 kg per warhead, that is enough for 140 to 225 warheads. [17]

The researchers then look at India’s missiles, aircraft and submarines and judge how many warheads that force needs. Their answer: up to 190. [18] Even the two big trackers read India differently. SIPRI estimates that 12 Indian warheads may now be deployed on a submarine on patrol; FAS lists none as deployed. [19] [5] The government’s stated policy is “credible minimum deterrence” and “no first use”: India says it will use nuclear weapons only in retaliation for a nuclear attack. [20] [21]

Karnataka has its own place in these reports. About 15 km from Mysuru, at Ratnahalli, sits the Rare Materials Plant, a uranium enrichment facility using gas centrifuges, machines that spin uranium gas to concentrate the useful kind. [22] Its work is secret, but analysts at the Institute for Science and International Security have watched it for years through commercial satellite images, the same method used on China. They believe it has supplied enriched uranium for India’s nuclear-powered submarine, INS Arihant. [23] A second, larger enrichment complex has been reported in Chitradurga district. [24]

Everything about the Mysuru plant’s output here comes from outside analysts and reports; the government does not publish details.
08

The world’s most important estimate

It is strange, when you think about it. The weapons that shaped the last 80 years of world politics are counted the way an astronomer counts stars in a distant galaxy: from light, shadows and arithmetic, never by touching them.

And the method only works as well as the clues allow. With New START gone and fewer governments publishing anything, the clues are getting thinner just as the numbers start rising. Whatever is said at Shenzhen, the first question for any future deal is the one in this article’s title: how many are there, and how would anyone check?

Until someone opens the doors, the best answer we have will keep coming from people looking down from space and adding up kilograms.
