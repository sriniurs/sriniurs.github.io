India's New Plastic Money: Can You Wash a ₹20 Note?

India has approved field trials of polymer ₹10 and ₹20 notes. The promise sounds almost magical: notes that last longer, resist grime and carry security features paper cannot easily copy. But what exactly is a plastic note, and what was Mysuru doing on the old trial list?

ABOUT 8 MINUTES

1

The ₹20 note in the washing machine

Most of us have learnt one thing about paper currency the hard way: notes age fast. They crumple in pockets, soak up sweat, collect grime at tea stalls, survive vegetable markets only just, and occasionally go through the washing machine by mistake. Low-value notes suffer the most because they change hands constantly. That is the practical problem behind India’s latest currency experiment.[1]

In July 2026, the government approved the Reserve Bank of India’s proposal to print one billion ₹10 notes and one billion ₹20 notes on polymer for field trials. That is two billion trial notes in all, with a combined face value of ₹3,000 crore. The approval is important, but the word trial is even more important. India is not replacing all paper money. It is testing whether polymer works better in Indian conditions.[1]

2

Plastic, yes — but not shopping-bag plastic

When people hear “plastic money”, they often imagine something thick and glossy like a laminated card. Polymer banknotes are not like that. They are thin, flexible banknotes made from a special plastic substrate. The Bank of England describes polymer notes as “a thin, flexible plastic”, while the Reserve Bank of Australia simply calls polymer “a type of plastic”.[3][4]

The point is not to make money feel like a toy. It is to make a note that can stay in circulation longer. Paper notes rely on cotton-based substrate and many printed security elements. They work well, but they get limp, dirty and torn. Polymer has a smoother surface, resists moisture better, and springs back more easily after folding or scrunching. That is why central banks that moved to polymer often talk first about durability and cleanliness, not glamour.[3][4]

Familiar feel, proven printing methods, but tends to soil, tear and wear out faster in heavy everyday use.

3

The invisible window that paper cannot easily mimic

Polymer becomes really interesting when you look at security. Because the substrate itself is plastic, central banks can build a clear window into the note rather than trying to imitate one on top of it. The Reserve Bank of Australia says the clear window is an integral part of the note, not an added patch, and that multiple security features can live inside it.[4]

That matters because counterfeiters do not merely photocopy colours; they try to imitate the difficult bits. A genuine polymer note can combine a clear window, embossed detail, specialised foils, see-through devices and tilt effects. The Bank of England lists the see-through window and hologram change as key public checks on its polymer notes.[3]

India’s 2026 parliamentary answer is careful here. It does not promise that polymer will end counterfeiting. It says international studies suggest polymer notes have a significantly higher lifespan than paper notes, and earlier official answers said the primary objective of polymer notes was longer life. Security is an advantage, but longevity is the leading motive.[1][7]

4

Australia tried it first. Then much of the world followed.

Polymer banknotes are not a brand-new 2026 invention. Australia issued its first polymer banknote — a commemorative $10 note — in 1988. The Reserve Bank of Australia describes that note as a trial for the new substrate, followed by the world’s first full polymer banknote series introduced between 1992 and 1996.[5]

That history matters because central banks do not change currency materials casually. Banknotes must work in wallets, cash drawers, sorting machines, ATMs and counting rooms. If a country keeps polymer for decades, it is a sign that the substrate has passed many boring but essential tests.

Other central banks later made similar arguments. The Bank of England moved to polymer because the notes are harder to counterfeit, last longer and have smoother surfaces that resist dirt and moisture better. In short, polymer won not because it looked futuristic, but because it solved several old practical problems at once.[3]

5

Is plastic money actually cheaper?

Here is the clever part. Polymer notes usually cost more to produce per piece. But if one note lasts much longer, the total cost of keeping that denomination alive may fall. The Bank of England says a polymer £5 note is expected to last about 2.5 times longer than its paper predecessor. An earlier Indian parliamentary answer referred to Australian experience suggesting polymer notes can last around four times longer than paper notes, while also citing a TERI study that found polymer could be more environmentally friendly if the longer life is taken into account.[3][6]

That means the arithmetic is about lifecycle cost, not printing cost alone. Suppose a polymer note costs more to print but survives two or three times as long. The central bank may need fewer replacement batches, fewer transport cycles and less frequent destruction of soiled notes. The public sees one note. The currency system sees a supply chain.[6]

6

The Mysuru connection: this story began years ago

This is where the article becomes deliciously local. India’s polymer-note story is older than it sounds. In 2012, and again in 2013 and 2014 parliamentary replies, the government said field trials of ₹10 polymer notes were planned in five cities chosen for varied geography and climate: Kochi, Mysore, Jaipur, Bhubaneswar and Shimla.[2][7]

Mysuru was on that list because a field trial is not merely about whether a note looks pretty. It is about how the note behaves in different humidity levels, temperatures, handling habits and circulation patterns. A note that works in one city may curl, scratch or accumulate grime differently somewhere else. That is why trial geography matters.

Those early trials did not lead to a national polymer rollout then. That makes the 2026 approval more interesting: India is revisiting the idea, now with two denominations and a clearer pathway — field trials first, regular issue only if the results justify it.[1]

7

Will ATMs, counting machines and shopkeepers cope?

A new note substrate is not just a design decision. Machines must recognise it. Sorting equipment must judge whether it is fit or unfit. Cash handlers must learn how it behaves when folded, stacked and counted. Shops must get used to the slightly different feel. And the public must stop assuming that a smoother note is fake simply because it feels unfamiliar.

That is one reason field trials matter so much. They reveal whether notes jam in machines, whether people crease them in troublesome ways, whether printed elements wear out, and whether the note remains easy to authenticate. Polymer’s advantages are real, but a banknote is only successful if it survives human habits as well as engineering tests.

There is also a useful RBI reminder here. Even today, a paper note with writing or colour/oil stains remains legal tender as long as it is decipherable. Currency systems have to deal with messy real life, not ideal wallets.[8]

8

So, what changes for you?

For now, the answer is: not much, immediately. The government has approved field trials of ₹10 and ₹20 polymer notes. It has also said there is no proposal to replace paper currency with polymer notes across the board. If regular issue follows successful trials, polymer and paper notes are expected to circulate alongside each other.[1]

But the bigger story is what this says about money. We often think of a note as a piece of printed paper. Central banks think of it as a carefully engineered public tool: durable enough for vegetable markets, secure enough to deter counterfeiters, simple enough for ordinary people to trust, and cheap enough to keep replacing at scale. Polymer is an attempt to improve all four at once.

So can you wash a ₹20 note? If India eventually issues polymer ₹20s, an accidental spin in the washing machine may no longer feel like a minor financial emergency. Just do not treat that as a new laundry recommendation.

Bottom line: India is testing polymer notes because they may last longer and support better security features. Mysuru was already part of the story years ago, and the next chapter depends on what the field trials actually show.
