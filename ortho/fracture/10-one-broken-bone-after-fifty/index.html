THE BROKEN BONE

ONE BROKEN BONE AFTER FIFTY.
COULD ANOTHER FRACTURE BE WAITING?

The wrist may heal. But what did that fracture reveal about bone strength — and how can a family protect the years ahead?

FIRST FRACTUREBONE HEALTHPREVENT THE NEXT

About 8 minutes to read · Patient and family education

01

The wrist healed. So why did the doctor ask about her bones?

When 66-year-old Shanta slipped on the front steps, she reached out to save herself. Her wrist broke. A plaster, several follow-up visits and weeks of careful movement later, the fracture was healing. At her next appointment, she expected only one question: “Is the wrist all right?”

Instead, the clinician asked whether she had ever broken another bone, how she had fallen, and whether anyone had checked her bone strength. Shanta was confused. “Why look at the rest of my skeleton? It was my wrist that broke.”

The short answer: a broken bone after about age 50, particularly after a minor fall, may be the first warning of fragile bones. The wrist needs care, but so does the possibility of another fracture in the future. A previous fragility fracture is an important risk factor for another one. [1][2][9]

Shanta is fictional. Her story is a way to explain the questions every patient and family should understand after a low-trauma fracture.

02

What makes a fracture a warning sign?

Picture two cups. One is strong; the other has become thin and brittle. The same bump may leave one intact and crack the other. Bones are much more complicated than cups, but the comparison helps explain why the amount of force matters.

A fragility fracture is a fracture caused by a force that would not ordinarily break healthy bone, often a fall from standing height or less. Common sites include the wrist, hip, spine and upper arm. Osteoporosis is one possible reason, but the clinician also considers other causes of weakened bone. [3][8]

Not every fracture in somebody over 50 is a fragility fracture. A high-speed road accident could break a healthy bone. Conversely, a minor fall in an older adult should raise a useful question: was the bone unusually weak?

The broken bone tells us what happened today. The force that broke it may tell us something about tomorrow.
03

Can osteoporosis be present without any pain?

Yes. Osteoporosis often has no obvious symptoms before a fracture. Bone slowly loses strength, but there may be no ache, swelling or outward sign. Shanta could cook, travel and climb the stairs right up to the day she fell. That did not necessarily mean her bones were strong. [3]

Ageing and menopause can increase risk. A family history of hip fracture, previous low-trauma fractures, low body weight, smoking, some long-term medicines and certain medical conditions may also matter. None of these alone proves osteoporosis. The pattern of risk factors helps the clinician decide what to investigate. [1][8]

The spine deserves special attention. Sometimes a vertebra — one of the bones in the back — develops a compression fracture without a dramatic fall. New persistent back pain, loss of height or an increasingly stooped back may be reasons to ask for assessment. [1]

ZappySo no pain doesn’t mean no osteoporosis?
Prof. BoltExactly. Bone strength cannot be judged by how normal someone feels.
04

How can doctors look for weak bones?

Shanta imagines a machine that scans every bone and gives one score. The real assessment is broader. Her clinician starts with her fracture history, how the injury happened, other illnesses, medicines, falls, nutrition and family history. Sometimes blood tests help identify treatable contributors to bone loss. [8][10]

A DXA scan measures bone mineral density, often at the hip and lower spine. A low result can support the diagnosis of osteoporosis. But bone density is not the entire story: a person may have a fragility fracture even without a result in the osteoporosis range. A previous fracture itself can influence the decision to treat. [1][8]

Clinicians may use FRAX, a tool that combines age and other risk factors, sometimes with bone density, to estimate the chance of certain fractures over the next ten years. The interpretation depends on the population and clinical context. It is not a crystal ball, and it does not predict exactly who will fracture. [1][8]

One number does not replace a proper fracture-risk assessment. The fall, previous breaks and the bone-density result all matter.
THE DEEP DIVEHow to protect the bones — and prevent the next fall
05

Why do doctors take the next two years seriously?

Shanta says, “At least I have twenty years before I need to worry again.” Her clinician explains that future-fracture risk is not spread evenly over time. After a fragility fracture, the risk of another is often particularly high soon afterwards, especially during the first couple of years. This is sometimes called imminent fracture risk. [2]

That does not mean Shanta is destined to break another bone. It means there is an opportunity worth acting on now, while her fracture is still fresh in the family’s mind. A prevention plan should not be forgotten simply because the plaster has come off.

In some healthcare systems, a Fracture Liaison Service helps make that happen. It links fracture treatment with bone-health evaluation, possible osteoporosis treatment and follow-up. In hospitals without such a service, the patient can still ask which clinician will coordinate the next steps. [4][10]

ZappyIs this a countdown to another fracture?
Prof. BoltNo. It is a reason to make prevention part of recovery, not an afterthought.
06

Can milk, sunlight and walking solve the whole problem?

Good nutrition and safe activity are important. Bones need adequate calcium, and vitamin D helps the body use it. Muscle strength, balance and weight-bearing activity also matter. But there is no single food, walk or supplement that guarantees protection from another fracture. [6]

For someone with significant osteoporosis or high fracture risk, lifestyle changes alone may not be enough. Medicines that reduce fracture risk are often considered. Different treatments work in different ways, and their choice depends on fracture risk, kidney function, other illnesses, costs, preferences and follow-up. That decision belongs to the treating clinician and patient. [1][7]

More is not automatically better. Taking large quantities of supplements or starting prescription treatment without evaluation is not a substitute for a plan. Calcium intake, vitamin D testing or supplementation, and exercise should be tailored to individual circumstances.

The foundation

Nutrition, appropriate activity, strength, balance and safer surroundings.

When risk is high

A clinician may recommend prescription bone-protecting treatment and structured follow-up.

07

What if the bigger danger is the next fall?

The bone and the fall are two sides of the same event. Imagine strengthening the door but leaving a slippery step outside it. Shanta’s family makes the entrance safer: better lighting, a secure handrail and less clutter. The clinician also asks whether she has dizziness, poor vision, troublesome footwear or medicines that might affect balance.

A proper falls assessment looks beyond the floor. Depending on the patient, it may involve checking walking and balance, muscle strength, blood pressure, medications, vision and home hazards. NICE recommends comprehensive falls assessment for older people with certain higher-risk features, including falls requiring medical treatment. [5]

Exercise can reduce falls risk when it is matched to the person and includes the right balance and strength components. But Shanta’s recently broken wrist and any other conditions affect what she can safely do now. Rehabilitation must be individualised. [5][6]

When to seek medical help: A new fall with significant pain, inability to bear weight, a visibly deformed limb, or a new severe back pain needs medical assessment. Sudden weakness, loss of consciousness, chest pain or symptoms suggesting a stroke need emergency care. Do not test a suspected fracture by repeatedly making the person walk. [3][5]
08

What should the family ask at the next appointment?

Shanta returns with her daughter and a short set of questions. Was the fracture caused by a low-energy fall? Does she need DXA or other tests? Should she receive treatment to reduce future fracture risk? Who will review the treatment and check for falls hazards? These are useful questions, not demands for a particular scan or pill.

Her clinician explains that risk reduction is a plan, not a one-time purchase. It may involve reassessment, attention to medicines and nutrition, safe strengthening, and changes at home. The goal is not to promise that no fracture will ever happen. The goal is to reduce avoidable risk while preserving independence. [1][4][5]

That brings the ten-article series full circle. We began with a fall in a bathroom and a broken hip. We end with the idea that matters after any fragility fracture: treating the break is vital, but asking why the bone broke may protect the years ahead.

After a low-trauma fracture in later life, ask two questions: How will this bone heal? And how can we lower the chance of another one?
ZappySo the best time to prevent fracture number two is after number one?
Prof. BoltBetter still, before number one. But after it happens, the opportunity is too important to miss.
KEEP

The fridge-door cheat sheet

1. Why ask about osteoporosis after a fracture?

A low-trauma fracture after 50 may reveal weak bones and an increased risk of another fracture.

2. Does every fracture after 50 mean osteoporosis?

No. The force of injury, fracture pattern and other risk factors matter.

3. Is a bone-density scan the only test?

No. Clinical history, medicines, prior fractures, falls and sometimes blood tests are also important.

4. Is food and walking enough?

They help, but people at higher risk may also need prescription treatment.

5. What should the family do next?

Discuss bone-health assessment, fall prevention and a follow-up plan with the treating team.
