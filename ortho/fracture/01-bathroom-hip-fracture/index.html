THE BROKEN BONE

I ONLY SLIPPED IN THE BATHROOM.
HOW DID MY HIP BREAK?

A short fall. A major fracture. The hidden story of bone strength — and the journey from hospital to walking again.

ONE SMALL FALLA FRAGILE BONE?WHAT NEXT?

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

A slip, a cry, and one impossible question

Lakshmi, 74, was reaching for her towel after a bath. Her foot slipped on the wet tiles. She fell sideways and landed on her right hip. It was not a staircase accident or a violent collision. It was the sort of fall any family might dismiss as a bad moment.

But Lakshmi could not get up. Each attempt to move her leg produced sharp pain near her groin. Her daughter asked the question that gives this story its title: “How can such a small fall break such a big bone?”

Here is the short answer: the fall supplies the force, but the bone’s strength helps determine whether it breaks. As we get older, bones sometimes lose strength silently. A seemingly minor fall can then produce a major injury. [3][4]

Lakshmi is a fictional patient whose experience combines common features of hip fractures. The explanation, rather than the individual story, comes from published clinical guidance.

02

A bone can look strong and still be fragile

Imagine a sturdy-looking chair whose wooden legs have slowly weakened inside. It may hold up for years. Then one ordinary movement exposes the hidden damage. Bone is not wood, of course. It is living tissue, continually renewed by cells that remove and rebuild it.

With osteoporosis, the internal network of bone becomes less substantial and its structure more vulnerable. This usually causes no pain by itself. People can shop, cook, walk and climb stairs without knowing they have fragile bones. A fracture may be the first clue. [4][5]

Not every older adult has osteoporosis. Other conditions and medicines can affect bone strength. And a person with healthy bones may still fracture a hip after sufficient force. The lesson is simply that the apparent size of a fall does not tell the full story about the bone underneath.

The fall may be the trigger. Fragile bone may be the reason the trigger caused a fracture.

03

But isn’t the thigh bone the strongest bone?

The femur, or thigh bone, is large and strong. At the top, it has a rounded head that fits into the pelvis like a ball in a cup. Just below the ball is the narrow femoral neck. Slightly farther down are the bony bumps called the trochanters.

These are two common neighbourhoods for hip fractures: femoral-neck fractures and trochanteric fractures. A sideways fall often drives the impact into this upper part of the femur. The precise fracture depends on the direction of force and the condition of the bone. [2][3]

Families often say the “hip joint has broken”. More accurately, a hip fracture usually means a break in the upper femur. Where it breaks is important, because it changes the treatment choice.

04

If the X-ray is normal, can she go home?

A typical hip fracture causes pain around the hip or groin and makes standing or walking difficult. Sometimes the affected leg appears shorter or turned outward. But not every fracture looks dramatic. Some are impacted or have not moved much out of position. [3]

An X-ray is usually the first picture doctors take. If it shows a fracture, the team can plan treatment. If it looks normal but the story and examination still strongly suggest a hip fracture, doctors should not simply dismiss the possibility. NICE recommends MRI in that situation. If MRI cannot be obtained within 24 hours or is unsuitable, CT can be considered. [1]

After a fall: severe hip or groin pain, inability to bear weight, a visibly abnormal leg, or ongoing pain despite a normal X-ray needs prompt medical assessment. Do not force someone with a suspected hip fracture to stand or walk. [3]

Walking does not prove that a hip is unbroken. That misconception can delay the right diagnosis.

THE DEEP DIVEWhy treatment is not the same for every broken hip
05

Why is the family told she may need surgery?

Lakshmi’s daughter expects plaster, as with a broken wrist. But the hip is deep inside the body, carries body weight and cannot be immobilised in the same simple way. For most older adults with a hip fracture, surgery is the main treatment. The goal is to make the hip stable enough to relieve pain and allow rehabilitation. [1][2]

The operation is chosen according to the site and displacement of the fracture, the person’s mobility before the injury and other health factors. Some fractures are held together using a screw, plate or nail. For certain displaced fractures of the femoral neck, the damaged ball of the hip is replaced, either with a partial hip replacement or, for selected patients, a total hip replacement. [7][10]

It is not accurate to say that a nail is always better than a replacement, or that every older patient needs the same operation. The surgeon explains which pattern is present and why a particular operation makes sense.

06

Why are doctors keen to operate early?

After a hip fracture, pain, immobility and illness can feed into one another. An older person lying in bed for a prolonged period risks becoming weaker and losing confidence in walking. The hospital team therefore assesses pain, hydration, medicines, heart and lung conditions, and fitness for an operation promptly.

NICE recommends surgery on the day of admission or the following day, while identifying and treating correctable medical problems immediately. That is a guideline for good coordinated care, not a promise that every individual can safely be operated on at exactly the same hour. [1]

The operation is one part of a larger team effort. Surgeons, anaesthetists, physicians, nurses, physiotherapists and, when available, geriatric specialists each have a role. They also watch for confusion, nutrition problems, blood clots and other issues that can complicate recovery. [2][9][10]

A hip fracture is not just a bone problem. In an older person, it is a whole-person recovery problem.

07

The bone is fixed. Why isn’t she walking normally?

The day after surgery, Lakshmi expects to feel as she did before the fall. Instead, she is tired, her muscles feel weak and she needs a walker. That does not automatically mean the operation has failed. Surgery stabilises or replaces the damaged part; regaining balance, endurance and independence takes practice.

Unless there is a medical or surgical reason not to, NICE recommends physiotherapy assessment and assisted mobilisation the day after surgery, with regular review. The surgical team specifies how much weight the person can put through the operated leg. [1][8]

Recovery varies widely. Some people regain much of their previous independence; others need continued assistance or may not return to exactly their former level. Progress is shaped by pre-fracture strength, other illnesses, memory and cognition, support at home and rehabilitation.

For Lakshmi, a modest first victory is standing safely with help. Next comes walking a short distance, transferring to a chair and, gradually, building confidence. The family’s job is to follow the rehabilitation plan, not rush her into a contest with the calendar.

Prof. BoltExactly. A repaired bone and a recovered life are not the same milestone.

08

The fracture is treated. Has the real problem been treated?

Weeks later, Lakshmi is home. The bathroom floor is dry, the night light works and the family has cleared loose rugs. These changes matter. Yet one question remains: why did the bone break after a fall from standing height?

A fragility fracture should prompt assessment of bone health and future fracture risk. Doctors may review the person’s medicines, nutrition, calcium and vitamin D status, previous fractures and whether bone-density testing or osteoporosis treatment is appropriate. The right approach depends on the individual. [4][5]

Preventing the next fall matters as much as investigating bone strength. Vision, footwear, balance, muscle strength, medicines that cause dizziness, and hazards such as wet tiles or poor lighting are all worth reviewing. A structured falls assessment is preferable to assuming the person was simply careless. [6]

Lakshmi’s daughter finally understands: treating the broken hip repaired this accident. Looking after her bones and reducing fall risks may help prevent another.

The biggest lesson: a small fall with a major fracture is a reason to ask about bone strength—not only about the accident.

KEEP

The fridge-door cheat sheet

1. Small fall, big fracture?

It can happen when bone strength is reduced, often by osteoporosis.

2. Could I still walk with a fracture?

Sometimes. Walking does not rule it out.

3. Why surgery?

Usually to stabilise the broken hip or replace the damaged joint part and enable recovery.

4. What happens after surgery?

Supported movement, rehabilitation, medical care and gradual return to everyday activities.

5. What must not be forgotten?

Bone-health assessment and a plan to reduce future falls and fractures.

A suspected hip fracture after a fall requires urgent medical assessment; this article is not a guide to self-treatment.
