THE BROKEN BONE

MY FRACTURE HAS HEALED.
WHY CAN’T I MOVE MY JOINT NORMALLY?

The plaster is off. The X-ray looks reassuring. Why does the wrist still feel stiff, swollen and weak?

BONE UNIONSTIFFNESSREHABILITATION

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The X-ray says healed. But the hand says otherwise.

Six weeks ago, 58-year-old Anitha slipped while stepping off a wet verandah. She put out her right hand to stop the fall. The wrist swelled almost immediately. An X-ray showed a break in the radius — one of the two forearm bones — near the wrist. Her fracture was treated in a cast.

Today, the clinic removes her plaster. The follow-up X-ray is reassuring, and the doctor says the bone has healed enough to begin the next stage. Anitha smiles and reaches for her handbag. But her fingers feel clumsy. Turning her palm upwards is difficult. Even holding a steel tumbler feels unfamiliar.

“Doctor, if the bone has joined, why can’t I move my wrist normally?” she asks. The short answer: bone healing and recovery of movement are two different jobs. While the bone is protected, joints can become stiff, muscles weaker and tissues swollen. Those changes often need time and guided rehabilitation. [1][2]

Anitha is a fictional teaching example. Her question is familiar to many people after a fracture.

02

Was keeping it still a mistake?

No. In a suitable fracture, the cast protects the broken pieces while the body makes new bone. Imagine repairing a cracked garden pipe. First you stabilise the joint so the repair is not pulled apart. Only later can the pipe withstand its usual use. A cast is not a mistake simply because the arm feels unfamiliar afterwards.

But immobilisation comes with a trade-off. A wrist held in one position for weeks does not practise bending, turning or gripping. Nearby muscles are used less. Soft tissues around the joint can lose some of their normal flexibility. It is therefore common for the wrist to feel stiff or weak when a cast comes off. [1][3][4]

The important balance is enough protection to let the fracture heal, but not more restriction than the injury requires. The treating team decides that balance for each fracture.

03

Why does the wrist feel like a rusty door hinge?

A joint is not just two pieces of bone. It has a smooth moving surface, a surrounding capsule, ligaments, tendons and muscles. These structures must glide and stretch together for a movement to feel easy.

After an injury, swelling and pain encourage us to guard the area. During immobilisation, some tissues become less accustomed to movement. When Anitha tries to bend her wrist, the limitation may come from stiffness of these structures, discomfort, lingering swelling and reduced muscle control — not from a fresh break in the healed bone. [3][5][6]

Think of a door that has not been opened for weeks. The hinge may be intact, but it no longer swings freely at the first push. Unlike a door, a living joint also needs muscles and nerves to coordinate the movement.

A healed bone can be strong enough to start rehabilitation while the joint around it is still stiff.
04

And why does a light cup suddenly feel heavy?

Anitha has another surprise. Her wrist is not only stiff. Her hand feels weak. Before the fall she could squeeze a wet cloth, open a jar and lift a cooking vessel without thinking. Now the same tasks are tiring.

Muscles become less conditioned when they are not used normally. Grip strength and coordination may lag behind the X-ray. The NHS and AAOS both describe weakness and reduced movement after immobilisation, with rehabilitation helping to restore function. [1][2][3]

Swelling can add to the problem. Puffy fingers may not bend completely, and a swollen wrist may feel tight or uncomfortable. Some swelling can persist as activity resumes, but it should generally be improving rather than steadily worsening. [5][10]

This does not mean every patient needs the same exercise programme. Some fractures require extra protection; others allow earlier controlled movement.

05

Does “healed” mean everything should be normal?

On an X-ray, the doctor looks for signs that the fracture is joining or has united sufficiently. But an X-ray cannot directly measure how easily Anitha buttons a blouse, turns a doorknob or washes her hair. These are questions of function.

Consider three different milestones: bone union — the break is knitting together; movement — the joint regains a usable range; and function — strength and confidence return for real tasks. They overlap, but they do not necessarily arrive on the same date. [2][4]

The team may assess movement, grip, swelling, pain and how the injury is affecting ordinary activities. Someone who works at a desk may have different demands from someone who lifts heavy bags all day. That is why a single “six-week rule” cannot tell every patient when work or sport is safe.

Good recovery often means steady progress across these milestones, rather than demanding full strength the day the plaster comes off.

A reassuring X-ray is a milestone, not a guarantee of instant normal movement.
06

Should she force the joint until it bends?

Anitha’s neighbour has a confident suggestion: “Just push it hard every day. The stiffness will break.” That is an unsafe shortcut. The injury, the fracture pattern and the stage of healing determine how much movement and load are appropriate.

After cast removal, many patients are advised to begin gentle, progressive movements and gradually use the hand for suitable everyday tasks. Some benefit from a hand therapist or physiotherapist. But the exact timing, movements and amount of resistance should come from the treating team. [4][6][8][9]

Pain is information, not a competition. Mild discomfort can occur during approved rehabilitation; sharp, severe or increasing pain, or new symptoms, should prompt reassessment. Pushing a recently treated fracture aggressively can be harmful, particularly when the bone is not yet ready for a load. [2][4]

For Anitha, the useful question is not “How hard can I push?” but “What am I allowed to move, how much may I use it, and what progress should we expect at my next review?”

07

When is stiffness no longer ordinary?

Some stiffness, swelling and weakness after cast removal are common. They often improve gradually. Still, not every painful or stiff joint is simply “part of healing”. Problems such as a poorly aligned joint surface, tendon difficulty, nerve irritation, infection or other complications may require assessment. [1][3][7]

Tell the treating team if movement is not improving as expected, pain persistently worsens, or ordinary activities remain unusually difficult. A new loss of sensation, fingers becoming blue, white or unusually cold, severe swelling, fever or a sudden major increase in pain calls for urgent medical review. [1][7]

It is especially important not to compare recovery across unrelated fractures. A simple, well-aligned wrist fracture may follow a very different course from a complex fracture extending into a joint or one accompanied by significant soft-tissue injury.

Seek urgent assessment for severe or rapidly increasing pain, new numbness, marked colour or temperature change in the fingers, worsening swelling, or fever with concerning symptoms. Do not continue exercises through new severe symptoms. [1][7]
08

What does a good recovery actually look like?

At her next appointments, Anitha notices small changes. She can hold a light tumbler comfortably. Turning a key is easier. She can lift her hand to comb her hair. The improvements seem ordinary — but they are exactly what rehabilitation is trying to restore.

The timetable varies. Some people regain movement relatively quickly; others continue improving for months. The hospital guidance for wrist fractures recognises that stiffness and weakness can persist, even after bone healing is satisfactory. Progress is judged by the particular fracture, examination and daily function — not by a fixed promise. [1][5][9]

If movement remains limited, the team may review the original injury and recent images, check swelling and nerve or tendon function, and decide whether therapy or further investigation is needed. The patient’s question changes from “Is the bone joined?” to “How do we restore the activities that matter to me?”

A fracture can be healed on an X-ray before the limb feels normal. Bone union repairs the break; rehabilitation helps restore movement, strength and confidence.

KEEP

The fridge-door cheat sheet

1. Why is the joint stiff after a fracture?

Swelling, soft-tissue stiffness, pain and time spent immobilised can restrict movement.

2. Why does the limb feel weak?

Muscles and coordination need time to recover after reduced use.

3. Is a good X-ray enough?

No. Bone union, joint movement and everyday function are different milestones.

4. Should I force movements?

No. Follow your own fracture-specific movement and loading instructions.

5. When should I report symptoms?

For worsening pain or poor progress, seek review. New numbness, colour change, severe swelling or fever needs urgent assessment.
