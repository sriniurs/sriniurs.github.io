01

A fracture, an X-ray — and a surprising answer

Forty-two-year-old Ravi slipped while stepping off a wet pavement. He landed on his outstretched hand. His forearm swelled, and turning a door handle became painfully difficult. At the hospital he expected an X-ray, a white plaster cast, and a trip home.

The X-ray showed a broken forearm bone. But the orthopaedic team explained that the pieces were not sitting in a reliable position. They might move again even after being pushed back into place. Surgery was being considered.

Ravi stared at the screen. “It is only a broken bone. Why can’t you just put a plaster?”

Here is the short answer: a cast works when it can hold a fracture in an acceptable position until healing occurs. If the break is unstable, badly displaced, affects an important joint surface, or has other complications, the surgeon may need a different method of stabilization. [1][3][5]

Ravi is a fictional teaching example. The best treatment for a real patient depends on the actual examination and X-rays.

02

Plaster is a bodyguard — not a bone glue

Imagine two broken pieces of a pencil. If you line them up and wrap a protective tube around them, the tube does not reconnect the pencil. It simply keeps the pieces from wobbling while you work on a proper repair.

A cast acts as an outside support. It protects the injured area and limits unwanted movement. The bone itself heals through a biological process in which new tissue bridges the break. The cast does not manufacture new bone or “stick” the fragments together. [1][2]

This is why a well-aligned, reasonably stable fracture can often heal without an operation. But an unstable fracture may shift inside the cast. The skin can look perfectly covered while the X-ray tells a different story.

The question is not “Can we cover it with plaster?” It is “Will the broken pieces stay in a suitable position while healing happens?”

03

Three questions hidden inside every X-ray

Two neighbours may both say, “I broke my wrist.” Yet one goes home in plaster and the other needs surgery. That is not necessarily inconsistent treatment. The breaks may be completely different.

First: where is the break? A fracture near a joint surface may demand more precise alignment than some breaks through a bone shaft. Poor joint alignment can affect how smoothly the joint moves. [1][4]

Second: have the pieces moved? A fracture is called displaced when the fragments have shifted out of their usual relationship. The clinician may need to realign them. That is called reduction. [1][3]

Third: will that alignment hold? Some breaks are relatively stable. Others are likely to shorten, rotate, angle or slip back out of position. Forearm bones are particularly sensitive to alignment because their coordinated movement turns the palm up and down. [5]

04

When is a plaster cast enough?

Consider a simple break whose pieces are already well aligned. Or one that the clinician can gently reposition and then hold securely in a cast. This is the kind of situation where non-operative treatment may make sense. [1][2]

The treating team may first use a splint — a partial cast or supportive device — because a newly injured limb can swell. A complete cast may be applied or adjusted once swelling settles. Depending on the fracture, the team may arrange further X-rays to confirm that the alignment remains acceptable. [2][5]

A cast is not “second-best” treatment. For suitable fractures, it avoids an incision and the risks of surgery. Equally, a cast is not a promise that every break will remain in position: a fracture can shift during follow-up and the plan sometimes changes.

Good non-surgical care is an active plan: correct alignment, appropriate support, follow-up and rehabilitation.

THE DEEP DIVEWhy some fractures need a different kind of support
05

What can a plate, screw or nail do that plaster cannot?

Ravi’s surgeon points to two drawings. In the first, a cast surrounds the outside of the forearm. In the second, a metal plate and screws hold the fragments in line from inside. Both aim to control unwanted movement. They simply do it from different places.

Plates act like internal splints. Screws attach plates or hold certain fragments directly. Nails or rods can run along the central canal of long bones, sometimes with screws that control rotation and length. Wires or pins may secure smaller pieces. The design depends on the bone and break pattern. [3]

This is called internal fixation. Surgeons may choose it when a fracture cannot be held reliably with external support alone, when accurate position is important for function, or when the overall injury demands it. It can make earlier controlled movement possible in some cases, though permitted activity still depends on the injury and surgeon’s instructions. [3][5]

Important: a metal plate does not replace the body’s healing. It holds the pieces while the body does the biological repair.

06

Does an operation mean the fracture was worse?

Not always. A simple-looking break in a very important position may need precise fixation. A more dramatic-looking fracture elsewhere may still heal well in a cast. Decisions depend on alignment, stability, fracture location, open wounds, age, health, activity needs, and the expected balance of benefit and risk. [1][3][4]

There are also cases in which a cast is not a safe final answer. If a fracture communicates with an open wound, bacteria may have a pathway to the bone. An open fracture is an emergency and generally needs urgent specialist treatment, including wound cleaning and stabilization; a plaster alone cannot solve the contamination problem. [6][7]

But surgery has trade-offs: anaesthesia, wound problems, infection, and possible irritation or failure of implants. Even with careful fixation, healing can be slow or incomplete. That is why surgery is not automatically “stronger treatment” for every patient. [3][7]

The right treatment is the one most likely to achieve safe healing and useful function for that particular fracture — not the one with the most metal.

07

The cast is on. Why does follow-up still matter?

A week later Ravi asks another familiar question: “If the bone is protected, why another visit?” Because the first treatment is only the start of healing. Swelling changes; the cast may loosen; and the fracture position may need rechecking. [2][5]

Doctors also assess fingers or toes beyond the injury: colour, warmth, sensation and movement. Increasing tightness, new numbness, worsening pain or difficulty moving the digits can mean pressure on nerves or circulation. Rarely, severe pressure within injured muscles — called compartment syndrome — can threaten the limb. [2][8][10]

Seek urgent medical attention if pain becomes severe or rapidly worsens, fingers or toes turn pale or blue, new numbness or weakness develops, or a cast feels dangerously tight. An open wound with a suspected fracture, or a visibly deformed limb after injury, also needs emergency assessment. Do not attempt to straighten the bone yourself. [6][8][9][10]

As healing progresses, the team may advise when to start moving nearby joints, how much load is safe and when rehabilitation is appropriate. Different fractures have different timelines. [4][9]

08

Which question should you ask instead of “Why not plaster?”

At his review, Ravi understands the choice better. The real issue was never whether plaster was a good or bad invention. It was whether his particular break could stay aligned and function well with outside support alone.

Before agreeing to a treatment plan, he can ask: Where exactly is the fracture? Is it displaced? Is it stable after reduction? Does it involve a joint? What might happen if we choose a cast? What are the benefits and risks of surgery in my case? Clear explanations and discussion of expected recovery are part of good fracture care. [4]

He can also ask what follow-up images are needed, which movements are allowed, when work or sport might be realistic and which symptoms demand an earlier visit. Those answers should come from the treating team, not a general internet timeline.

And the short answer to his first question has not changed: plaster can hold a suitable fracture from outside; fixation may be needed when that support is not enough.

The aim is not to put plaster or metal on a broken bone. The aim is to keep the bone in a useful position while it heals — and help the person regain function.

KEEP

The fridge-door cheat sheet

1. Does a cast heal the bone?

No. It supports the fragments while the body heals them.

2. What makes a cast suitable?

The fracture can be kept in an acceptable position with external support.

3. Why might an operation be needed?

Unstable alignment, important joint involvement, open injury or other complications may make fixation appropriate.

4. Does a plate make bone heal instantly?

No. Metal holds the pieces; living bone still has to join.

5. What must I ask my specialist?

Why this treatment, what can go wrong, what follow-up is needed and which warning signs need urgent review?
