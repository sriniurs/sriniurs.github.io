THE BROKEN BONE

MY X-RAY LOOKS FINE.
COULD I STILL
HAVE A FRACTURE?

The picture looks normal. The pain says otherwise. Why some broken bones hide — and how doctors find them.

NORMAL X-RAY?HIDDEN BREAKSMRI OR CT?

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The X-ray says nothing. The hip says otherwise.

Meera, 69, missed the last step outside her house and landed on her left side. She could move her leg, but standing made her groin hurt. At the hospital, the doctor examined her and requested an X-ray. The family expected to see a clearly broken bone.

The first report did not show an obvious fracture. Her son looked relieved. “So it is only a bruise?” But when Meera tried to stand, the same sharp pain returned. The doctor was not reassured by the picture alone.

The short answer: yes, a bone can be fractured even when the first X-ray looks normal. Some breaks are too subtle to show on that image, especially when the broken pieces have barely moved. Doctors call these occult fractures — fractures that are hidden on ordinary radiographs. [1][2][3]

A normal picture can be good news. It is simply not always the whole story. Meera is a fictional example, not a report of a real patient.

02

Why can a camera miss a crack in a bone?

Think of an X-ray as a shadow picture. It records how X-rays pass through the body and produce an image of its dense structures. Doctors usually take more than one view because a crack can be difficult to see from a particular angle.

Now imagine a thin crack in a ceramic cup. When the edges remain lined up, it may be nearly invisible from across the room. Turn the cup, change the light, or look more closely, and the crack becomes easier to recognize. An undisplaced bone fracture can be similarly difficult to spot.

Small fractures may blend with normal bone outlines; some injuries are clearer on later images. The initial X-ray is still the sensible starting point for most suspected hip fractures. It identifies many breaks quickly, is widely available and helps guide treatment. [2][3]

“Not seen on this X-ray” is different from “definitely not present.”

03

How does a doctor suspect what the picture misses?

Meera points to the same painful spot in her groin every time someone helps her change position. The doctor reviews how she fell, checks the location of the pain, and assesses how safely she can move. Those details matter as much as the first image.

A suspected hip fracture may cause pain in the hip, upper thigh or groin. Some people cannot stand or bear weight. Others can take a few painful steps, particularly if the fracture has not displaced much. So walking after a fall is not a reliable test of whether the hip is unbroken. [4][9]

Pain alone cannot identify a fracture: sprains, bruises and other conditions can hurt too. But the combination of injury, symptoms and examination may justify further testing even when the radiograph seems reassuring.

04

If the X-ray is normal, what happens next?

The doctor explains that Meera should not be sent home to “walk it off” while a significant hip fracture is still suspected. Her pain and inability to bear weight comfortably require further assessment. She receives appropriate supervised care while the team arranges additional imaging. [1][4]

For a suspected hidden hip fracture, MRI is usually the preferred next test. NICE recommends MRI when adequate hip X-rays are negative but clinical suspicion persists. If MRI cannot be performed within 24 hours or is unsuitable, CT is a recognised alternative. ACR guidance also rates MRI without contrast and CT without contrast as appropriate next tests. [1][2][3]

Which test is chosen depends on urgency, safety, availability and the clinical question. If a CT is negative but the doctor still strongly suspects a fracture, the assessment may need to continue rather than treating that result as a guarantee.

Urgent assessment: severe hip or groin pain after a fall, or difficulty standing or bearing weight, needs prompt medical evaluation. Do not force the person to walk in order to “test” the hip. [4]

THE DEEP DIVEDifferent scans answer different questions
05

What can MRI see that a plain X-ray cannot?

An X-ray is especially good at displaying dense bone outlines. MRI creates a different kind of picture: it can reveal changes inside the bone and in surrounding soft tissues. An injured bone may show an abnormal signal before a fine fracture is obvious on plain radiographs. [3][7]

Meera’s MRI reveals a small fracture in the upper femur — the top of the thigh bone near the hip. The bone pieces have not separated dramatically, which helps explain why the first X-ray was difficult to interpret.

The MRI has not “created” a new fracture. It has provided a more sensitive view of the existing injury. The treating specialist now knows where the break is and can decide how to protect and treat it.

Does everyone with pain need an MRI? No. Many straightforward fractures are visible on X-ray, and many painful falls cause no fracture. Additional scans are selected when symptoms and examination create a specific unanswered question. [2][3]

06

CT or MRI — is one always better?

Imagine viewing a house. An X-ray gives a fast outline. CT provides detailed cross-sectional views that are particularly useful for bone shape and fracture lines. MRI is often better at revealing subtle injury inside bone and many soft-tissue problems.

Neither is a magic machine for every situation. CT is generally fast and widely useful; MRI may be unavailable immediately or unsuitable for a particular person. There are also differences in scan duration, radiation exposure and the structures each shows best. [2][3]

For an older adult with persistent suspicion of a hidden hip fracture, the guidance gives MRI an important role, with CT as an appropriate alternative in defined situations. For another bone, the best second test may differ.

The right question is not “Which scan is the most expensive?” It is “Which scan will answer the clinical question safely and in time?”

07

Can a wrist or an overworked foot hide a fracture too?

Yes. A person falls onto an outstretched hand. The wrist hurts near the base of the thumb. The first X-ray does not clearly show a break. One important concern is a scaphoid fracture — a break in one of the small wrist bones. A clinician may protect the wrist in a splint while arranging further assessment. Depending on the local pathway, MRI or CT may be used to investigate an occult fracture. [5][6][8]

There is a different story in a runner whose foot starts hurting after a sudden increase in training. Repeated loading can create a bone stress injury. Early X-rays may look normal, because the earliest changes are not always visible on the radiograph. MRI can show changes within the bone. [7]

These examples are not interchangeable. The examination, likely injury and urgency determine the next steps. A painful hip after a fall, a tender scaphoid and a runner’s foot pain do not all receive the same imaging plan.

One shared principle: a normal first X-ray should be interpreted alongside the story and examination, not in isolation.

08

What should the family take home from Meera’s story?

Once Meera’s fracture is identified, the orthopaedic team discusses treatment based on its location, stability and her overall health. The scan has clarified the diagnosis, but it has not automatically decided whether a cast, restricted activity, fixation or another procedure is appropriate. That remains an individual clinical decision.

Her son asks a better question than before: “The X-ray looked normal, but she was still in pain. What clues made you look again?” The answer is the point of this entire article: the mechanism of injury, persistent pain, difficulty with weight-bearing and examination findings did not match a simple reassuring conclusion. [1][9]

If a clinician reviews you after an injury, ask what the X-ray showed, whether the symptoms fit it, what changes would prompt re-examination, and whether further imaging or follow-up is needed. Do not substitute internet advice for a personal assessment.

A normal X-ray is reassuring only when it fits the person in front of the doctor. When the symptoms tell a different story, the investigation may not be over.

KEEP

The fridge-door cheat sheet

1. Can a fracture be hidden on X-ray?

Yes. Some small or undisplaced fractures are not visible on the first radiograph.

2. Does walking rule out a hip fracture?

No. Walking or taking a few steps is not a reliable way to exclude it.

3. What if hip pain persists after a fall?

Seek medical reassessment promptly, especially if standing or weight-bearing is painful.

4. What can clarify a hidden fracture?

MRI or CT may be appropriate, depending on the body part and clinical findings.

5. What should I ask the specialist?

Do my symptoms fit the X-ray? Do I need another scan, protection, or planned review?

Important patient-education notice

This article is intended to help you understand your symptoms, diagnosis and treatment after you have been assessed and treated by your doctor. Persistent pain or difficulty walking after a fall requires appropriate medical assessment. This article is not for self-diagnosis or self-treatment.
