GETTING DOWN AND BACK UP

Will I Be Able to Kneel, Sit Cross-Legged, or Use a Floor Toilet Again?

What floor activities ask of the knee and how to plan for prayer, family meals and bathroom access.

KNEELINGCROSS-LEGGED SITTINGFLOOR TOILETS

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The question Farida saved for last

Farida’s first question after total knee replacement was when she could walk to the gate. At a later visit she has three questions she has been saving. Can she kneel on her prayer mat? Can she sit cross-legged with her grandchildren? And when she visits relatives whose bathroom has a floor toilet, will she be able to use it? She can already walk much farther than she could before the operation. The answers to her new questions still feel uncertain.

These activities ask different things of the operated leg. Kneeling puts pressure on the front of the knee. Cross-legged sitting needs movement at the hips and knees and a way to get down and stand again. A floor toilet usually requires a deep squat and a steady rise, often in a small space. Tell the rehabilitation team which position matters to you and describe where you need to use it. [3][4][5][6]

02

Why can kneeling feel uncomfortable?

The skin over the scar or near it may feel sensitive or numb after surgery. Pressure on the front of the knee can feel strange even when walking feels comfortable. The surface matters too. Kneeling briefly on a soft pad can feel very different from kneeling on tile, and a few seconds can be easier than the time needed for prayer or work. Studies have found that kneeling remains difficult for many people after replacement. [1][2][3]

After the wound has healed and your team says kneeling is suitable, the pressure of kneeling itself generally does not harm a standard replacement. The date at which you can try it depends on your operation, healing and the advice you received. Some services advise waiting several months. Ask directly if you have had additional surgery or have been given specific instructions about loading the knee. [1][2][3]

Farida tells the physiotherapist she needs to be able to stay in position, shift her weight and get back up. That information matters more to the plan than a simple answer about whether she can put one knee down for a moment. [3][5]

03

What would a supervised kneeling trial look like?

Ask the physiotherapist to show you a setup appropriate for your stage of recovery. They may suggest a clean padded surface and a stable support within easy reach. You can decide together how much weight to put through the knee and how to reverse the movement. If the front of the knee is numb, ask how to protect the skin during the trial. [1][2][3][5]

Farida practises with the therapist present. They talk through where her hands will go, which leg will do most of the work and how she can stop if she feels unsteady. She tells the therapist that she has a thin prayer mat at home and usually prays where there is no rail. They discuss using a chair when that setup would be hard to manage. She can also discuss any questions about adapting her position with a faith adviser she trusts. [2][3][5]

If you work at floor level, describe the job too. A gardener who needs to move along a bed and someone who kneels briefly to reach a shelf have different needs for support. [3][5]

04

What about sitting cross-legged?

To sit cross-legged, your hips need room to turn outward and your knees need enough bend for the position. One side may feel easier than the other. A person can have a useful amount of knee bend for chairs and stairs and still struggle to bring both legs into a cross-legged position. In one observational study, people reported more difficulty with this position than with ordinary walking even a year after knee replacement. [4][5]

Ask the physiotherapist to assess how you get into and out of the position, as well as how the knee feels while you sit. Tell them about trouble in the other knee, hips or back. If the therapist clears you to practise, they can help you try an appropriate setup, such as a firm raised surface that reduces the amount of lowering you need. Pulling on the ankle to force the leg into place can make the movement harder to control. Keep the trial within the limits the therapist gives you. [4][5]

Farida wants to play a board game on the floor. For now she brings a low table closer to a firm chair so she can reach the game. She keeps floor transfer practice for her therapy sessions. [5][6]

05

Why is a floor toilet especially demanding?

Using a floor toilet involves lowering into a deep squat, staying balanced and rising again. It also takes enough room to place your feet and reach any support. Clothing, a wet floor and a narrow doorway can make that harder. Doing the movement once in a clinic tells the team something about your ability. They also need to know whether the actual bathroom has enough space and a firm support in the place you need it. [4][5][6][7]

Tell the team before discharge if this is your main toilet. Mention the height and type of toilet, the route to it, whether there are steps and whether any rails are fixed to the wall. A photo or simple measurements can help an occupational therapist understand the space. If the team recommends equipment, ask who will fit it and how you should practise using it before you rely on it. [6][7]

Think about whether someone could assist without blocking your exit, and tell the therapist if there is a doorstep or a slippery patch on the way in. Addressing one of those obstacles may make more difference to safe access than practising a deeper squat. [5][6][7]

06

What can I use while I’m recovering?

If you have access to a seated toilet, the team may suggest a seat raiser or toilet frame when a low seat is difficult. If the only toilet at home is at floor level, ask whether a stable commode is suitable for your space and circumstances. A seat raiser for a seated toilet and a freestanding commode solve different access problems. The occupational therapist can talk you through transfers and check that there is room for you, your walking aid and anyone helping. [2][6][7]

Farida and her family plan ahead for the visit. They ask whether there is a seated toilet on the same level before deciding how long to stay. At home she practises sitting and standing from the height her therapist recommended. As she gets stronger, she asks the therapist to reassess how much support she needs. [5][6][7]

Ask how the arrangement will work at night, when you may be tired or the route is darker. If you are using a walking aid, find out where it can be placed so it stays within reach during the transfer. [5][6][7]

07

What should I practise first?

Describe one real activity to your physiotherapist. Include the height, how long you need to hold the position, what you would normally hold on to and how often you do it. They may start by checking your current bend, thigh strength and balance. They can then suggest a smaller part of the task to rehearse safely, such as rising from the seat height you use at home or moving towards a padded surface with supervision. [3][5][6]

Tell the therapist how your knee responds afterwards. A position you can reach briefly may be tiring or uncomfortable when you hold it longer. Ask when to increase the time or depth of practice and what symptoms mean you should stop and call. If a task stays out of reach, a change to the setup may still let you take part comfortably. [3][5][6]

Farida chooses to work first on getting down and up with a steady support. She agrees with her therapist to review kneeling again once she can manage the movements in the plan with confidence. [3][5]

08

What should I take to the next appointment?

Farida brings a photo of the bathroom at her relatives’ house and explains which positions she uses for prayer. She describes how long she can sit comfortably and whether she needs another person nearby when standing up. She and the therapist agree on what she can practise, when to review and what setup she can use in the meantime. [3][5][6]

You can make the same plan with three questions. What can I try now? What equipment or help should I use? What should I be able to do before attempting the full activity? If your knee suddenly becomes more painful or swollen, or you fall and cannot bear weight, get urgent advice. [5][6][8]

The fridge-door cheat sheet

What should I ask before kneeling?

Ask when your wound and knee are ready, what support to use and how to get back up. A soft pad can make pressure more comfortable once you’re cleared. [1][2][3]

Can I sit cross-legged if I walk well?

Maybe. It also takes knee bend, hip movement and a way to get down and up. Ask the physiotherapist to assess the whole activity. [4][5]

What if my only toilet is at floor level?

Tell the team early. Ask whether a stable commode or another accessible toilet is suitable and how to use the available space safely. [6][7]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Ask your surgical team when floor practice and kneeling are suitable for you. Seek urgent help if a fall leaves you unable to bear weight, or if you develop increasing knee redness or swelling, wound drainage, fever or new calf pain or swelling. Call your local emergency number for sudden breathlessness or chest pain. [1][6][8]
