LIVING WITH YOUR NEW KNEE

How Long Will My New Knee Last, and What Happens If It Wears Out?

How to read the numbers, when to get the knee checked and what a second operation may involve.

IMPLANT SURVIVALWEAR AND LOOSENINGREVISION SURGERY

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The date Anand circled

Anand has had his total knee replacement for twelve years. On his morning walk a friend says, “Those knees last fifteen years, don’t they?” Anand goes home and finds the date of his operation. His knee still lets him walk to the shops and climb the stairs at home. He wonders whether he should start planning another operation anyway.

A knee replacement has no fixed expiry date. Many total replacements stay in place for decades. The figures you hear usually come from studies that follow a group of people and count how often another operation is needed to revise the replacement. They can help you put your own experience in context. Your current symptoms, the condition of the knee and your surgeon’s assessment guide what happens next. [1][2][3]

Anand writes two questions before his next appointment. What does “last” mean in the published figures? And if his knee does develop a problem, what changes should he call about? He leaves space beside each question to jot down what applies to his own knee. [1][3][4]

ZappyIf I feel well, should I plan another operation because I’m getting closer to fifteen years?
Prof. BoltKeep following the review plan your team gave you. The date of surgery is one detail we use, along with how the knee works and whether anything has changed. [3][4]
02

What does “82% at 25 years” mean?

A 2019 review combined long-term data from joint replacement registries. It estimated that about 82% of total knee replacements had gone 25 years without revision. Think of that as an estimate of about 82 in 100 at that point. Revision involves an operation to remove, exchange or add one or more components. A knee can still be counted as surviving even if the person has pain or stiffness. [1][2][3]

That estimate comes from earlier generations of implants and from people treated in the settings represented by those registries. A brand introduced recently needs time to accumulate equally long follow-up. Registries also report outcomes for different groups and designs. If you ask about a specific implant, find out which patients and operations the figure describes, how long they were followed, and what counted as a revision. [1][2]

When you compare figures, check that both refer to the same type of replacement and the same number of years. A ten-year result for a total replacement answers a different question from a twenty-five-year result for a partial replacement. Talk through which information applies to the operation you had or are considering. [1][2]

03

What can happen to the parts over time?

In a total knee replacement, metal components cover the ends of the thighbone and shinbone. A tough plastic insert sits between them. That insert can wear gradually. Tiny particles from wear can sometimes trigger a response in the tissues around the implant, causing bone to be lost. A component can also become loose. Wear, bone loss and loosening are related possibilities the surgeon looks for when reviewing the knee. [3][5]

There are other reasons for revision, including infection, a fracture near the implant or instability, when the knee feels unreliable during movement. These problems can occur at different times. When someone says their replacement “wore out,” ask what the surgeon actually found. A change in the insert, a loose component and an infection may each require a different plan. [2][3]

ZappyIf my knee hurts after a long walk, does that tell me the plastic is worn?
Prof. BoltPain has several possible causes. Tell your team if it keeps coming back, gets worse or changes the way you walk. They can decide whether the knee needs an examination or tests. [3][4][5]
04

What will the team check if the knee changes?

Tell the clinician when the change began, whether it followed a fall, what brings it on and how it affects daily life. Mention swelling, repeated giving way or a new limp. The team may watch you walk, check the knee’s movement and stability, and look for tenderness or swelling. Bring your implant card or operation details if you have them, along with any previous images you can readily access. [3][4][5]

X-rays are usually the first imaging test for a painful replacement. Comparing them with older films can help show whether the components or the surrounding bone have changed. Further imaging may be useful for particular questions. If infection is suspected, the team may take blood tests or collect fluid from the knee with a needle. The choice of tests depends on what the examination and earlier results suggest. [3][5]

Ask the team to show you what they found and explain how it relates to your symptoms. If they recommend monitoring a change, ask when it should be checked again and what would bring that visit forward. If they recommend surgery, ask which problem it would address and how urgent the decision is. [3][4][5]

THE DEEP DIVEImplant lifespan, wear and revision surgery
05

Does revision mean replacing everything?

Revision surgery can involve changing one part or removing and replacing several components. A surgeon may consider exchanging the plastic insert in selected cases if the other components are suitable to keep. A loose component, substantial bone loss or an infection can call for a more extensive procedure. The surgeon may need to rebuild or support areas where bone has been lost. [3]

Revision parts sometimes have longer stems that extend into the bone for support, or extra metal pieces that fill gaps. The surgeon also considers how well the ligaments support the knee. The operation can take longer and be more complex than the first replacement because the old parts must be assessed or removed and the bone and soft tissues may have changed. [3]

ZappyWhy might my surgeon give me more than one possible plan?
Prof. BoltThe tests help us prepare. What we find about how firmly parts are fixed, or how much sound bone is available during surgery, can affect which parts we use. [3]
06

What if infection is the reason for another operation?

The team first assesses whether infection is present and what treatment is likely to help. Depending on the circumstances, surgery may involve cleaning the joint and keeping well-fixed components, sometimes with a new insert. Another approach involves removing the components. Antibiotics are usually part of treatment. The timing of the infection, test results, the condition of the knee and your general health all help shape that decision. [3][5]

If the plan involves two operations, the team may put a temporary spacer in after removing the components. The spacer can contain antibiotics. The team then treats the infection and decides when it is appropriate to fit a new replacement. Ask what movement and weight bearing will be allowed between operations and what help you may need during that period. [3]

Fever or chills with a hot, swollen knee, new wound drainage or rapidly worsening symptoms need urgent medical assessment. Tell the clinician you have a knee replacement and when it was put in, even if the operation was years ago. [3][6]

07

What should I plan for after revision?

Ask what improvement is realistic for the problem being treated. Relief of pain, a steadier knee or getting back to everyday activities may be among the goals. The operation and recovery depend on which components need replacing, the condition of the bone and soft tissues, and your overall health. A previous experience of knee replacement gives you a point of reference, and your team can tell you what may be different this time. [3]

Before going home, ask how much weight you can put on the leg, which walking aid or brace to use if one is prescribed and when physiotherapy should begin. If you have steps at home or need help getting to appointments, mention that before discharge. Make sure you know whom to call about pain that is getting worse, wound drainage, fever or new calf symptoms. Sudden breathlessness or chest pain needs emergency help. [3][6]

Anand writes down the tasks he would want a future operation to help with. His list starts with walking to the shops and managing the stairs at home. If he ever needs a revision consultation, he can use that list to ask how the proposed treatment might affect the things he does every day. [3]

Ask for an estimate of when you might return to the activities you rely on, such as driving or caring for someone, and who will review your progress before you resume them. [3]

08

What can I do while my knee is working well?

Keep the follow-up plan your team gave you, and ask what to do if you no longer know how to get an appointment. Keep the implant details somewhere easy to find. Continue the activities that suit your knee and your health. Walking, cycling or swimming may be options depending on your circumstances. If you want to take up an activity that puts more demand on your knee, discuss it with your team. [3][4][8]

Anand still goes for his morning walk. He keeps the operation date alongside the surgeon’s contact details and knows to ask for a review if his knee starts to hurt more, gives way repeatedly or becomes harder to use. He’ll bring the implant details and explain how those changes affect his day. [3][4]

ZappyWhat’s the next step if my knee starts working differently?
Prof. BoltTell your usual clinician or surgical team what changed and when. Seek urgent help for a sudden severe change, a fever with a hot swollen knee, or inability to put weight on the leg. [4][6][7]

The fridge-door cheat sheet

Does my knee have an expiry date?

No. About 82% of total knee replacements in pooled registry data were still in place without revision at 25 years. Your own outlook depends on your circumstances and implant. [1][2]

What changes should I report?

Ask for an orthopaedic review for new or worsening pain, a limp, repeated giving way or loss of function. [3][4]

Will the whole replacement have to come out?

Revision may replace one part or several. Your surgeon will explain what needs changing and how the cause affects the plan. [3]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Arrange a review for new or worsening pain, a limp or loss of function. Seek urgent medical help for sudden severe pain, inability to bear weight, a hot swollen knee with fever or chills, wound drainage or new calf pain or swelling. Call your local emergency number for sudden breathlessness or chest pain. [4][6][7]
