Why Did My Neck Hurt the Day After a Minor Car Crash?

You felt fine after a rear-end bump. By breakfast, your neck was stiff. Follow what happens at the first assessment and how clinicians decide whether you need a scan.

Why pain can start laterWhen a scan helpsWhen to get help

ABOUT 7 MINUTES

01

The morning after the bump

Imagine you’re sitting in the passenger seat at a red light when another car bumps yours from behind. You feel shaken, but you can turn your head easily. The next morning, you reach for your cup and feel a pull across your neck. A glance towards the kitchen door feels stiffer than it did yesterday.

The short answer. Neck pain and stiffness can take several hours to appear after a sudden jolt. That timing can fit whiplash, a neck injury caused by sudden movement of the head. Delayed pain is one clue. The way the crash happened and any other symptoms still matter when deciding what needs assessment. [1] [2]

Our passenger and the crash are imagined. We’ll follow the same sore turn from the kitchen to the first clinical assessment.

ZappyDid something happen overnight?

Prof. BoltThe jolt happened yesterday. The pain can become noticeable hours later.

02

What happened while you were sitting still?

You were sitting still just before the bump. Then the car and seat moved suddenly. Your head and upper body could move relative to each other in the jolt. That sudden movement can strain the neck. You can get a whiplash injury without hitting your head on anything. [1] [3]

Later, you might notice stiffness, pain across the shoulders or a headache. In our story, the first thing you notice is a tight turn when someone speaks from beside you. The exact timing and location of the ache give the clinician something to ask about, alongside what you remember of the crash. [1] [3]

It’s tempting to reconstruct the whole movement from the sound of the impact or the mark on the bumper. Start with what you know. Which direction did the hit come from? Was your car stopped? Did your head strike anything? When did the pain first appear? Those details are more useful at the visit than guessing exactly how far your head moved. [2] [3]

03

Why yesterday felt different

You could turn your head at the roadside. Now you have to shift in your chair to see who’s at the door. Both observations can be true. Some people develop whiplash symptoms hours after the injury. The passage of several hours does not, by itself, tell a clinician which tissues were injured or how seriously. [1] [2]

If the clinic asks when the pain began, give the timeline in your own words. “I was fine when I got out of the car. I felt a little sore before bed. Turning was harder at breakfast.” If those times are approximate, say so. The order matters more than remembering the exact minute.

Also say whether anything came with the stiffness. A local neck ache is one story. Tingling in the hand, weakness or trouble walking changes the assessment. That’s why the clinician asks about your arms and legs even when all you wanted to discuss was your neck. [1] [2]

ZappySo feeling fine at the scene settles it?

Prof. BoltIt tells us how you felt then. We also need to know what you feel now.

04

Why the clinician asks about the crash

At the visit, you say it was “just a bump.” The clinician asks you to describe it. A simple rear-end bump and a higher-speed impact or rollover carry different implications for imaging. The details help the clinician decide whether an injury to the bones or spinal cord needs to be considered. They’ll also ask about head impact, where your neck hurts and any changes in feeling or strength. [2] [3]

The examination can include checking for tenderness directly over the bones in the middle of the neck and checking the nerves. Whether it is safe to ask you to turn your head depends on what the clinician has already learned. Give them the story. Leave the movement assessment to them. [2] [3]

You can bring a short account on your phone if it helps. Include where you sat, how the cars collided, when you noticed symptoms and how those symptoms have changed. Mention any tingling that happened at the scene even if it has since settled. [2]

THE DEEP DIVEHow the assessment guides the next step
05

Who needs a scan?

The Canadian C-spine Rule helps clinicians decide when imaging is needed after certain injuries in alert, stable patients. Researchers developed it using information from 8,924 adults assessed for blunt head or neck trauma at ten Canadian emergency departments. They looked for findings that could identify clinically important injuries to the neck portion of the spine. [4]

NICE guidance uses the rule in suspected cervical spine injury. Age 65 or older, a dangerous mechanism or pins and needles in the limbs are high-risk features. A simple rear-end collision or delayed pain can be low-risk features in the right setting. The clinician considers these only as part of the full assessment. If imaging is indicated in an adult with suspected cervical spine injury, NICE recommends CT. [2]

Think of our passenger. “It hurt the next day” is a piece of the decision. It does not settle whether a scan is needed. The rule was designed for a trained clinician who can check the other findings safely. [2] [4]

06

What recovery looks like at home

Suppose you’ve been assessed and advised that you have uncomplicated whiplash. The next morning you can make breakfast but still feel stiff when you look sideways. Many people improve over the following weeks, although recovery can take longer. NHS guidance encourages a return to ordinary activities as tolerated and advises against routinely wearing a collar for uncomplicated whiplash. Follow the instructions you were given if a clinician fitted a collar for a suspected injury. [1] [3]

Start with the tasks you can manage comfortably. That might be a short walk or a meal at home. Ask a pharmacist or clinician if you need help choosing pain relief. If the pain is worsening or has not begun to improve after a week, seek medical advice. [1]

The passenger in our story wants to know whether tomorrow will be better. It may be easier to track one familiar task, like turning to talk at breakfast, and notice how it changes over several days.

07

Can I drive while my neck is stiff?

You were the passenger yesterday. Today, you’re thinking about taking the car yourself. At the gate, you realise that checking over your shoulder would be difficult. Even if you feel quite well sitting still, driving asks for quick, reliable movements when something unexpected happens. NHS advice is to avoid driving or cycling when restricted neck movement makes it unsafe. [5]

Arrange a lift or another way to travel until you can check traffic safely. If you have new tingling, weakness or other concerning symptoms following the crash, get assessed before driving. [1] [5]

At a follow-up, mention the tasks you still avoid. “I can look at the person next to me, but I can’t check behind the car comfortably” helps describe where you are in recovery.

ZappyI feel fine facing straight ahead. Does that count?

Prof. BoltIt tells you how you feel while facing straight ahead. Driving also needs a safe look to the side and behind.

08

The symptoms that change the plan

Imagine a different breakfast. The pain is severe, you feel pins and needles in an arm, or an electric-shock sensation shoots down your back when you move. Those symptoms after a neck injury need urgent medical assessment. [1] [2]

Get urgent medical help after a crash if you have severe neck pain, pins and needles, or a shock-like sensation down the spine or limbs. Get emergency help for new weakness, difficulty walking, or severe neck pain after a serious crash. Have someone else arrange transport or call for an ambulance if needed. [1] [2] [6]

Back in the original kitchen, you can now describe the jolt, when the stiffness began and what you noticed alongside it. Those details tell a clinician much more than the size of the dent.

ZappyWhat should I say first when I call?

Prof. BoltSay you were in a crash, when the pain started, and whether you’ve noticed any other symptoms.

Prof. Bolt’s verdict

Neck pain can start hours after a crash. The timing, how the crash happened and what else you feel all help determine what needs checking. [1] [2]

If you seek advice, start with three details: how the collision happened, when the pain began, and whether you noticed tingling, weakness or trouble walking.

Fridge-door cheat sheet

  • Whiplash symptoms can take hours to appear. [1]
  • A clinician uses the crash details and examination to decide whether you need imaging. [2]
  • Follow your care plan after assessment and avoid driving if you can’t check traffic safely. [1] [5]
  • New weakness or trouble walking after a crash needs emergency help. [2] [6]
