My X-ray Says “Cervical Spondylosis.” What Does That Mean?

Bring one neck X-ray report to the clinic. Learn what the words describe, why the examination matters, and what symptoms need attention.

Read your X-rayAge-related changesWhen to get help

ABOUT 7 MINUTES

01

Two words on the report

You open your X-ray report while the kettle boils. The last line says “Cervical spondylosis.” Your neck has been stiff when you turn to talk to someone beside you. Now you wonder if the report has just explained the stiffness, and whether it means your neck is going to get worse.

The short answer. Cervical means the neck portion of the spine. Spondylosis describes age-related changes in the discs and joints there. These changes are common and can be present in people who feel perfectly well. They can contribute to pain or stiffness in some people. The words on the report are one part of working out what’s happening in your case. [1] [2]

This is an imagined consultation. Bring the same report with you as we follow the clinician’s questions, from the spacing between two bones to how your neck moves when you look to the side.

ZappyIs that long word an explanation for my stiff neck?

Prof. BoltIt describes what’s visible on the X-ray. Your symptoms and examination help put it in context.

02

What can an X-ray actually see?

The clinician points to two small white blocks on the side-view image. They’re vertebrae, the bones that stack up to form the spine. Between them sits a disc. On an ordinary X-ray, the space between the bones is much easier to see than the disc itself. When the report says “disc-space narrowing,” it means that gap looks smaller. [2] [3]

The report might also mention osteophytes, or bone spurs. These are extra bone at the margins, where the spine has changed over time. Think of the side view as a silhouette. The reader can see the outline of the bone and the spacing between bones. An X-ray gives much less detail about the nerves and spinal cord than an MRI. [2] [3]

You can ask the clinician to show you where the finding is, especially if the report mentions a level such as C5–C6. That’s the space between the fifth and sixth neck vertebrae. Now you know which part of the picture the report is talking about.

03

Why the same picture can go with different symptoms

You turn your head in the clinic. It feels tight towards the end of the movement, but the ache stays near the neck. That’s useful information. A neighbour could have similar changes on an image and be able to turn comfortably. And someone else with neck pain may describe a sharp feeling running into the hand. [1] [2]

The report and the history are answering two different questions. The report describes the structures that were visible. The history tells the clinician what happens when you turn, how long it has been going on and how much it affects what you do. The examination adds what the clinician can observe, such as restricted movement or a change in strength or sensation. [1] [2]

Think of what you’d like to do more comfortably. In our story it’s a simple thing: look to the side without turning the whole chair. Mention that at the visit. It gives you and the clinician a practical way to talk about progress.

ZappyCan my neck feel better even if the X-ray would look the same?

Prof. BoltYes. How comfortably you can turn is something you can track alongside what the report says.

04

Why the clinician asks about your hands

You came because your neck was stiff. Then the clinician asks whether you’ve noticed tingling, dropped objects or had trouble with buttons. Those questions help work out whether the story extends beyond local neck pain. [1] [2]

A nerve root is where a nerve connects near the spine. Irritation there can cause symptoms down an arm, such as pain, pins and needles, numbness or weakness. The clinician may check sensation, strength and reflexes if the history suggests it. [1] [2]

The spinal cord carries signals between the brain and the body. When the cord in the neck is affected, someone may develop clumsy hands, unsteadiness or difficulty walking. A change in those abilities matters even when the neck itself feels only mildly sore. If you’ve noticed it, say when it began and how quickly it has changed. [1] [5]

05

What researchers saw in people who felt well

In 1998, researchers looked at neck MRIs from 497 people without symptoms. They examined five disc levels per person and found that changes such as disc degeneration were more common at older ages. It helps explain why a scan can contain unfamiliar words even when the person has no neck complaint. [4]

The team used MRI, which shows more detail about discs than an X-ray. They were counting imaging findings in people without symptoms. The study cannot tell you whether a particular finding is causing your stiffness. That takes the rest of the clinical picture. [2] [4]

Back in the clinic, you ask the question that matters for your report. “Does what you see here fit the movement that hurts and the rest of my examination?” That brings the conversation back to why you came in.

ZappySo a more detailed scan can give me more words to worry about?

Prof. BoltIt can give more detail. That detail helps when it answers a question raised by your symptoms or examination.

06

When would an MRI add something?

In our story, the clinician hears about stiffness on turning and finds no concerning changes in strength or sensation. The next step can be based on the history and examination. A report containing the word spondylosis alone gives no reason to assume you need an MRI. [2] [6]

Now imagine a different visit. The ache runs into the arm and has persisted, or an examination suggests a nerve or the spinal cord may be affected. An MRI may be useful because it can show those structures in more detail. How urgently you need assessment depends on what has changed and how quickly. [1] [2] [6]

The American College of Radiology’s guidance treats these presentations differently. It generally rates MRI as unsuitable as the first test for new neck pain without trauma, warning signs or signs of nerve-root involvement. For chronic neck pain with nerve-root symptoms and no warning signs, it generally rates MRI without contrast as appropriate. [6]

07

Plan around what you want to do again

At the end of the visit, you ask what you can do about the stiffness. For straightforward neck pain linked to spondylosis, care often starts with everyday movement within comfort. A clinician can discuss physiotherapy or other ways to manage symptoms if they keep limiting you. If you’re considering pain relief, ask a pharmacist or clinician what suits your health conditions and medicines. [1] [2] [5]

Pick something you’d like to be easier. Perhaps it’s looking towards someone at the dining table, finishing an hour at your desk or turning comfortably during a walk. Notice how often you have to stop or change position and whether that improves over time.

Arrange a review if pain persists, worsens or keeps interfering with ordinary tasks. Mention any new pain travelling down the arm, pins and needles, numbness or weakness rather than waiting for another X-ray to tell you what changed. [1] [5]

08

The symptoms that change how soon you need help

The report you opened over breakfast gives you a name for changes in your neck. Now picture a different morning. You struggle to button a shirt that was easy yesterday. Or you find yourself holding the wall to walk to the kitchen. Those changes deserve immediate attention. [1] [5]

Get urgent medical assessment for new or worsening limb weakness or gradually increasing hand clumsiness. Get emergency help for new trouble walking, sudden loss of coordination, or new loss of bladder or bowel control. [1] [5]

In the original scene, you came in because it hurt to look to the side. You leave knowing what the phrase describes, what the clinician checked and which changes you’d mention straight away.

ZappyNext time I see those two words, what’s my first question?

Prof. Bolt“Which of these findings fits what I feel and what I can do?”

Prof. Bolt’s verdict

Cervical spondylosis describes common age-related changes. To make sense of your report, ask how the findings fit your symptoms, the examination and what you want to do more comfortably. [1] [2]

Bring the report to your visit and name one movement or task that has become harder.

Fridge-door cheat sheet

  • Spondylosis means age-related changes in the spine. [1]
  • A narrower gap or a bone spur is an X-ray finding. [2] [3]
  • Tell your clinician what sets off your symptoms and whether they reach your arm. [1] [2]
  • New trouble walking, sudden loss of coordination or loss of bladder or bowel control needs emergency help. [5]
