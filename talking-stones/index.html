The Stone
They Prayed To
Was a Land Deed

Across Karnataka, old carved stones sit in shrines, fields and even graveyards. Some are gods. Many are records of kings, battles and lakes. Some even gave your locality its name. Here’s how to read them.

ABOUT 7 MINUTES

01

The murti with a secret

In Dibbur, a village near Hesaraghatta on Bengaluru’s northern edge, people worshipped a carved stone under a small shrine for generations. Nobody knew its lower half was covered in writing, because that half was buried in the ground. [1]

Then came a temple renovation. Villagers moved the slab under a peepal tree, the buried part came up, and a researcher doing a field survey noticed the letters. Epigraphists read it and dated it to 1750, in the reign of the Mysore king Immadi Krishnaraja Wadiyar. [1]

The “god” turned out to be a public notice. It records that the king gave Dibbur’s taxes and revenue to the Ranganatha temple at Srirangapatna, to pay for its festivals and daily worship. [1] A village about 140 km from Srirangapatna was quietly funding its temple, and the receipt was set in stone.

ZappySo all this time they were doing pooja to a tax document?

Prof. BoltIt was a temple donation. Honestly, it deserved a little respect.

02

How to read a stone without reading a word

You don’t need to know old Kannada to get the gist of most stones. The pictures are a code.

A sun and a moon at the top means “forever.” A 1431 grant stone in Kodigehalli, Bengaluru, carries both, and its closing lines ask that the gift stand candrarka-sthayi: as long as the moon and sun endure. [5]

On the Dibbur stone, the sun and moon sit above a Kirthimukha, the fierce lion-like face the Mysore Wadiyars used as an emblem, along with a conch, a discus and the Vaishnava namam. [1] Put together: a royal gift, to a Vishnu temple, forever. You’ve read the headline before a single letter.

A veeragallu (hero stone) works like a comic strip, read from the bottom up. The lowest panel shows how the hero died: a battle, a cattle raid, defending the village. In the middle, celestial women carry him to heaven. At the top, he sits before his god. [6] Karnataka has the largest collection in India, about 2,650 of them, the earliest from the 5th century. [7]

Sun and moon: a gift meant to last forever. Three panels: a hero’s death, read from the bottom up.

ZappySo it’s a comic. With a sad ending.

Prof. BoltA happy one, actually. Read the top panel again. He made it to heaven.

03

The 1,500-year-old proof that Kannada was here

In 1936, M.H. Krishna, the director of archaeology of the old Mysore state, came across a sandstone pillar in Halmidi, a small village near Belur in Hassan district. [8] On it were sixteen lines of text in a very early form of Kannada.

It is dated to about 450 CE, and it is the oldest known full-length inscription in Kannada. [9] Kannada words appear even earlier, for example in Ashoka’s edict at Brahmagiri. But Halmidi is the first time a whole public record was written in the language, which shows Kannada was already being used to run things 1,500 years ago. [9]

Scholars still argue over the exact date, with estimates ranging from 450 to about 500 CE. [8] The original is kept safely by the state; the village has a fibreglass replica in its own small mantapa. [9]

ZappySo the first long thing ever written in Kannada was… a government record?

Prof. BoltOf course. Poets come and go. Paperwork is forever.

04

The curse at the bottom of the deed

Land grants weren’t just carved prayers. They were legal documents, and Indian law books listed what a proper one had to contain: who gave it, what was given and to whom, that the gift was permanent, the date, the official in charge, and a set of blessing and curse verses. [10]

The curses are the fun part. A grant from Krishnadevaraya’s time, published in Epigraphia Indica, warns that anyone who takes back land given by himself or by another will be “born as a worm in ordure for sixty thousand years.” [11]

The same record has a smarter line for future rulers: protecting someone else’s gift earns twice the merit of making your own, and confiscating it makes your own gifts worthless. [11] In other words: the next king, don’t you dare.

Without courts or land registers, a stone in the middle of the village worked as both. Everyone could see it, and nobody wanted to be the worm.

ZappySixty thousand years as a worm. That’s a serious penalty clause.

Prof. BoltAnd no appeal. Our land courts could learn something.

05

A stone that settled a 900-year-old argument

Historians treat these stones as evidence, and sometimes one stone changes the textbook.

This year, residents of Neeragunda village in Arsikere taluk, Hassan district, dug up a 12th-century hero stone while restoring their temple. Its Kannada text dates the death of the great Hoysala king Vishnuvardhana to 11 December 1141, settling a question scholars had argued over for a long time. [12]

Bengaluru has its own. The Begur hero stone, from about 890–900 CE in the Ganga period, shows a battle at a place called Bengaluru in its lowest panel. It now stands in the Bengaluru museum. [13] The city’s oldest dated stone, the Hebbal–Kittayya hero stone of about 750 CE, was so worn that it was fully read only in 2019, from a 3D digital model. It names the attackers as the Rashtrakutas. [2]

Three stones, three corrections to the history books
~750 CE · Hebbal · who attacked→~890–900 CE · Begur · a battle at Bengaluru→1141 CE · Neeragunda · when a king died
06

Your locality’s name may be carved on a stone

Take Kodigehalli in north Bengaluru. Kodige means a grant and halli means village. A Kannada inscription of 1431 records a grant there, made in Devaraya’s reign during a solar eclipse, an auspicious time for giving. Over the centuries the “grant village” name replaced its original one, Virupakshapura. [14]

That stone lay on a roadside in poor condition until it was moved to a safe spot around 2018. [14] India Post has since honoured it with a special permanent postmark. [5] It also explains a common suffix: in the Vijayanagara period, ‑samudra (“ocean”) was used, a little grandly, for big lakes and reservoirs. [15]

Closer to Mysuru, a stone at Kaliyuru in T. Narsipur taluk records that the village was once called Krishnarayapura, in Krishnadevaraya’s reign, about 500 years ago. It lay damaged and neglected until villagers, panchayat officials and a local history enthusiast had it put back up near the Kolalu Gopalaswamy temple in December 2025. [16]

ZappySo somewhere there’s a stone that explains my pin code?

Prof. BoltMaybe. And it’s been waiting 500 years for someone to ask.

07

Where did 100 stones go?

Bengaluru city once had about 175 recorded stone inscriptions, dating from 750 CE to 1700 CE. Only 60–70 can be traced today. Over 100 have been lost. [2]

Much of what we know about them comes from one stubborn man. B. Lewis Rice, the first director of the Mysore archaeology department, toured the old state until 1906. [4] His Epigraphia Carnatica volumes published 8,869 inscriptions from old Mysore and Coorg. [3] For many stones, his printed copy is now all that survives.

The fix being tried today is to scan, not dig. A handheld scanner projects light patterns onto the stone and cameras record the reflections. Software then rebuilds the surface in 3D, which can bring out letters the eye can’t see. It’s slow work, about 4 days for every 3 stones, and Bengaluru district alone has about 1,200 inscriptions. [2]

These stones still matter outside history books. An 1043 CE inscription in Kadugodi, which records the building of Pattandur lake, has been submitted as evidence in an encroachment dispute. It currently stands in a graveyard. [2]

ZappyA thousand-year-old stone giving evidence in court?

Prof. BoltBest witness you’ll find. It was there when the lake was dug.

08

The stone at the end of your street

Many of the recent finds didn’t start with experts. They turned up during temple renovations, as at Dibbur and Neeragunda, or were spotted by villagers and local enthusiasts, as at Kaliyuru. [1] [12] [16] The next one could be in your neighbourhood.

Look near old temple entrances and village lakes, and for slabs with a sun and moon on top or stacked carved panels. If you find one, the most useful thing is to photograph it and tell the state archaeology department or a local history group. Fresh paint and cement are the enemy: one 1669 inscription in Malleshwaram was damaged by splashes from construction work. [17]

Every stone in this story was put up so that people would remember: a king’s gift, a hero’s death, a new lake, a village’s name. For centuries they did their job, quietly, in the open. The only thing they can’t do is ask us to keep reading.

ZappyOkay. Next time I see an old stone with a sun and moon, I’m stopping.

Prof. BoltGood. Photograph it first. It may be telling a story nobody’s read in 500 years.
