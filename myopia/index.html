It isn’t “weak eyes.” The eyeball itself is growing too long, and sunlight looks like the brake.

ABOUT 7 MINUTES

TODAY IS SPECIAL!It’s Children’s Day, Jawaharlal Nehru’s birthday. 🎈 So today’s story is about something every class photo now shows: a lot more spectacles.
01

Count the glasses in a class photo

4% → 21%urban Indian children aged 5–15 with myopia, 1999 vs 2019

Look at an old school photo, then a new one. You are not imagining it. A review of Indian studies found that myopia, or short sight, among urban children aged 5 to 15 rose from 4.4% in 1999 to 21.1% in 2019. In rural areas, the rate barely moved. [1]

That is roughly one child in five in a city classroom, up from one in twenty-two. The same researchers project the urban figure could approach one in two children by 2050 if the trend continues. [2]

India is not alone. A large global analysis estimated that by 2050 nearly 5 billion people, half the world, will be short-sighted. [3] Genes have not changed that fast. Something about how children now grow up has.

City children are becoming short-sighted much faster than village children. That gap is the first clue.
02

It’s not a weak eye. It’s a long eye.

Most people think short sight means the eye is “weak.” In most children it is a shape problem. Normal sight needs the eyeball to be just the right length, so light lands exactly on the retina, the screen at the back of the eye. In the common form of myopia, the eyeball grows too long. Light from far away comes into focus in front of the retina, so the board, the bus number and the cricket ball blur. [4]

Think of an old film projector with the screen pushed a little too far back. The projector is fine. The distance is wrong.

RIGHT LENGTH

Light focuses exactly on the retina. Far and near both look sharp.

TOO LONG

Light focuses before it reaches the retina. Near is sharp; far is blurred.

Here is why it matters. Glasses bend the light so the picture lands on the retina again, but they do not shorten the eye. And once an eye has grown long, it does not shrink back. That is why myopia that starts at 7 is usually still there at 70, and why researchers worry about the eye that keeps growing through school. [2] [3]

Prof. BoltLong, not tall. Only the eyeball grew too much.

03

Why a pair of glasses isn’t the end of it

If glasses fix the blur, why worry? Because the eye that starts growing too long often keeps growing through the school years, and the earlier it starts, the more years it has to grow. Researchers in Taiwan noticed exactly this: as myopia became more common, the age it began kept falling, leaving more time for it to become severe. [8]

Severe, or “high”, myopia is more than a thick lens. A very long eye stretches the thin layers at its back. The global study projected nearly 1 billion people with high myopia by 2050, and linked it to higher risks of retinal detachment, glaucoma, cataract and a form of macular damage, conditions that can cost sight permanently. [3] [4]

The goal is not only “give the child glasses.” It is “start late, and grow slowly.”

ZappySo the number on my glasses isn’t just a number?

Prof. BoltIt’s a ruler. It tells you how far the eye has grown.

04

The 40-minute experiment

If indoor life is part of the problem, can more outdoor time help? Researchers in Guangzhou, China, tested exactly that with about 1,900 first-graders in 12 schools. Six schools added one 40-minute outdoor class at the end of every school day; six carried on as usual. [5]

USUAL SCHOOL DAY

39.5%

became short-sighted within 3 years

+40 MINUTES OUTSIDE

30.4%

became short-sighted within 3 years

One extra period of fresh air, and fewer children developed myopia. That is a modest change from a modest dose, but it came from a randomised trial, which is strong evidence. [5]

Why would sunlight matter? The leading explanation is that bright light makes the retina release dopamine, a chemical that seems to act as a brake on the eyeball’s growth. [6] Outdoor daylight is many times brighter than a classroom, even on a cloudy day. An international review adds a useful detail: hats and sunglasses do not appear to cancel the benefit. [7]

Scientists still say the mechanism is not fully settled. What is settled is the pattern: children who spend more time outdoors are less likely to become short-sighted. [6]

ZappySo playing outside is basically homework for my eyes?

Prof. BoltThe only homework you’ll ever ask for more of.

THE DEEP DIVEA country that sent its children outside, screens on trial, and what doctors can do.
05

The country that sent its children outside

One trial is good evidence. A whole country is a bigger test. Taiwan tracked the eyesight of 1.2 to 1.9 million primary school children every year. From 2001 to 2011, the share with reduced distance vision climbed from about 35% to 50%. [8]

In September 2010, Taiwan launched a programme called Tian-Tian 120, “120 minutes every day”, encouraging schools to take children outdoors for two hours daily. [8]

BEFORE · 2001–2011

35% → 50%

Rising every year

AFTER · 2012–2015

49% → 46%

Falling for the first time

After the programme began, the long upward trend turned downward. [8] One caution: these figures measure reduced vision on a school eye chart, which is closely linked to myopia but not identical, and other changes over the same years may also have played a part.

Why two hours? Earlier research suggested that about 14 hours a week outdoors could keep the risk low even for children with two short-sighted parents, so Taiwan rounded it to two hours a day. [8]

06

Screens on trial, and a famous rule that doesn’t do what we think

Are phones to blame? A major review of 33 studies found that high smartphone and tablet use was linked to roughly 30% higher odds of myopia, and close to 80% when heavy computer use was added. [9]

But the authors were careful: every study measured screen time with questionnaires, not devices, so the link is real but its size is uncertain. [9] A phone may hurt eyes in two ways at once: it is held close for hours, and it keeps a child indoors, away from daylight. A book read indoors for six hours does the first. A cricket match does neither.

The 20-20-20 rule prevents short sight.

The rule (every 20 minutes, look 20 feet away for 20 seconds) was meant for tired, strained eyes, not for myopia. A test of 20-second breaks found no measurable benefit even for eye strain, and eye researchers have pointed out there is no evidence that it prevents myopia. Some suggest a “20-20-2” version instead, with the 2 meaning two hours outdoors a day. [10] [11]

Breaks from close work are not useless; they just need to be longer than 20 seconds, and they don’t replace daylight.

ZappySo I can keep my phone if I play outside too?

Prof. BoltNice try. Two hours outside, and the phone stays in your pocket.

07

If a child already has myopia

Once myopia starts, outdoor time still matters, but it is less clear that it slows an eye that is already growing. [6] That is where eye specialists now have tools aimed at slowing the growth, not just correcting the blur.

The best-studied is low-dose atropine eye drops, used at a tiny fraction of the usual strength. In a Hong Kong trial of children aged 4 to 12, the 0.05% strength slowed myopia the most, about double the effect of 0.01% over two years. [12] There are also specially designed spectacle lenses and contact lenses for myopia control. [13]

ORDINARY GLASSES

Sharpen the picture. Do not aim to slow the eye’s growth.

MYOPIA-CONTROL TREATMENTS

Drops, lenses or contacts chosen and monitored by an eye specialist to slow growth.

None of these is a cure, and they are not over-the-counter fixes. They need a prescription, regular checks, and patience; in the atropine trial, myopia sped up again when treatment stopped. [14]

The simplest step comes before all of them. Eye-health researchers advise a regular eye examination for children, preferably every year, so problems are caught early. [13] A child who squints at the board, sits close to the TV or holds the phone very near may simply not know the world is supposed to look sharper.

08

The cheapest eye medicine is a playground

120minutes of daylight a day: the most-studied prevention, free for everyone

The story of children’s glasses turns out to be a story about indoor childhoods. Tuition after school, homework at the dining table, games on a phone: each is ordinary on its own, and together they keep a growing eye indoors, focused close, for most of the day.

The research points to a surprisingly gentle answer. Daylight, even on a cloudy day, even under a cap, seems to tell the eye to slow down. It doesn’t need a special sport or a gadget. It needs time outside the four walls.

On Children’s Day, that may be the most useful gift of all: a little less homework, a little more sky.

ZappySo the best thing for my eyes is going out to play?

Prof. BoltFinally, a prescription you won’t forget to take.

The evidence file

Checked 10 November 2026, for Children’s Day, 14 November. General information, not medical advice; a child’s eyes should be assessed by an eye-care professional.

  1. Priscilla & Verkicharla: Time trends on the prevalence of myopia in India – a prediction model for 2050 — Ophthalmic and Physiological Optics, 2021. Systematic review: urban children aged 5–15, 4.44% (1999) to 21.15% (2019); rural prevalence changed little. Based on a limited number of urban studies.
  2. IANS report: myopia in every third urban Indian child by 2030 — May 2024, quoting the projection of about 31.9% by 2030 and 48.1% by 2050 for urban children. A trend projection, not a certainty.
  3. UNSW / Brien Holden Vision Institute: Half the world short-sighted by 2050 — February 2016, summarising Holden et al., Ophthalmology 2016: about 4.8 billion myopes projected by 2050.
  4. Brien Holden Foundation: What is myopia? — axial myopia explained as an eyeball that is too long, focusing light in front of the retina.
  5. He et al., JAMA 2015 (Guangzhou Outdoor Activity Trial), as reported by News-Medical — 12 schools, first-graders aged about 6.6; 3-year incidence 30.4% vs 39.5%.
  6. French et al.: Time outdoors and the prevention of myopia — Experimental Eye Research, 2013. Light-stimulated retinal dopamine as the proposed mechanism; evidence has some inconsistencies.
  7. Eye on Optics: The role of light in eye growth — 15 December 2025, reporting the International Myopia Institute white paper; outdoor light above ~1,000 lux, hats and sunglasses do not diminish the benefit.
  8. Wu et al.: Increased time outdoors is followed by reversal of the long-term trend to reduced visual acuity in Taiwan primary school students — Ophthalmology, 2020. 1.2–1.9 million children a year, 2001–2015; Tian-Tian 120 from September 2010; reduced VA 34.8% (2001) to 50.0% (2011), then 49.4% (2012) to 46.1% (2015); notes earlier onset with rising prevalence and the 14-hours-a-week basis for 120 minutes. Observational; measures reduced visual acuity, not myopia directly.
  9. Foreman et al.: Association between digital smart device use and myopia — Lancet Digital Health, December 2021. 33 studies; smart device OR 1.26, with computers OR 1.77; screen time measured by questionnaire in all studies.
  10. Review of Optometry: 20/20/20 still not enough to alleviate eye strain — reporting Johnson & Rosenfield, Optometry and Vision Science 2022/23: no significant effect of 20-second breaks on symptoms.
  11. Gawne & Pucker: letter in Eye — January 2026: no evidence the 20-20-20 rule prevents myopia; the “20-20-2” proposal including 2 hours outdoors daily.
  12. Yam et al.: LAMP study phase 2 report — Ophthalmology, 2020. Randomised trial in Hong Kong; over 2 years, 0.05% atropine had double the efficacy of 0.01%.
  13. AOP: Half of the world’s population could be myopic by 2050 — 17 February 2016, quoting Prof. Kovin Naidoo on yearly eye examinations and on specially designed spectacle lenses, contact lenses and drug interventions.
  14. American Academy of Ophthalmology EyeNet: Low-dose atropine for myopia control — July 2023 journal highlight on LAMP 3-year results; progression faster after treatment was discontinued.
