ABOUT 6 MINUTES · A PATIENT’S EXPLAINER

01

A new surface, not a whole new leg

The name suggests a dramatic exchange: old knee out, new knee in. In a total knee replacement, the surgeon actually removes damaged cartilage and a small amount of underlying bone, then covers the prepared thighbone and shinbone ends with metal components. A medical-grade plastic insert provides a gliding surface between them. [1]

The undersurface of the kneecap may also be resurfaced, depending on the case. Muscles and other living tissues remain important parts of the working joint.

The operation is considered when pain and disability remain substantial despite suitable nonsurgical care. “Robotic” describes help during that operation, not an electronic device left inside the leg.

02

How does a machine find your knee?

A plan on a screen is useful only if it matches the real knee. Surgeons establish that connection through registration: recording anatomical landmarks so the digital model corresponds to the patient.

Many systems use cameras and tracking arrays attached to the bones. These allow the equipment to follow the knee’s position as it moves during surgery. Some platforms use a CT scan obtained beforehand; others collect their mapping information during the operation. [7]

The surgeon chooses the plan and remains responsible for the procedure. Robotic guidance is an operating tool, rather than a replacement for clinical judgement. [2]

03

The hidden challenge: ligament balance

A knee is more than two bone surfaces. Its ligaments help control movement and stability. An implant can occupy its intended position while the surrounding soft tissues still need careful assessment.

During surgery, the team assesses how the knee behaves while bending and straightening. Robotic planning can help the surgeon explore how changes in component position affect the spaces and ligament tension around the joint. [7]

That is why alignment and balance belong in the same conversation. Alignment concerns the orientation of the components and limb; balance concerns how the supporting tissues behave through movement. Research examines their relationship, and the ideal targets remain debated. [8]

Prof. BoltExactly. The joint also has to work when the picture starts moving.

04

Different instruments, the same basic operation

Conventional replacement uses physical guides and surgical instruments to prepare bone. Robot-assisted replacement adds digital planning, tracking and guided tools. The exact tools and imaging requirements vary between systems. [2]

CONVENTIONAL

Physical guidance

The surgeon uses instruments and cutting guides to prepare the joint surfaces.

ROBOT-ASSISTED

Digital assistance

A computer model and tracked tools help the surgeon execute the selected plan.

The implant and the method used to position it are separate parts of the story. Robotic assistance alone does not tell us which implant is being used, whether the incision is smaller, or how the recovery will feel. Both approaches remain operations involving anaesthesia, wound healing and rehabilitation.

05

Why precision and benefit need different tests

Researchers can measure how closely an implant matches a planned position. Patients experience something broader: pain, confidence, walking, stairs and how often they notice the artificial joint.

TECHNICAL RESULT

How accurately was the surgical plan carried out?

PATIENT RESULT

Does daily life feel meaningfully better?

Two randomised trials published in 2026 illustrate why the distinction matters. A single-centre Spanish trial of 68 patients using ROSA reported better early bending and less pain at three months than manual surgery. [3]

The larger British RACER-Knee trial, with 339 patients using Mako, found no clinically meaningful patient benefit at twelve months, with higher costs. Its main outcome assessed how aware patients were of their joint during everyday life. [4]

These studies used different systems, settings and outcome measures. They do not establish one brand as universally better. Early benefits in one study and no meaningful difference later in another are not interchangeable findings.

Nor can a short follow-up establish whether an implant will last longer over decades. That question requires longer observation.

06

Why recovery has its own timetable

Placing the components completes only one stage. Afterwards, the wound and surrounding tissues heal while strength and movement return. Pain relief, walking support and physiotherapy are therefore part of recovery after either approach. [5]

Hospital discharge and full recovery are different milestones. A person may be safe to return home while still having swelling, needing a walking aid and rebuilding confidence. Full recovery can take months, with variation between people.

The operation also retains risks such as infection, blood clots, stiffness or ongoing pain. [6] Tracking pins used by robotic systems can introduce an additional fracture risk at their insertion sites. [2]

In an Indian home, “better movement” may mean stairs, getting into a car, floor sitting or kneeling. Those activities ask different things of a knee. Replacement can improve everyday function without restoring every movement of a young, unaffected joint. [1]

07

What is the extra bill paying for?

The price of the implant is not the price of the whole operation. Robotic assistance may add equipment-related costs and, depending on the platform, an additional scan. AAOS lists increased cost among the potential disadvantages. [2]

A hospital package can also include—or exclude—anaesthesia, room charges, medicines, physiotherapy and follow-up. Comparing two totals without their contents can therefore conceal what actually differs.

No single extra rupee amount fits every Indian hospital, patient or insurance policy. The useful distinction is between the extra resources used and the extra patient benefit demonstrated. A British trial’s cost finding cannot provide an Indian hospital quotation.

08

The most interesting part is still human

The deeper story is a meeting between a planned mechanical reconstruction and a living joint. Digital tools help the surgeon see and execute a plan. Ligaments, muscles, healing and the person’s daily activities determine how that reconstruction is experienced.

That explains why a technically impressive operation and a satisfying recovery need different measures of success. The screen can describe component position; the person can describe what it feels like to walk to the gate or get up from a chair.
