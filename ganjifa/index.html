Ganjifa:
The Mysore King Who
Invented Card Games

A Wadiyar maharaja designed card games of up to 360 hand-painted cards. Then the games were forgotten, until a painter bought a few old cards from the Palace.

A gallery in your hand

ABOUT 7 MINUTES

01

A painting you can play with

Schematic illustration · not a reproduction of a historical deck

A playing card does not have to be a rectangle with a heart in the corner.

Imagine opening a box and finding little painted circles: a goddess on a lion, a king on horseback, a cluster of tiny repeated emblems, all done with a brush finer than an eyelash. Spread across a table, they look like a miniature exhibition.

That is Ganjifa, India’s old hand-painted card game. Many regions made it, but Mysuru has a claim no other place can match: here, a maharaja himself sat down and invented new games, some needing hundreds of cards. [6] [7]

To see why that was remarkable, we first need to know what an ordinary Ganjifa pack looked like.

02

From Babur’s gift to a 96-card pack

Schematic illustration · not a reproduction of a historical deck

Ganjifa has Persian roots and became established in India in the Mughal period. One very concrete record: in 1527, Babur sent Ganjifa cards as a gift. [2]

8 suits × (1 king + 1 minister + 10 number cards)
= 96 cards

The classic Mughal pack had eight suits of twelve cards: a king, a minister and ten number cards shown by repeated emblems. [3] As the game spread, Indian courts and painters filled the suits with their own stories. One popular version used the ten avatars of Vishnu, giving ten suits and usually 120 cards. [4]

That is roughly where things stood when the game reached the Mysore court. What happened next went far beyond 120 cards.

03

The king who designed card games

Mummadi Krishnaraja Wadiyar III (1794–1868) ruled Mysore for most of the nineteenth century and was deeply interested in art, Sanskrit and astrology. [7] [9] Among his projects was a vast illustrated book, the Sritattvanidhi (1818). Its last chapter, the Kautuka Nidhi, records card games devised under his guidance: the number of cards, the pictures, the colour schemes and the verse that goes with each. [6] [8]

Craft records credit him with 13 Ganjifa games; some accounts count as many as 18. [7] [10] They needed anywhere from 36 to 360 cards, depending on how many suits were used. [6] Some used as many as 18 suits, with permanent trumps and wild cards. [8]

Notice what this does. A card game became a way to learn mythology, astrology and the epics while playing. The cards taught stories; the stories taught the cards.

04

Ivory for the palace, paper for the people

The same game could be made very differently depending on who would hold it. Royal Mysore sets were made from ivory and mother of pearl; sets for ordinary players used cloth or paper. [7] In the king’s time, artisans also made stiff card boards by gluing sheets of paper together. [16]

The painting is the Mysore signature. Colours came from natural sources, such as minerals, herbs, flowers and seeds, and the finest details were laid in with squirrel-hair brushes. A coat of lacquer protected the work from the hands of players. [10] [16]

That is why Mysore Ganjifa is often described in the same breath as Mysore painting: the same court, the same fine line, shrunk to the size of a palm.

05

The number can surprise you

Recognise the suit → recall its ranking → follow the cards played

A glimpse of play, not a rulebook.

The Mysore games kept the basic play of the Mughal version, only with far more suits. [6] Players compete to win rounds, or tricks. [4]

Here is the twist. In many Ganjifa packs, the number cards don’t run in the same direction in every suit: in some suits ten beats one, and in others one beats ten. [4] Now add the king’s permanent trumps and wild cards across a dozen or more suits. You cannot just play your biggest number. You have to remember which suit follows which rule, and what has already been played.

The art was not decoration. The picture told you the suit, and the suit told you the rule.

ZappySo the king made a game where one can beat ten?

Prof. BoltA king can rewrite the rules. That is rather the point of being king.

06

How a royal game went silent

By the early twentieth century, demand for the Mysore games had fallen sharply, as Western playing cards took over. [6] With the end of royal rule, the patrons disappeared too. [9]

The cards survived; the games mostly did not. Today they are made mainly as decorative pieces, not packs for play. [7] A retired teacher at Mysuru’s Chamarajendra Academy of Visual Arts put it bluntly: “Nobody knows how to play Ganjifa games anymore.” [9]

Painters faced another problem. The work is slow and hard to make pay, and cheaper imitations done in acrylic paint undercut traditional artists. [9] [17]

The rules were written down in a king’s book. What was lost was the habit of playing them.

07

The painter who bought cards from the Palace

Raghupathi Bhat, born in 1957 and originally from Udupi district, settled in Mysuru and became so identified with this art that people call him “Ganjifa” Raghupathi Bhat. [13] [17] In his own telling, it began when he bought a few Ganjifa paintings from the Mysore Palace in the early 1970s. [11] He came across around 200-year-old Mysore originals and was struck that they had kept their beauty. [17]

From the early 1980s he worked to recreate them, keeping to natural colours and squirrel-hair brushes. [16] He set up a Ganjifa Art Museum in Srirangapatna in 1991, moved it to Wellington House in Mysuru in 1994, though it later closed. [12] A set of his cards is in the Victoria and Albert Museum in London. [15] He is a National Award winner, has run workshops to train others, [14] and has worked on restoring wall paintings and gold-leaf work in the Durbar Hall and Kalyana Mantapa of the Amba Vilas Palace, and has received the Madhya Pradesh government’s Kalidas Samman. [13]

He was not alone. Subramanya Raju, of a Mysuru painters’ family, started a Ganjifa painting course at Bengaluru’s Chitrakala Parishath in 1971, and the Parishath later published a book on the art, Splendours of Ganjifa Art (2019). [6] Mysuru artist Sudha Venkatesh learnt it from her father, a curator at the Mysore Palace. [16]

08

Mysore on India’s Ganjifa map

Mysuru · Raghurajpur & Chikiti (Odisha) · Bishnupur (West Bengal) · Sawantwadi (Maharashtra)
A selection of centres, not an exhaustive map.

Ganjifa lives in several places. The national arts centre IGNCA holds sets from Odisha, West Bengal and Maharashtra, including round 120-card Dashavatara packs by Odisha painters such as Banamali Mahapatra of Raghurajpur. [1]

Mysore’s identity is distinct: the most suits, the biggest packs, themes tied to the city’s own goddess and king, and the court-painting finish. In 2008, “Ganjifa Cards of Mysore” received Geographical Indication protection, which ties the product’s name to its place. [5] The cards are still made by traditional painters, the Chitrakars, mostly now as artworks and the odd display pack. [5]

09

A king wrote the rules. A painter read them back.

Two hundred years ago, a Mysore maharaja turned a game into a teaching tool, recorded it in a book, and had his painters fill hundreds of tiny circles with goddesses, planets and heroes.

Then the games fell quiet. What survived was the painting, kept alive by a handful of Mysuru artists who started from a few old cards and a stubborn idea that the brushwork mattered.

The next time you see a small round painting of Chamundeshwari in a Mysuru shop or gallery, it is worth a second look. It may be the face card of a game nobody has played for a century, still waiting for someone to deal.
