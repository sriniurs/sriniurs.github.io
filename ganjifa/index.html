Ganjifa:
A World Painted
on Playing Cards

A little box opens onto royal courts, stories of Vishnu, regional artists—and a game where the bigger number does not always win.

ABOUT 6 MINUTES

01

A painting you can play with

Schematic illustration · not a reproduction of a historical deck

A playing card does not have to be a rectangle with a heart in the corner.

Imagine opening a box and finding little painted circles: a royal figure, a fish, a cluster of repeated emblems. Spread across a table, they look like a miniature exhibition waiting for visitors.

Ganjifa brings that pleasure into a playing-card tradition. Many cards are round, but rectangular and even oval examples exist in the Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts collection. [1] Shape alone cannot identify every deck.

The useful question comes next: how do these pictures work together? A single card offers an image. The whole pack offers a system—and the possibility of a game.

02

A traveller in the royal courts

Ganjifa has Persian connections and was established in India in the Mughal period. IGNCA records a particularly tangible historical detail: in 1527, Babur sent Ganjifa cards as a gift. It also points to descriptions of packs in Abul Fazl’s Ain-i-Akbari, the account of Akbar’s reign. [2]

These references place cards within the exchange of objects and pleasures around a court. They do not tell us that every later Indian deck looked or played exactly like Babur’s gift.

As the tradition spread, its imagery developed in different directions. The story becomes more interesting when we follow that change: a game associated with imperial life could also become a vehicle for religious stories and local painting traditions.

03

Inside a 96-card pack

Schematic illustration · not a reproduction of a historical deck

8 suits × (1 king + 1 minister + 10 number cards)
= 96 cards

A standard Mughal Ganjifa pack has eight suits of twelve cards. A suit is a family of cards sharing an identifying sign. Each contains a king, a minister and ten numbered cards. The numbered cards use repeated emblems, rather than requiring the same numeral in every corner. [3]

The Horniman Museum describes a set whose suit signs include a crown, a sword and coins. Such objects give a deck its vocabulary: the player learns to recognise a suit before judging the card’s rank.

Its museum record also offers a small detective story. The collection has 95 cards, because the king of the red-coin suit is missing. Knowing the pack’s structure tells us exactly what the surviving object lacks. [3]

04

When the suits tell sacred stories

Schematic illustration · not a reproduction of a historical deck

In Dashavatara Ganjifa, the imagery draws on the ten incarnations of Vishnu. A familiar form has ten suits and 120 cards; IGNCA also describes standard Dashavatara packs of 144 cards. [4] The name therefore does not guarantee one pack size.

Here the pictures offer another route into the deck. Someone familiar with the stories can recognise a figure or emblem and connect it with a larger narrative. Someone unfamiliar with them has a reason to ask what the image represents.

The distinction is useful: the story helps us read the imagery, while the rules explain how the card functions in a game. Appreciating one does not automatically teach us the other. We can enjoy the artwork immediately and still have something substantial left to learn.

05

The number can surprise you

Recognise the suit → recall its ranking → follow the cards played

A glimpse of play—not a universal rulebook.

IGNCA’s workshop account describes players competing to win rounds, or tricks. A trick is a small contest within the larger game, with players contributing cards and a winner taking the round. [4]

One striking feature is that the numbered cards do not run in the same order in every suit. The account describes groups of suits with opposite numerical sequences. A player cannot simply assume that ten always beats one.

That changes what you need to remember. Recognising the symbol is only the first step; you must also know the ranking used for that suit and keep track of what has appeared. The artwork becomes information you use under pressure.

Exact dealing, opening and scoring conventions need the rules for the version being played. This glimpse explains the attraction without pretending to teach a complete game.

06

One tradition, several local accents

Odisha · West Bengal · Maharashtra · Karnataka
A selection of regional connections, not an exhaustive map.

Place matters. IGNCA’s catalogue records sets from Raghurajpur and Chikiti in Odisha, Bishnupur in West Bengal, and Sawantwadi in Maharashtra. It also records subjects beyond the familiar ten-incarnation theme, including Mahabharata scenes. [1]

Karnataka has its own connection. The Government of India’s tourism account identifies Mysore Ganjifa as a regional tradition and records its geographical indication recognition in 2008. [5] A geographical indication connects a product’s identity with its place of origin.

These names are more than stops on a map. They give us a better way to describe an object: where was it made, what subjects does it use, and which local tradition does it belong to?

A generic label begins the conversation. The regional details make it precise.

07

The maker changes what we see

Take one documented example. IGNCA lists a round, ten-suit, 120-card Dashavatara set by Banamali Mahapatra of Raghurajpur, dated 1996. The same catalogue records much larger sets by this artist, including a 288-card, 24-suit set from 2007. [1]

Those records challenge the idea that every deck must fit the most familiar formula. They also return a person’s name to objects that might otherwise be described simply as “traditional cards”.

Materials vary too. The World Culture Museum describes historical cards made from paper or cloth as well as costly materials such as ivory. [6] We should not imagine one recipe behind every surviving pack.

Look closely at the spacing of emblems, the figure inside its border and the consistency across a whole suit. A deck asks its maker to balance individual pictures with a coherent visual system.

08

The picture is only the first move

The World Culture Museum has reprinted a Ganjifa deck from its collection and offered visitors a chance to try playing it. [6] That is an appealing way to bridge two experiences: looking at an object and discovering what it asks people to do.

A display can reveal the painted details. A playing session reveals another layer: which marks matter, what a player must remember, and how the cards create a shared challenge.

That is what makes Ganjifa such a rewarding subject. We can enter through history, painting, religious imagery or the pleasure of a game. Follow any one of those paths far enough and the others begin to appear.

Open a little box of cards, and there is room for an artist, a historian and several players around the same table.
