Why Does Your Knee Hurt More Going Downstairs?

The landing feels fine. The third step hurts. Follow one step in slow motion to see what your thigh and kneecap are doing, and what the pain can tell you.

Thigh musclesKneecap loadWhen to get help

7–8 MINUTE READ · 9 EPISODES

01

The third step

On her way out of a Mysuru apartment, Meera took the stairs because the lift was busy. Across the landing her knee had felt fine. On the third step down, a sharp ache appeared around the kneecap. She caught the rail, waited, then brought both feet onto the same step. By the time she reached the ground floor, she was walking normally again.

Zappy had seen her climb those same stairs that morning with a bag of vegetables. He figured going down would be the easy half of the trip. Meera had expected the same thing. Her knee seemed to have an opinion about the direction of travel.

Meera’s story is fictional, but the pattern is familiar. A step down asks the leg to lower the body under control while supporting its weight. That is a different task from walking on the landing. Where the pain is, how it began and what else happens alongside it help determine what the symptom means. [1] [5]

02

Your thigh is working the brakes

Pause just before Meera’s lower foot lands. The other foot is planted above it, supporting her. As that supporting knee bends, the quadriceps at the front of the thigh develops tension while lengthening. This is called an eccentric contraction. The muscle helps control the drop, like the arm muscles lowering a full kettle slowly to the counter.

The important bit happens before the reaching foot takes over. If the supporting leg gave way too quickly, the next foot would have to catch the body more abruptly. The hip and ankle also help manage the descent. In a laboratory study of 17 healthy young adults, the knee extensors did the largest share of the measured work of dissipating mechanical energy during the descent studied. That describes a movement strategy, with individual variation. [1]

On the landing, Meera can move forward without lowering herself the height of a stair on every step. On the staircase, she repeats that controlled lowering several times in a row. A sore knee can make that specific demand obvious long before she notices trouble on level ground.

03

What the kneecap has to do with it

The kneecap, or patella, sits at the front of the knee. The quadriceps tendon attaches above it and the patellar tendon continues below it towards the shin. As the thigh muscle pulls, the kneecap helps transmit that pull to straighten the knee. It also presses against the groove at the front of the thighbone. The meeting point is the patellofemoral joint. [2]

Think of the difference between bending your knee while sitting on a chair and bending it while lowering yourself off a step. The angle may look similar in a photograph. In the second situation, your leg is controlling a moving load. The pull from the thigh and the angle of the knee both matter to the force at the kneecap.

Force and pressure tell you different things. As the knee bends, the area over which the kneecap contacts the thighbone changes too. The same force spread across a different area can produce a different pressure. This is one reason a single headline number can’t tell you how a specific spot in your knee feels on the stairs. [2]

04

Down hurts more. Up can load more.

Here’s the surprise in the numbers. A systematic review reported average peak force at the kneecap joint in healthy participants of about 0.9 times body weight during level walking, 2.8 times going down stairs and 3.2 times going up. The figures are estimates from the studies reviewed. They refer to the force between the kneecap and thighbone, and they describe peak values rather than the load throughout the whole step. [2]

Take the ratio of 2.8 to 0.9 and you get about 3.1. That is a comparison between two group estimates. If Meera finds descent worse than ascent, her experience can still fit the research. Pain depends on the person and the task. The angle where it hurts, how she controls the lowering and how sensitive the knee is that day can all matter. The review also reported a different pattern of averages in people with patellofemoral pain. [2] [3]

The useful question is which part of the movement brings on symptoms for Meera. That detail is worth more in a consultation than trying to assign her knee the force reported for a group of volunteers.

05

Same staircase, different clues

Meera points around the kneecap with her whole hand. Pain around or behind the kneecap during stairs, squatting or other bent-knee activities can fit patellofemoral pain. A clinician will ask about the pattern and examine the knee before settling on that explanation. [3]

Picture her neighbour, who has gradually developed aching and stiffness and now struggles with both stairs and long walks. Knee osteoarthritis becomes one possibility. NICE describes a common clinical pattern in people aged 45 or over with activity-related joint pain and either no morning stiffness or stiffness lasting 30 minutes or less. That pattern helps guide assessment. [4]

Now picture someone whose knee swelled after a twist and who can no longer straighten it normally. The injury and movement restriction change what needs to be checked, including possible meniscus or ligament injury. A localised sore spot just below the kneecap can also point towards a tendon in some people. A clinician considers timing, swelling, movement and tenderness together. [5]

For an appointment, note whether you can point to the pain with one finger or need a whole hand, when it started, what triggers it, and whether the knee swells, locks or gives way.

Before an appointment, note the location, the onset and any swelling, locking or giving way.

06

How a busy week can change a step

Meera looks back over her week. She’d helped move boxes, walked farther than usual at the market, then gone up and down three floors while the lift was being serviced. Those details give a clinician something to explore. A recent change in activity can be relevant, even when no single step felt like an injury. [3]

Here’s a fictional count. If she normally descends three flights a day and does nine one day, she has tripled the number of flights. That says nothing precise about the force on her knee. It does show why an ordinary-looking staircase can be the last of many repetitions that day. The pace, number of steps, bags she carries and how much recovery she gets all help describe the workload.

For a manageable flare without urgent symptoms, she can use the rail, take the lift for some trips and break up repeated journeys while she works out what she can tolerate. If symptoms keep recurring or interfere with everyday activity, an assessment can help her decide how to build back. [3] [5]

The Deep DiveWhat helps and when to get assessed
07

What rehab is trying to change

Imagine Meera is assessed and the clinician finds that her symptoms fit patellofemoral pain. A 2024 best-practice guide recommends education and knee-targeted exercise, with hip-targeted exercise added where appropriate. Depending on the individual, a clinician may also consider approaches such as taping or prefabricated foot orthoses. The starting point is what she can do and what she needs to get back to. [3]

For someone whose stairs are difficult, an early exercise might be standing from a higher chair, followed later by a supervised or supported low step-down when appropriate. The height, support and repetitions are adjustable. A clinician can select a starting point and help progress it according to symptoms, function and recovery. These are examples of ways to vary demand.

If Meera instead has knee osteoarthritis, tailored therapeutic exercise is also a core treatment. NICE recommends exercise such as local muscle strengthening or general aerobic fitness, matched to the person’s needs. With either condition, a useful goal is concrete. Meera might choose getting from her floor to the gate with enough confidence to go out again in the evening. [4]

08

When the stair story needs a clinician

When Meera says her knee “stuck”, Prof. Bolt asks what happened. Did she stop because bending hurt, or did the joint actually get stuck so she could no longer straighten it? Was there a fall, a twist, rapid swelling or a feeling that the knee wouldn’t hold her? The words matter because they change how soon she should get assessed. [5]

Seek urgent medical assessment if you suddenly cannot bear weight or move the knee, it is badly swollen or visibly deformed, it truly locks or gives way, or you have a hot or red knee with fever or chills. Severe pain also warrants urgent advice. After an injury, get help getting off the stairs safely if you can’t trust the leg. [5]

For pain that keeps returning, limits normal activity or fails to improve over a few weeks, book a clinical assessment. The clinician can ask about the pattern and check strength, movement and swelling. In a typical osteoarthritis presentation, diagnosis can often be made clinically. Imaging is considered when the history or examination raises another question. [4] [5]

Get urgent medical assessment for a hot or red knee with fever, inability to bear weight or move it, major swelling or deformity, true locking, giving way or severe pain. [5]

The VerdictBack to the third step
09

Back on the landing

Meera is back at the top of the same flight. This time she takes the handrail before starting and asks Zappy to carry the bag. At the third step, she notices exactly when the pain arrives. The foot reaching for the lower stair is still in the air. It’s the supporting knee above that is doing the lowering.

That gives her a better way to describe it at her appointment. She can point to the area around the kneecap and say the pain comes on during descent, while walking on level ground is comfortable. She can also report when it started and whether there has been swelling, a twist, locking or giving way.

The practical verdict is to treat the staircase as a clue about which task is difficult. Use support to get down safely. If the pattern persists or keeps you from everyday activities, get it assessed and agree on a plan that helps you regain the movement you need. Seek urgent care if the warning signs above appear.
