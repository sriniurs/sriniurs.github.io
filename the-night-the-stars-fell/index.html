ABOUT 9 MINUTES

01

A date with a comet’s dust

Every November, Earth drives through a trail of dust left behind by a comet. When those specks hit our air, they burn as shooting stars. This is the Leonid meteor shower.

In 2026 it runs from 6 to 30 November, and the busiest night is 17 November, according to the International Meteor Organization’s calendar. [1] The name comes from the constellation Leo, the Lion: trace the streaks backwards and they all seem to start there.

Here is the lucky part for India. Forecasters put the peak at about 23:45 to 00:00 GMT on the night of 17 November. [2] [3] In Indian time, that is about 5:15 to 5:30 am on Wednesday, 18 November 2026 (our conversion). That is the dark hour just before dawn, when the Leo part of the sky is high and the half-moon has already set. [1] [4]

When to look: the night of Tuesday, 17 November into Wednesday, 18 November 2026, from about 1 am until the sky begins to brighten (sunrise in Mysuru is about 6:20 am). [4] [7] No telescope needed: lie back, face east and look up. Give your eyes 20 minutes to adjust. The mornings of 17 and 19 November are worth a look too. [4]

Keep expectations honest. Under a truly dark sky you might see 10 to 15 meteors an hour. [2] From a lit city terrace, fewer. This is a quiet year, not a spectacular one.

02

A grain of sand that lights up the sky

The dust comes from Comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle, which loops around the Sun about once every 33 years. [2] Each time it swings close to the Sun, it warms up and sheds a fresh trail of grit along its path. Earth crosses that path every mid-November, like a car driving through a cloud of flies.

The pieces are tiny. Most visible Leonids are between 1 mm and 1 cm across, about the size of a grain of sand or a small pebble. [5] So how can a speck that small be seen from hundreds of kilometres away?

Speed. Leonid dust hits our atmosphere at about 71 km per second. At that speed you could go around the whole Earth in under four minutes. [5] They are among the fastest meteors of any shower, because Earth and the comet’s dust stream meet nearly head-on. [8]

How a speck becomes a shooting star
Comet sheds dust near the Sun→Earth crosses the trail in November→Dust hits the air at 71 km/s→Air and grain glow, about 100 km up

Sources: [2] [5] [6] [9]

The glow is not the grain “catching fire” like a matchstick. Slamming into air that fast heats the grain until its surface turns to vapour, and it strips electrons off atoms of both the grain and the air. When those electrons rejoin their atoms, they give off light. [6] [9]

All this happens remarkably high. A typical meteor starts glowing around 100 km above the ground, and most are gone before they get below 80 km. [9] For scale, a passenger jet cruises at about 11 km.

A shooting star isn’t a star. It’s a speck of comet dust, often smaller than a peppercorn, vaporising about 100 km above your head.
03

Why they all point to the Lion

Watch for a while and you’ll notice something odd. The streaks shoot off in every direction, yet if you trace each one backwards, they all seem to start from the same patch of sky in Leo. That patch is called the radiant.

It’s a trick of perspective. The grains in the stream are travelling almost parallel to each other when they reach our atmosphere. Stand between the rails of a long, straight railway line and the two rails seem to meet in the distance, even though they never do. Parallel meteor paths, seen from the ground, do the same thing: they appear to fan out from one vanishing point. [13]

Two handy consequences follow. Meteors near the radiant look short, because they are coming almost straight at you; the long, dramatic streaks appear further away from it. [13] And you don’t need to find Leo at all. Just look at a wide stretch of dark sky; the shower will find you. [2]

04

The night the stars fell

Most years the Leonids are a modest show. But roughly every 33 years, around the time the comet passes, Earth can hit a thick, fresh strand of its dust. Then the shower becomes a meteor storm, officially at least 1,000 meteors an hour, or one every second. [5]

The two most famous storms, in 1833 and 1966, produced tens of thousands of meteors in their peak hour. [4] On 17 November 1966, observers in North America reported 40 or more meteors a second. That storm turns 60 this year. [1] [3]

The most recent storms came in 2001 and 2002. [10] [11] 2026 is an ordinary year in between. Ordinary, but each of those 10-odd streaks an hour is still a speck of dust the comet shed centuries ago. [4]

Storms have a way of frightening people. To understand how one terrifying night turned into a branch of science, we have to go back to 1833.

05

The professor who asked the public

In the early hours of 13 November 1833, the skies over the United States filled with meteors. [15] At the peak, an estimated 100,000 meteors an hour were streaking down. [14] Some people thought the world was ending. [27]

Denison Olmsted, a professor at Yale College, did something unusual for his time. He wrote a letter about the storm that newspapers across the country reprinted, and asked ordinary people to send in what they had seen. [15] In effect, he crowdsourced it.

Sifting through the reports, he noticed two things. The meteors all radiated from one point in Leo, and that point moved across the sky along with the stars as the night went on. [14] If the meteors were weather, something in our air, the point would have stayed fixed to the ground. Because it moved with the stars, the meteors had to be coming from space. [14] [15]

His papers in the American Journal of Science are often counted as the birth of the study of meteor showers. [14] The last piece came in 1866, when Giovanni Schiaparelli showed that a meteor shower shares its orbit with a comet. That is how the Leonids were tied to Tempel-Tuttle. [16]

The clue that meteors come from space was hiding in plain sight: their starting point moved with the stars, not with the Earth.
06

Forecasting a storm to the minute

For more than a century, predicting a Leonid storm was guesswork. Forecasters knew the 33-year rhythm, but some “storm years” fizzled and the timing could be off by hours. [18]

The breakthrough came in the late 1990s from two astronomers, David Asher of Armagh Observatory and Rob McNaught of the Australian National University, building on methods pioneered by Russian researchers. [17] Their insight: each time the comet passes the Sun, it leaves its own thin ribbon of dust. Those ribbons stay intact for centuries, and they don’t sit exactly on the comet’s path. [17]

OLD METHOD

Track the comet itself. Storm timings were sometimes right within hours, sometimes missed completely. [18]

DUST-TRAIL METHOD

Track each separate ribbon of dust, nudged by Jupiter’s gravity. Uncertainty came down to about five minutes. [17] [18]

The test came in November 1999. The pair had predicted when Earth would cross a ribbon of dust the comet shed in 1899, and the storm arrived on cue, peaking at roughly 4,000 meteors an hour. [17] [19] They also called the bigger storms of 2001 and 2002. [18]

So when you watch a Leonid, you can know roughly when that grain left the comet. The next close approach of Tempel-Tuttle to the Sun is expected in May 2031, and modellers are already working out what the 2030s might bring. [11]

07

The night the satellites ducked

A grain of sand is harmless to you on the ground. In orbit it’s a different story, because nothing stops it before it hits.

When a storm was expected in November 1998, the space agencies took no chances. The Hubble Space Telescope turned its back on Leo for about ten hours around the peak. [20] European operators switched off the instruments on their ERS-1 and ERS-2 Earth-observation satellites. [20] The biggest worry was not a hole but a spark: electrical discharges and short circuits were seen as the most serious hazard. [20] [21]

The fear wasn’t imaginary. After the 1993 Leonids, engineers found a crater about 0.9 mm wide in a radiator on one of Hubble’s cameras; the camera itself was fine. [21]

08

Darkness is the best telescope

The biggest thing standing between you and the Leonids isn’t clouds or the moon. It’s streetlights. One widely quoted study found that 80% of the world lives under a light-polluted sky. [22] A faint meteor that would be obvious in a village simply vanishes into the orange glow over a city.

India now protects one patch of night on purpose. Hanle, in Ladakh, is the country’s first Dark Sky Reserve, set up with the Indian Institute of Astrophysics in Bengaluru, which runs a high-altitude observatory there. [22] An area of roughly 22 km around Hanle has been notified as the reserve. [23] To keep it dark, residents have been given lamp shades, blackout curtains and warm-toned bulbs. [24]

You don’t need Ladakh for the Leonids. Even a short drive out of town, away from the brightest lights, makes a real difference. Switch off the terrace bulb and keep your phone screen dark, so your eyes stay adjusted.

Want a photo? What actually works on a phone
  1. Keep it still. A tripod helps, but leaning the phone against something firm works too. Use the self-timer so your finger doesn’t shake it. [25]
  2. Go long. Night-sky exposures typically run 5 to 30 seconds. [25] On Pixel phones, Night Sight’s astrophotography setting switches on automatically once the phone is steady on a tripod. [26]
  3. Know the catch. Some night modes blend several frames together, which can wipe out a meteor that appears in only one of them. [26] Take many shots and hope one is lucky.
09

A very old visitor

Put it all together and the shooting star you might catch on Wednesday, 18 November 2026 becomes quite a story. It is a speck of dust that left a comet centuries ago. It has circled the Sun ever since, waiting for Earth to cross its path. In its last second it will travel 71 km and glow 100 km above Mysuru.

Nearly two hundred years ago, a professor worked out where these specks come from by asking ordinary people what they saw. Today the forecasts are good to within minutes. Only one thing still depends on you: setting the alarm.
