Green Crackers,
Clear Rules

The chemistry inside the cracker—and the atmosphere outside it.

01

First, the Karnataka window

8 pm–10 pm

Karnataka · 8, 9 & 10 November 2026
Reported Deepavali directions: only green crackers permitted.

Reports of the state’s directions set out a two-hour window on these three dates. They also describe restrictions around hospitals, educational institutions and other prohibited areas. [1]

Police guidance separately calls for licensed sellers, adult supervision for children and suitable firefighting material nearby. [2] These are conditions of use; a product’s green label does not change the time or location rules.

The original state circular was not available for inspection here, so the current local rule is attributed to reporting. The science behind the label answers a different question: what happens when the product burns?

02

A small package of chemistry

A firework brings together a fuel and an oxidiser, a chemical that supplies oxygen for the reaction. The familiar effects come from energy released as heat, light and sound. Different products use different formulations. [5]

Colour often comes from metals or metal compounds. Heating them can produce characteristic light: the chemicals matter, rather than a dye simply colouring the smoke.

That makes the word “green” slightly confusing. A green-looking flame is an optical effect. A green-cracker label concerns the product’s emissions performance. Those two meanings of green do not establish each other.

This is also why changing a formulation is an engineering task. The ingredients contribute to how a product burns and what it releases, as well as the visual effect people expect.

03

What NEERI changed—and what gets compared

CSIR-NEERI, the National Environmental Engineering Research Institute in Nagpur, developed reduced-emission formulations. Its description of green crackers concerns modified ingredients or proportions that reduce emissions compared with a conventional product in the same category. [3]

Its criteria include at least 30% lower particulate emissions. An alternative route combines at least 20% lower particulate emissions with at least 10% lower gaseous emissions, under the stated comparison. [3]

These percentages describe different measured outputs. They are not a promise that every pollutant falls by the same amount, and the second route should not be read as 30% less particulate matter.

The key idea is a comparison between products under testing. “Green” means an improvement against that reference, rather than an absence of combustion or its by-products.

THE DEEP DIVEWhat leaves a product is not the same measurement as what remains in the air.
04

Emissions and air concentration are different

Emissions describe what a source releases. Concentration describes how much pollutant is present in a volume of air at a particular place and time.

Suppose an improved cracker releases fewer particles than its conventional counterpart. That is a product-level finding. A neighbourhood air monitor, meanwhile, samples air containing contributions from many sources, mixed and moved by the weather.

PRODUCT QUESTION

How much particulate matter did the tested cracker release?

AIR QUESTION

How much particulate matter is present in the air here, now?

A 30% reduction in the first measurement cannot simply be copied into the second. The number of fireworks, other pollution sources and movement of the air all matter. [6]

That distinction is useful beyond Deepavali. A cleaner individual source and cleaner outdoor air are connected, but they are not interchangeable claims.

05

The arithmetic has a second variable: quantity

Here is a deliberately simple example. Imagine one conventional cracker releases 100 units of particles and an improved version releases 70 units. That is a 30% reduction for each item.

Using twice as many improved items produces more particles in this example than the single conventional one. Using the same number would preserve the per-item reduction.

This is illustrative arithmetic, not a measured result for a brand, an exposure estimate or an outdoor-air forecast. It reveals the missing variable in a product label: the amount used.

The calculation does not erase the technical improvement. It shows why emissions per item and total emissions need to be considered separately.

06

The weather joins the experiment

Smoke does not stay where a firework releases it. Wind can carry pollutants away from a source or towards another location. When air is still, local pollution can build up. [6]

Another influence is a temperature inversion: cooler air near the ground lies beneath warmer air. Such conditions can limit vertical mixing and help trap pollutants close to the surface.

Picture two evenings with the same products and quantities, but different air movement. The resulting concentrations need not be the same. Product chemistry explains the source; weather helps explain where its emissions go.

This is a general explanation, not a forecast of Bengaluru’s weather on these dates. It also explains why a brief view of visible smoke cannot by itself establish how clean the surrounding air is.

07

What the mark and QR code are for

When India launched green crackers in 2019, it also introduced an identification logo and QR-coding system to help address counterfeit products. [4]

The purpose is traceability: connecting the claim on a packet with information in the prescribed verification system. A green wrapper is a design choice; an identification system is meant to support a check.

The West Bengal Pollution Control Board’s technical FAQ describes green-cracker identification and verification. That is background about the product system, not a source for Karnataka’s current permitted hours. [7]

A QR square is only a way to encode information. Its presence alone does not prove that a product meets a standard; the relevant information and verification route matter. Nor does a verified product override local restrictions or eliminate ordinary firework hazards.

08

One word, three different questions

The word “green” sits at the meeting point of chemistry, measurement and everyday language. The formulation concerns what burns. Testing concerns what is released. The atmosphere determines how those releases mix with everything else in the air.

That is the useful discovery behind the label: improving a product is one part of a much larger system. The spark may last only a moment, but understanding it takes us from the ingredients inside a small packet to the moving air above a whole neighbourhood.
