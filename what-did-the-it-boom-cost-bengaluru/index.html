What Did the IT Boom Cost Bengaluru?

Bengaluru got jobs, exports and the state’s highest incomes. It also paid in lakes, water, traffic and rent. Here is the full ledger, for a Mysuru that wants to be next.

ABOUT 8 MINUTES

01

Before you copy a city, check its bill

Karnataka wants Mysuru to become the next Bengaluru. So it is fair to ask what becoming Bengaluru actually cost Bengaluru.

The 2026–27 state budget announced that Mysuru would be developed as Karnataka’s “second IT city”, partly to take pressure off Bengaluru. [1] In July, the Karnataka Digital Economy Mission put Mysuru’s IT exports at ₹5,700 crore a year, the second-largest in the state. [2]

We covered Mysuru’s side of the story in Mysuru: Palace to Pixels. This article covers Bengaluru’s side: what the city gained from IT and what it paid for it, laid out like a shop ledger with income on one side and costs on the other.

ZappyBengaluru is rich. Who cares what it cost?

Prof. BoltEveryone who sits in its traffic. A shop that only counts its sales and never its bills goes bust.

This is a ledger, not a verdict. Bengaluru gained a great deal and paid a great deal.
02

Income: what Bengaluru got

Start with the income. Karnataka exports software and services worth about ₹4.09 lakh crore a year, 43.67% of India’s total, the most of any state. Its 5,500-plus IT companies employ about 12 lakh people directly and support another 31 lakh jobs indirectly. [3]

Most of that money lands in one district. In 2024–25, income per person in Bengaluru Urban was ₹8,55,960, against a state average of ₹3,86,156 and ₹1,44,449 in Kalaburagi. [4] Bengaluru Urban produces almost 40% of the state’s economy on its own. [5]

People followed the jobs. Census counts put Bengaluru city at about 33 lakh people in 1991, 57 lakh in 2001 and 85 lakh in 2011. [6]

03

Bill 1: the garden city went grey

Bengaluru was called the Garden City and the City of Lakes. Researchers at the Indian Institute of Science (IISc), led by T.V. Ramachandra, have tracked its land from satellite images since 1973.

In 1973, about 8% of the area they studied was built up and about 68% had vegetation. [7] By 2023, vegetation had fallen to 2.9%. The team projects that paved surfaces will cover more than 98% of the city by 2038 if the trend continues. [8]

The lakes fared badly too. A 2015–16 field survey by the same group found that 98% of the lakes it checked had been encroached on and 90% were fed by sewage. [7]

ZappyCan’t they just plant trees again?

Prof. BoltOn a rooftop, maybe. You can’t dig a lake back out from under an apartment block.

Green cover and lakes are the one bill that money can’t pay back later.
04

Bill 2: the summer the borewells ran dry

Lakes and open ground let rainwater soak in and refill the groundwater. In March 2024, Chief Minister Siddaramaiah said 6,900 of the city’s 14,000 government borewells had dried up. Against a need of 2,600 million litres a day (MLD), the city was short by about 500 MLD. [9] The Cauvery river supplied about 1,450 MLD of that need and borewells about 650. [9]

The fix came from far away. In October 2024 the fifth stage of the Cauvery water scheme opened, bringing an extra 775 MLD from about 100 km away for some 50 lakh people. [10] Its foundation was laid in 2014, and the water board has taken about ten years to launch each Cauvery stage. [11]

A city that loses its own water has to pipe it in from elsewhere, and each new pipeline has taken about ten years.
05

Bill 3: 168 hours a year in traffic

TomTom, a Dutch navigation company, ranked Bengaluru the world’s second most congested city in 2025, behind Mexico City. By its count, a 10 km drive took 36 minutes on average, and a rush-hour commuter lost 168 hours in the year, about seven days. [12]

Bengaluru Traffic Police dispute the numbers. Their own data says a 10 km peak-hour trip took 34.5 minutes, not the 38 minutes TomTom reported for peak hours, and that congestion eased after July 2025. [13]

Public transport is catching up, slowly. Namma Metro grew from 6.7 km in 2011 to 96.1 km in 2026. [14]

Whichever figure you trust, Bengaluru’s metro is still catching up with traffic that built up long before it.
06

Bill 4: the rent

Well-paid jobs push up what everyone pays for a home. ANAROCK, a property consultancy, found that average rents along IT corridors such as Sarjapur Road rose as much as 76% in three years. [15] Another firm, PropTiger, found that Bengaluru home prices rose 24% in a single year to early 2026, the fastest of India’s eight biggest cities. [16]

That is good news if you own a house. It is bad news for the cook, the driver and the teacher whose pay didn’t rise with an IT salary (our observation).

The boom made Bengaluru’s homeowners richer and its renters poorer.
07

When the bill reached the office

In the end, companies pay the bill too. On 30 August 2022, heavy rain flooded the Outer Ring Road, the corridor where more than half a million people work in IT and banking. The companies’ association wrote to the Chief Minister that staff were stuck for over five hours and that the flood cost them about ₹225 crore in a single day. [17]

The same letter said firms on that road earn about $22 billion a year, 32% of Bengaluru’s IT revenue. [18]

ZappySo the lakes that were filled in came back as floods?

Prof. BoltRain still has to drain somewhere. If the lakes and drains are built over, the water ends up on the roads.

The costs that started with lakes and roads ended up hitting the IT companies themselves.

Look closely and some items appear on both sides. The metro and the new Cauvery water are gains, but they are also the price of fixing earlier damage.

Many of the costs trace back to land: lakes built over, ground paved and drains blocked.
09

Is Mysuru already on the same road?

Partly, yes. A study of the Mysuru planning area (507 sq km, including Nanjangud) by C.S. Manjunatha of Karnataka State Open University found that built-up land rose from 15.9% in 2000 to 44.7% in 2016. Farmland shrank from about 78% to 50%, and lakes and ponds lost 3.42 sq km. [19]

An earlier IISc study of a smaller area of Mysuru found vegetation fell from about 51% in 1973 to about 9% in 2009. [20] The two studies measured different areas, so their numbers can’t be compared directly (our observation).

Meanwhile the tech plan is just getting started. KDEM’s target is $10 billion in exports; Mysuru’s ₹5,700 crore today is about 1.4% of the state’s ₹4.09 lakh crore (our arithmetic). [2] [3]

Mysuru’s land has been changing for 20 years already. The IT boom hasn’t really arrived yet.
10

Two ways to read the ledger

The case for growing fast. Supporters say the jobs are the point. Young people from Mysuru shouldn’t have to leave home to find good work, and a second hub takes pressure off Bengaluru, which is the stated aim of the budget plan. [1] They add that Bengaluru shows the damage can be fixed: it now has new Cauvery water and a growing metro. [10] [14]

The case for growing carefully. Sceptics say Bengaluru’s fixes came about a decade after the damage, and some damage can’t be fixed at all: a lake that has been built over is gone. [11] [7] The Mysuru study itself recommended a joint committee of the city corporation, the development authority (MUDA), and the forest and irrigation departments to manage growth, and satellite mapping to track land use. [19]

Both sides want the jobs. They disagree about whether to protect the lakes, roads and water first or build first and fix later.

The real choice isn’t between growth and no growth. It’s about what gets protected first.
!

OPINION: copy the jobs, not the bill

This section is our opinion, not reporting.

LAKES FIRST

Map every lake, pond and drain in the Mysuru planning area now, while they still exist, and keep the map public.

TRANSPORT BEFORE TRAFFIC

Plan buses and transport for new tech parks before the jams start, not ten years after.

WATER BEFORE TOWERS

Ask how much water a new layout needs before it is approved, not after its borewells run dry.

So, should Mysuru become the next Bengaluru?

For the jobs, many would say yes. Bengaluru’s ledger shows where the bill comes from: lakes, water and roads. Mysuru still has most of what Bengaluru lost. Whether it keeps them depends on planning decisions made now.
