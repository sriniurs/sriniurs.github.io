ABOUT 7 MINUTES

01

Three things with Mysuru’s name on them

A string of jasmine in the hair at a wedding. A betel leaf folded with areca nut after the meal. A small, fragrant banana on the pooja plate.

For generations, the best of each in old Mysore carried the city’s name: Mysore Mallige, Mysore Vilyedele (betel leaf), and the Nanjangud Rasabale banana. All three received a Geographical Indication (GI) tag in the mid-2000s, the same legal protection that guards Darjeeling tea and Mysore silk. [1] [2]

And yet a recent Deccan Herald report found all three “on the verge of extinction,” with hardly 10 acres of each under cultivation. [3]

How does a protected crop disappear? The short answer: a GI tag protects a name. It does not plant a field, pick a flower, or stop a fungus.

ZappyIf they’re protected by law, how can they vanish?

Prof. BoltThe law protects the label. Nobody passed a law about who’ll grow it.

02

Mysore Mallige: the fragrance next door

When the Horticulture Department applied for the GI tag, it described Mysore jasmine’s special trait simply: a fragrance that lasts long and can be felt in the vicinity. The filing listed growing areas around the city such as Devanoor Kere, Hole Kesaruru, Matti and K.R. Mill. [4] The flower is tied to the Wadiyars, and grown mostly around Mysuru and partly in Srirangapatna. [5]

Here is a surprising detail from that same application, written two decades ago: merchants in Mysore’s market were already selling Tamil Nadu’s dundu mallige as “Mysore mallige.” [4] Today, Tamil Nadu jasmine makes up a major share of what arrives at Devaraja Market. It looks similar, but differs slightly in colour, shape, fragrance and shelf life. [6]

When the department tried to form a growers’ group to revive the flower, it could find only five or six growers, against the 15 to 20 it needed. A shortage of labour to pluck the flowers made things worse. [7]

The real Mysore Mallige is still out there, but most “Mysore mallige” you buy probably isn’t.
03

Mysore Vilyedele: a farm inside the city

Few people know that betel was farmed right inside Mysuru. Betel gardens, the yele thota, dotted Ramanuja Road and the Vidyaranyapuram–Ashokapuram area, and growers said the farms dated back to the Maharajas’ time. [8] The leaf’s character is credited to black clay soil and a hot, humid climate. [9] Its taste is described as sweet and burning-hot at once, with a strong aroma. [10]

Then the city grew outward. Growers reported their farms shrinking from about 700–800 acres to 500 as land was eyed for layouts. [8] A Devaraja Market trader estimated that only about 2% of betel leaves reaching the city market were genuine Mysore Vilyedele, with yellower, smaller leaves from Salem filling the gap. [9]

But this one has a refuge. In Udbur, about 12 km from the city, betel is still grown on hundreds of acres, in three varieties: bili yele (white), kappu yele (black) and yenne kempu (oily red). A leaf sells for about ₹1.50. [11] Every afternoon, growers gather for a market so quick it is called the ardha ghante veelyadele santhe, the half-hour betel fair. [10]

ZappyA whole market in thirty minutes?

Prof. BoltBetel leaves wilt. Nobody waits for a slow buyer.

04

Nanjangud Rasabale: the banana a fungus nearly killed

The Rasabale is small, just 5–8 cm long, with buttery-soft flesh and a strong aroma, and is traditionally traced to the area around Nanjangud. [12] It grows in black soil along the banks of the Kapila river. [13]

Its enemy is Panama wilt, a soil-borne fungus (Fusarium oxysporum). It is the same disease that wiped out the world’s favourite export banana, the Gros Michel, in the 1950s. [14] The fungus lives in soil and spreads through running water, farm tools and infected planting material. Farmers in one village recall the trouble starting when canal water from the Kabini dam replaced their rain-fed pond. [14] In a survey of banana farms, the Rasabale had the worst wilt rate of any variety, about 60%. [15]

05

What a GI tag can’t do

A GI TAG CAN

Stop others from legally selling their produce under the name “Mysore” or “Nanjangud.”

A GI TAG CAN’T

Kill a fungus, stop a land sale, find pluckers at dawn, or guarantee a fair price.

A former additional director of horticulture put it plainly: so far, the GI tag has only protected these varieties from unauthorised use. [17] Each crop fell to a different everyday pressure:

CropMain pressureWhere it survives
Mysore MalligeCheaper look-alikes, labour to pick, long wait for yieldA handful of families near Mysuru
Mysore VilyedeleCity land prices, price swings, labour costsUdbur and a few city farms
Nanjangud RasabalePanama wilt fungusNanjangud taluk, now recovering

Sources: [3] [6] [7] [10] [17]

There is money on the table: the department offers about ₹99,000 per hectare to farmers growing a GI crop. [17] But a subsidy cannot outbid a real-estate offer next to a ring road, and it cannot make a diseased plant grow.

06

How the banana came back

Saving the Rasabale took science, not just subsidy. The fungus spreads through suckers, the side-shoots farmers use as planting material. So labs began growing disease-free plantlets from tiny shoot tips through tissue culture. The University of Agricultural Sciences started a project with a ₹1 crore state grant back in 2007. [18] In 2025, researchers at JSS in Mysuru published a protocol that also uses DNA barcoding to confirm a plantlet really is Rasabale and not a look-alike. [19]

One honest caution from the experts: tissue-culture saplings are disease-free, not disease-resistant. [14] Farmers also rely on crop rotation and drip irrigation to keep the fungus in check. [14]

The results show in the numbers: from 15 farmers to about 200 in four years. [16] Scientists at the ICAR–JSS Krishi Vigyan Kendra in Suttur have even spoken of exports with APEDA, if quality and packaging can meet the standard. [20]

07

How to find the real ones

If you want to taste or wear the genuine article, here is what the reporting suggests:

  1. Mallige: trust your nose. The real thing announces itself from a distance; a string that looks right but barely smells is likely from elsewhere. Ask the seller where it was grown. [4] [6]
  2. Vilyedele: go to the source. Udbur’s afternoon santhe is where growers sell their own leaves. In the city, ask specifically for Mysore leaf; Salem leaves are smaller and yellower. [9] [10]
  3. Rasabale: look in and around Nanjangud, where it is grown and sold; it is small, short and intensely fragrant, not a long yellow supermarket banana. [12] [14]

Every genuine purchase sends a small signal back to the farmer that the old variety is still worth growing.

08

Two fading, one fighting back

Mysuru’s name has always travelled on its things: silk, sandalwood, paintings, incense. These three were the humblest of them, the flower, the leaf and the fruit that turned up at every wedding and festival.

Their stories end differently. The jasmine is clinging on with a few families. The betel leaf has retreated from the city to a village that still loves it. And the banana, written off a few years ago, is coming back because farmers and scientists treated it as a problem to solve, not a heritage to mourn.

A GI tag tells the world where something comes from. Keeping it there is a daily job, done by the people who still grow it.
