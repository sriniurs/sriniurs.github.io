Who Is the Shocked Woman Staring From Every Half-Built House?

Drive past a construction site in Karnataka and a pair of kohl-lined eyes may stare back. She is the newest member of a very old family: faces put up to catch a jealous look before it lands on your house.

Drishti gombeFrom temple doors to a memeThe science of being watched

ABOUT 7 MINUTES

01

The face on the scaffolding

A saree. A big bindi. Eyes opened so wide they seem to have just seen your electricity bill.

On 5 January 2026, a woman from Maharashtra living in Bengaluru asked X a simple question. She kept seeing the same poster hanging from buildings under construction across Karnataka, especially outside the city, and Google Lens could not tell her who it was. Her post was viewed over 3.2 million times. [1] [2]

The replies came quickly, and most said the same thing. The poster is a nazar battu, a charm hung on a new building to keep away the evil eye. [3] In Kannada, the older version has its own name: the drishti gombe, literally a “gaze doll.” [4]

So the woman is new. The job she is doing is very, very old.

02

So whose face is it?

Here the story gets slippery. The name that went around in January came from an AI chatbot, which matched the poster to a Karnataka YouTuber. Several news sites repeated that answer. [2] [5] A later round of reports pointed out that the match had never been confirmed, and the internet stayed divided. [6]

What is on record is that the face was working long before 2026. In May 2024, a photo of the same kind of wide-eyed woman, hung over a tomato stall at a Bengaluru vegetable shop, went viral on X. People joked it was more dangerous than CCTV. [7] Some guessed the shopkeeper used it to scare off thieves; others thought it simply made curious customers stop and look. [8]

That is how a modern charm is born. Nobody issues it. A face becomes famous for being startling, printers start selling it, and it ends up tied to scaffolding from Bengaluru to small-town Karnataka.

We are not naming her. Reports disagree, and a face that guards a thousand buildings deserves a little privacy of her own.
03

Drishti: the look that is said to do harm

The belief behind all this is called drishti in the South and nazar in the North. [9] The idea: an envious or even admiring stare can bring bad luck. It is not only people who are said to be at risk; animals and even trees can “catch” drishti too. [10]

Notice who gets protected. The cutest babies are said to be the most vulnerable, which is why many get a small black dot on the cheek or forehead, a deliberate flaw to spoil the perfect picture. [11] A brand-new car gets a garland. And a half-built house, the most-looked-at object on any street, gets a face. [4] [10]

The logic is the same each time. You cannot stop people from looking, so you give their eyes something else to land on first. The charm is meant to absorb or deflect the look, which is why it must be loud, odd and impossible to miss. [4]

ZappySo the black dot is a decoy?

Prof. BoltA tiny one. The poster is the same decoy, just three floors high.

04

The old toolkit, and its upgrades

Long before posters, South Indian homes had a whole kit for the job. Each item works on the same principle, but they have very different personalities:

Ash gourdA white pumpkin hung at the door, often with a painted faceIt is said to rot quickly if an evil eye has been cast [12]

These charms travel with their people. Udupi hoteliers who moved to Mumbai took the ash gourd with them, and you can still see it hanging outside their restaurants. [12] The fierce face shows up on trucks too, sometimes painted right on the rear axle. [11]

Then came the upgrades. In 2018, a farmer in Nellore district, Andhra Pradesh, put up large posters of a film actress beside his ten acres of cabbage and cauliflower. Villagers had been eyeing his good crop, he said; now nobody looked at it. [14] Reports since then describe a farmer in Karnataka’s Yadgir district doing the same in his cotton field. [15]

The shocked-woman poster is the next step on the same road: from a demon you fear, to a celebrity you can’t stop staring at, to a face that is simply too startling to ignore.

05

The face over the temple door

Look up at the doorway of an old South Indian temple and you will often find a grinning monster with bulging eyes and fangs. It is the Kirtimukha, Sanskrit for “face of glory.” [16] Karnataka has fine examples, including one carved at the Kasivisvesvara temple in Lakkundi, Gadag district. [17]

The legend (from the Skanda and Shiva Puranas) goes like this: Shiva created a ravenous creature, and when it begged for food, told it to eat itself. At Shiva’s word it devoured its own body until only its face remained. Pleased, Shiva named it the face of glory and declared that it should always guard the door of his temples. [16] [18]

Now go west, and back about 2,500 years. Ancient Greek temples carried roof tiles bearing the face of Medusa, the monster whose stare turned people to stone. [19] Athenian potters around 530 BCE made drinking cups with a giant pair of eyes painted on the outside. [20] Scholars call such images apotropaic, from the Greek for “turning away”: an image of evil used to repel evil, much like the modern evil eye. [19]

06

Why does it have to be a face?

WHAT THE BELIEF SAYS

The charm takes the hit. A jealous look lands on the scary face instead of the house, the baby or the crop.

WHAT THE EYE ACTUALLY DOES

Our attention is pulled straight to faces, and above all to eyes looking at us. A startling face wins that first glance almost every time.

The researcher behind a famous experiment on this put it plainly: our brains are programmed to respond to eyes and faces, whether we are aware of it or not. [21]

That is why every version of the charm, from the Kirtimukha to the poster, has enormous eyes and an expression you cannot read as friendly. A calm face would not work. A pretty landscape would not work. The thing has to look at you, and look a bit alarmed while doing it.

The old explanation and the modern one agree on one point: the charm wins the glance. They disagree only on what happens next.

Prof. BoltAnd she, poor thing, never gets a day off.

07

The tea room that got honest

In 2006, researchers at Newcastle University in Britain ran a sneaky test. Their department paid for tea and coffee through an honesty box. Each week they changed a small picture above it: some weeks a pair of eyes, other weeks flowers. When the eyes were up, people paid nearly three times as much. [21] [22]

The same team then put large photos of staring eyes, with a short anti-theft message, above bicycle racks on campus. Over two years, thefts at those racks fell by 62%. The catch: thefts at racks without the posters rose by 63%, so the thieves seem to have moved, not reformed. [23]

Is it real? Partly. Several later attempts failed to repeat the “eyes make people generous” result. [24] But a 2019 review of 15 experiments with 2,035 people found that images of eyes cut antisocial behaviour, such as littering or stealing, by about 35%. [25]

None of these studies tested a drishti poster, so we can’t say it protects anything. But a building site is full of cement bags and steel left out overnight. A face that seems to be watching may, quite by accident, be doing a little of what the Newcastle posters did.

08

An old job, a new face

Strip away the meme and the shocked woman is doing exactly what the Kirtimukha has done over temple doors for centuries, and what Greek eye-cups did at dinner parties: staring back first.

Whether you believe in drishti or not, the people who put her up are making a sensible observation about human beings. We cannot resist a face, and we behave a little differently when one is watching. Our grandparents explained it with envy and bad luck; psychologists explain it with reputation and attention.

What has changed is only the face. The demon has been replaced by a woman in a saree, chosen by no priest and no committee, just by thousands of builders who found that she works.
