📰 THIS WEEK'S CLINICAL QUESTION

Should we resurface every patella in primary TKR?

The evidence has moved, but it has not settled the argument. Recent meta-analyses and large registries lean towards fewer anterior-knee-pain events and reoperations with resurfacing. Yet global function, range of motion and satisfaction are not consistently better. What should change in our next OT list?

THE PATELLA PROBLEMPart 1 · A small bone, a big argument

EP 1

Why does the patella still start arguments?

The patella is not a decorative button. It is part of the extensor mechanism and the patellofemoral articulation. A painful patella after TKR can mean cartilage disease, maltracking, impingement, altered thickness—or a problem elsewhere in the construct.

ZappyIf the femur and tibia are replaced, why leave the patella native?

Prof. BoltBecause replacing it has its own risks. The question is not simply “metal or no metal”. It is whether the patellofemoral joint will function better with the bone retained or resurfaced.

Remember

Anterior knee pain after TKR is multifactorial. Component rotation, patellar tracking, patella baja, overstuffing, instability, soft-tissue balance and implant geometry all matter. Do not label every painful knee with a native patella as a “missed resurfacing”.

EP 2

Three strategies—not two religions

1 · Resurface
Replace the articular surface with a patellar component. Potentially reduces anterior knee pain and later patellar procedures—but costs bone stock and introduces implant-related risks.

2 · Retain and perform patelloplasty
Preserve the native articular surface, remove osteophytes, contour the margins and assess tracking. Bone stock is preserved, but patellofemoral pain may persist.

3 · Selective strategy
Choose according to symptoms, patellar bone stock, cartilage, morphology, tracking, implant design and surgeon experience. It is pragmatic—but no universally validated selection rule exists.

Peripatellar denervation is often combined with either strategy. It should not be treated as a proven substitute for correcting malrotation, maltracking or patellofemoral overstuffing.

WHAT DOES THE EVIDENCE SAY?Part 2 · Recent synthesis, long-term data and guideline context

EP 3

The 2025 review: less anterior knee pain, not a new knee

Usman et al. · 20 May 2025

49 studies

Thirty-four RCTs and 15 observational studies. The database search ended on 6 February 2024, so this is a 2025 publication—not evidence collected through 2025.

Read the full open-access review ↗

OR 0.61

Resurfacing was associated with lower odds of anterior knee pain (95% CI 0.42–0.87; p=0.007). That is about 39% lower odds—not a 39-percentage-point absolute risk reduction.

What did not move convincingly?

  • No consistent difference in ROM
  • No significant difference in Oxford Knee Score
  • No consistent difference in pain scales
  • No clear global functional advantage

The review reports substantial heterogeneity for anterior knee pain (I²=70%). Its main pooled KSS-function analysis was not significant (I²=89%); a sensitivity analysis excluding one study showed a small 2.03-point advantage (95% CI 0.58–3.48; p=0.006; I²=53%). Statistical significance here does not automatically mean a patient-noticeable benefit. The abstract contains an apparent p-value inconsistency; the full-text sensitivity analysis is the source for the 2.03-point figure.

EP 4

Fifty RCTs: the network meta-analysis leans towards resurfacing

Liu et al. · 2024

9,283

Fifty randomized trials compared resurfacing, retention, and strategies combined with denervation.

Read the full open-access network meta-analysis ↗

OR 0.44

Lower postoperative reoperation odds with resurfacing versus non-resurfacing (95% CI 0.24–0.63).

OR 0.58

Lower odds of anterior knee pain (95% CI 0.32–1.00). The upper confidence limit touches 1.00: read this as borderline, not a slam dunk.

There was no significant difference across strategies in function score, ROM or patient satisfaction. Network rankings can combine direct and indirect comparisons; a top SUCRA rank is not proof that one strategy will be best for every patient.

Signal: resurfacing may reduce patella-related symptoms and reoperation. Certainty about a meaningful improvement in overall function remains much weaker.

EP 5

Registries ask a different question: who comes back for revision?

Australian registry · Coory et al.

570,735

Primary TKRs for osteoarthritis, 1999–2017. Unresurfaced knees had a higher revision rate (HR 1.31; 95% CI 1.28–1.35).

At 17 years, revision was 11.1% for unresurfaced PS knees versus 7.9% for resurfaced PS knees.

PubMed record ↗ Journal full text may require subscription.

American Joint Replacement Registry · Eiel et al., 2023

642,363

Unresurfaced patellae had higher odds of revision (OR 1.206; 95% CI 1.078–1.35), but no difference in the chance of achieving a 1-year PROM minimal clinically important difference.

Mean OT-time difference: about 1 minute 45 seconds.

PubMed record ↗ Journal full text may require subscription.

Registry strength: huge real-world numbers and revision endpoints. Registry weakness: selection bias and residual confounding. Surgeons do not randomly choose which patellae to resurface; the reason for that choice may itself predict outcome. A revision association is not the same as proof of causation.

EP 6

What does the guideline say?

AAOS guideline · 2 December 2022

Both viable

The AAOS Surgical Management of Osteoarthritis of the Knee guideline states that evidence shows no overall difference between resurfacing and non-resurfacing. It assigns high-quality evidence and a strong recommendation, while acknowledging contradictory high-quality studies.

Read the official guideline PDF ↗

Why it still matters

The guideline explicitly recognises bone-stock loss, fracture, avascular necrosis and extensor-mechanism injury as potential harms. It also notes that avoiding resurfacing may save time and cost—but a later secondary procedure has its own price.

How to reconcile it with newer reviews? The guideline predates the 2024 and 2025 syntheses. Those newer analyses suggest a signal for fewer anterior-knee-pain events and reoperations, but they still do not show a consistent, clinically important improvement in global function or satisfaction. The evidence has shifted at the margins; it has not produced a universal mandate.

INDIAN PRACTICE: WHAT CAN WE APPLY?Part 3 · Local evidence and patient selection

EP 7

Indian studies: clinically useful, statistically modest

Swaroop et al. · Odisha · 2022

68 patients

Single-centre randomized trial in Outerbridge grade IV patellae. Both groups underwent patellar denervation. Resurfacing improved stair-pain VAS at 1 and 6 months, but did not improve KSS function at 2 years.

Read full open-access trial ↗

Thilak & Mohan · Kochi · 2020

10–14 years

Retrospective series: 63 patients, 103 knees. Clinical KSS was similar; resurfaced knees had higher long-term function and Feller patellofemoral scores. Surgeon choice determined treatment, so selection bias is unavoidable.

Read full open-access study ↗

Agarwala et al. · Mumbai · 2018

60 bilateral cases

Prospective comparison of 120 knees; mean follow-up 19 months. No significant short-term difference in clinical, functional or radiographic outcomes.

Read full open-access study ↗

Indian takeaway: our published comparative evidence is still mostly small, single-centre work. The Odisha RCT is relevant to severe patellar cartilage loss, but it excluded several complex patients, was not registered in the Clinical Trials Registry of India, and had a sex imbalance between groups. Do not extrapolate it into a national rule.

EP 8

Who might benefit most from resurfacing?

Consider a lower threshold when…

  • There is convincing preoperative anterior knee or patellofemoral pain.
  • There is advanced patellofemoral cartilage loss, particularly symptomatic grade IV disease.
  • Patellar morphology and bone stock permit safe resection and fixation.
  • Tracking and component positioning can be restored reliably.

Be cautious when…

  • The patella is thin, osteopenic or structurally compromised.
  • There is a fracture history, poor bone stock or extensor-mechanism concern.
  • Resection risks over-thinning or overstuffing the patellofemoral compartment.
  • There is no clear patellofemoral indication and the native patella tracks well.

This is a practical framework, not a validated prediction score. Radiographic cartilage grade alone does not identify every patient who will develop anterior knee pain.

EP 9

If you resurface: the button is not the whole operation

1 · Measure first. Record native patellar thickness and morphology before cutting. Plan resection with the actual bone stock in mind.

2 · Resect evenly. Remove osteophytes, protect the extensor mechanism and avoid asymmetric resection or unnecessary loss of subchondral bone.

3 · Restore, don't overstuff. Match composite thickness to native anatomy. Centre the component, avoid lateral overhang and check the patellofemoral relationship.

4 · Track after the trial. Check patellar tracking through the full arc after femoral/tibial rotation and soft-tissue balance are correct. A lateral release is not a routine finishing step—use it only for persistent maltracking after correcting the underlying cause.

OT pearl: a perfectly cemented patellar button cannot compensate for a malrotated femoral component or a poorly balanced knee.

EP 10

If you retain: do not simply close and hope

Patelloplasty checklist

  • Remove marginal osteophytes completely.
  • Contour sharp edges and avoid residual impingement.
  • Assess patellar thickness and avoid creating an irregular surface.
  • Consider circumferential denervation as an adjunct, not a guarantee.
  • Check tracking with trial components through the full arc.

ZappySo retention is the “easy” option?

Prof. BoltIt saves a cut and a button. It does not excuse poor patellofemoral assessment. Retention still needs a deliberate technique.

Do not over-resect the peripheral rim or injure the tendon while denervating. Evidence for denervation is mixed; it is not a substitute for correct component rotation, patellar tracking and soft-tissue balance.

EP 11

The painful anterior knee after TKR: look beyond the patella

Do not anchor too early

  • Femoral or tibial component malrotation
  • Patella baja or altered joint line
  • Patellofemoral overstuffing or impingement
  • Residual instability or maltracking
  • Infection, loosening or referred hip/spine pain

Investigate in sequence

History + examination→Weight-bearing and skyline views→Targeted CT / infection work-up when indicated

Investigations should answer a clinical question; do not order CT rotation measurements as a reflex for every painful TKR.

Navigation and robotic assistance can improve the reproducibility of bone cuts and component positioning. They do not remove the need to assess patellar tracking, preserve bone stock and balance the soft tissues. Technology is an adjunct, not a patellofemoral diagnosis.

PROF. BOLT'S VERDICTEvidence first · Then a decision for the next list

EP 12

Resurface selectively—but do not underplay the signal OPINION

Prof. BoltMy reading: resurfacing probably lowers the chance of anterior knee pain and later patellar reoperation in aggregate. The signal is supported by recent meta-analyses and large registries. But the average patient does not reliably gain more ROM, better global function or greater satisfaction. And a poorly resurfaced patella can create a serious problem of its own.

ZappySo, routine for everyone?

Prof. BoltNo universal rule. In a symptomatic, severely worn patellofemoral joint with adequate bone stock, I would lean towards resurfacing. If the patella is thin or structurally compromised—or the patellofemoral joint is not the problem—I would preserve it and perform meticulous patelloplasty. Whichever route you choose, tracking and component rotation are non-negotiable.

The practical position

Selective resurfacing, with a low threshold when patellofemoral disease is clearly symptomatic and bone stock is adequate. This is an evidence-informed clinical position, not a proven universal algorithm. Be consistent, audit your own anterior-knee-pain and reoperation rates, and document why you chose the strategy.

NEXT OT LIST · 60-SECOND PAUSE

Before the drapes come down

1 · Pre-op

Document anterior knee symptoms, patellofemoral examination, patellar height and radiographic morphology.

2 · Intra-op

Measure bone stock. Decide resurfacing or retention deliberately. Remove osteophytes and avoid overstuffing.

3 · Trial

Check tracking through the arc after component position and soft-tissue balance are satisfactory.

4 · Audit

Record the choice and follow anterior knee pain, patellar reoperation and patient-reported function—not just radiographs.

🧠

OT Quiz · Three quick calls

Q1. In the 2025 review, what was the pooled odds ratio for anterior knee pain with resurfacing?

Reveal answer

OR 0.61 (95% CI 0.42–0.87). This is lower odds, not a 39-point absolute risk reduction.

Q2. Did the 2024 network meta-analysis find a significant difference in patient satisfaction or ROM?

Reveal answer

No. It found lower reoperation odds and a borderline signal for anterior knee pain, but no significant difference in satisfaction, ROM or function score.

Q3. In the Indian 68-patient RCT, did resurfacing improve KSS function at two years?

Reveal answer

No. Stair-pain VAS improved at 1 and 6 months; KSS functional outcomes did not differ at two years.

PIN THIS

Fridge-door cheat sheet

Evidence

  • 2025 review: lower odds of anterior knee pain (OR 0.61).
  • 2024 network meta-analysis: lower reoperation odds (OR 0.44).
  • Large registries: revision signal favours resurfacing.
  • Global function, ROM and satisfaction: no consistent advantage.
  • AAOS 2022: both strategies remain viable.

Decision

  • Symptomatic, severely worn PF joint + adequate bone stock: lean towards resurfacing.
  • Thin or compromised patella: preserve bone.
  • Selective strategy is practical, not a validated score.
  • Do not blame every painful native patella.

Execution

  • Measure before cutting.
  • Remove osteophytes.
  • Avoid over-resection and overstuffing.
  • Check tracking through the full arc.
  • Audit pain, reoperation and PROMs.
