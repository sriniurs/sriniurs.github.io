Tourniquet in TKR: Is a Bloodless Field Worth the Price?

A 2026 meta-analysis revives an old OT argument. Less visible blood, but what about DVT, pain and recovery?

Evidence, technique and innovation in arthroplasty.
5–6 minute surgical read · Conventional primary TKR

The clinical question

In an uncomplicated primary cemented TKR, should a pneumatic tourniquet be used routinely, selectively, or not at all? The answer depends on what outcome matters: operative visibility, total blood loss, early recovery, or safety.

The bloodless-field bargain

Zappy: A clean field means a better knee, surely?
Prof. Bolt: A clean field is convenient. A better patient outcome is a different endpoint.

The tourniquet can improve visibility during bone cuts and cementing, but intraoperative bleeding is not the same as total blood loss. Modern TKR also has tranexamic acid, meticulous haemostasis and controlled surgical technique.

The new evidence signal

Zappy: What changed this year?
Prof. Bolt: A larger pooled analysis made the safety question harder to ignore.

A 2026 systematic review identified 51 randomized trials. Its pooled estimate suggested higher DVT risk with tourniquet use (RR 1.60, 95% CI 1.25–2.05), without a clear benefit in total blood loss or transfusions. Importantly, most outcomes had moderate-to-high risk of bias and low-to-moderate certainty. Do not mistake a pooled signal for an individual patient's predicted risk.

What earlier RCTs showed

Zappy: Wasn't the tourniquet saving blood?
Prof. Bolt: It saves blood in the suction bottle. The whole-body ledger is less impressed.

The 2023 meta-analysis of 29 RCTs (2,512 operations) found approximately 139 mL less intraoperative blood loss and a 1.77-minute shorter operation, but no convincing difference in total blood loss, transfusion or overall complications. A 0.17-mm increase in cement mantle thickness was reported; its impact on long-term fixation remains unproven.

Why the reviews disagree

Zappy: One paper says DVT risk, another says no difference. Which wins?
Prof. Bolt: Neither by knockout. Examine definitions, power and confidence intervals.

A 2025 systematic review of 25 unique RCT datasets (3,183 patients) found no statistically clear VTE difference (RR 1.09; 95% CI 0.72–1.64). The 2026 synthesis found a larger signal. Different inclusion criteria, outcome definitions, event counts and perioperative thromboprophylaxis can shift pooled results. VTE is uncommon enough that individual trials are often underpowered; symptomatic DVT and screening-detected DVT should not be treated as equivalent.

Pain and quadriceps recovery

Zappy: Can a thigh cuff affect early mobilisation?
Prof. Bolt: It can. But don't claim a permanent functional penalty.

The 2023 review reported more early pain, swelling and temporary ROM/function disadvantages with tourniquets at selected time points. The 2026 review describes early adverse effects, although some pooled time-point comparisons were not significant. Most evidence concerns early recovery rather than durable patient-reported differences at years of follow-up.

Cement fixation: the counterargument

Zappy: What about a dry tibial bed?
Prof. Bolt: Good cement technique matters. Tourniquet superiority in implant survival is not established.

A bloodless field may make cementation easier, especially in technically challenging cases. However, radiographic mantle differences are surrogate endpoints, not proof of lower aseptic loosening or revision at 10 years. Pulsatile lavage, drying, appropriate cement pressurisation and component positioning remain essential regardless of cuff use.

Tranexamic acid changes the equation

Zappy: Does TXA make the cuff redundant?
Prof. Bolt: It reduces the need to use a cuff solely as a blood-conservation strategy.

The 2024 TXA-era review (19 studies; 1,094 patients) still found less intraoperative bleeding with a cuff but no significant reduction in total blood loss. Its search extended only through April 2017 despite later publication, an important recency limitation. TXA dosing and route should follow local protocols and individual contraindications.

India: the practical context

Zappy: What about our busy Indian OT lists?
Prof. Bolt: Think patient, resources and reproducible technique—not dogma.

Many Indian units balance cost, variable anaemia prevalence, rapid mobilisation, day-one physiotherapy and resource constraints. A selective-cuff protocol is practical, but no robust India-specific randomized dataset was identified here that establishes a different optimal strategy. Do not extrapolate western VTE rates uncritically; maintain locally appropriate thromboprophylaxis and monitor outcomes.

A sensible conventional-TKR workflow

Zappy: So should we abandon the cuff tomorrow?
Prof. Bolt: No. Change deliberately, document carefully.

For a routine primary TKR, discuss cuff-free surgery or inflation only for cementation where visibility is a concern. Confirm haemostasis before closure, use a standardized TXA pathway when appropriate, and document cuff pressure and inflation time. Reserve discretion for severe deformity, complex exposure, bleeding concerns and vascular contraindications; there is no one-size-fits-all protocol.

What robotics does—and doesn't—change

Zappy: Does a robot settle this?
Prof. Bolt: A robot cannot repeal tissue physiology.

Navigation and robotic workflows may change exposure, operative time and the need for visualisation, but tourniquet decisions still hinge on patient risk, haemostasis and technique. This issue makes no claim that robotics reduces cuff-related complications; direct comparative evidence is insufficient for that conclusion.

Evidence vs interpretation

What we can say—and what we cannot

Evidence: Tourniquets consistently reduce measured intraoperative bleeding, but pooled trials do not show a reliable transfusion advantage. Early pain/swelling and possible thrombotic harm are concerns, with inconsistent estimates across reviews.

Editorial interpretation: Routine full-duration inflation deserves reconsideration; selective or no-tourniquet protocols are reasonable in experienced hands. This is not proof that a tourniquet must never be used.

Uncertainty: Variation in cuff pressure/duration, TXA use, VTE prophylaxis, DVT screening, blinding and outcome follow-up limits certainty. Long-term implant survivorship remains insufficiently addressed.

Fridge-door cheat sheet

Before the next TKR

  1. Check patient-specific VTE and vascular risk; follow established prophylaxis.
  2. Agree with anaesthesia on TXA and blood management.
  3. Decide cuff-free, cementation-only, or full-duration use before incision.
  4. If inflating, document pressure and time; avoid unnecessary prolonged inflation.
  5. Dry and lavage bone; pressurise cement meticulously.
  6. Secure haemostasis and monitor swelling, pain, mobilisation and complications.
OT quiz

Three questions for the scrub-room debate

1. Does reduced intraoperative bleeding prove fewer transfusions?

No. Multiple pooled reviews found no clear transfusion advantage.

2. Is the 2026 DVT risk ratio definitive for every patient?

No. RR 1.60 is a pooled estimate; bias, event ascertainment and prophylaxis heterogeneity matter.

3. Does a thicker cement mantle prove better 10-year survival?

No. It is a surrogate measure, not direct revision or survivorship evidence.

Prof. Bolt's next-OT verdict

Do not inflate by reflex. For the next routine primary TKR, make the tourniquet an explicit decision. If using it, minimise unnecessary duration, record cuff details and keep meticulous blood management. Audit early mobilisation, swelling, transfusion and symptomatic VTE across your own cases.
