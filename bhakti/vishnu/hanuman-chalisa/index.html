WHERE WE ARE

The Hanumān Cālīsā is the most recited hymn in North India and, today, across the world. Its name means simply “the forty”: forty four-line verses (caupāīs) in Awadhī, framed by two dohās at the start and one at the end, in all 43 verses. Tradition gives it to Tulasīdāsa (c. 1532–1623), the poet of the Rāmacaritamānas, whose name is in its last caupāī.

It moves in a clear arc: an invocation; a portrait of Hanumān; his deeds in the Rāmāyaṇa, from the leap at the sun to the herb that saved Lakṣmaṇa; what he does for those who call on him; and a closing blessing. It takes about ten minutes to chant.

THE DUST OF THE GURU’S FEET
śrīguru carana saroja rajanija manu mukuru sudhāribaranaūṃ raghubara bimala jasujo dāyaku phala cāri ‖dohā‖

DOHĀ 1 · DEEP DIVE

THE DUST OF THE GURU’S FEET

🔎 In context

This opening dohā is not Tulasīdāsa’s own composition for the Cālīsā: it is the very first dohā of the Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa of his Rāmacaritamānas. Tradition placed it here so that the hymn to Hanumān begins, as all his work does, with the guru and with Rāma. A dohā is a two-line couplet of 13 and 11 beats in each half; the Cālīsā’s forty verses are caupāīs, quatrains in a 16-beat metre.

📜 Looking deeper

The image of the mind as a mirror is old in Indian thought: a dusty mirror shows nothing clearly, however bright the light. The surprise is the cleaning cloth: not effort or learning but the dust of the teacher’s feet, the humblest thing imaginable. Many read “guru” here as Hanumān himself, the teacher who leads the devotee to Rāma; others as one’s own human teacher. The four fruits are dharma, artha, kāma and mokṣa: right living, wealth, fulfilled desire and freedom.

🌱 For today

Before you start anything hard, clean the mirror: drop the noise and the pride, and look to the people who taught you.

↑ DOHĀ 1 · Clean the mirror of the mind first.

DOHĀ 2

GIVE ME STRENGTH, WISDOM, KNOWLEDGE
buddhihīna tanu jānikesumirauṃ pavana-kumārabala budhi bidyā dehu mohiṃharahu kalesa bikāra ‖dohā‖
Knowing this body to be without wisdom, I remember you, son of the Wind. Give me strength, wisdom and knowledge, and take away my troubles and my faults.
WordMeans
buddhihīna tanu jānikeknowing myself to be without wisdom
sumirauṃ pavana-kumāraI remember the Wind’s son
bala budhi bidyā dehu mohiṃgive me strength, intellect, learning

DOHĀ 2 · DEEP DIVE

GIVE ME STRENGTH, WISDOM, KNOWLEDGE

🔎 In context

This dohā turns from Rāma to Hanumān and states the request of the whole hymn. Together with the first dohā it forms the standard opening in printed editions such as Gītā Press. Many families begin chanting from this line alone.

📜 Looking deeper

The confession comes before the request: “buddhihīna”, I am without understanding. Only one who admits a lack can receive. The three gifts are chosen with care: bala, strength of body; budhi, sharp judgement; bidyā, learning. Hanumān has all three in the Rāmāyaṇa, and Rāma himself praised his learning before his strength. Kalesa (kleśa) is pain, and in Yoga philosophy the five root afflictions; bikāra (vikāra) means distortions of the mind such as anger, greed and fear.

🌱 For today

Ask for all three: a strong body, a clear head and real knowledge. Any one without the others goes wrong.

↑ DOHĀ 2 · Strength, wisdom, learning: ask for all three.

CAUPĀĪ 1 · DEEP DIVE

OCEAN OF WISDOM

🔎 In context

The forty caupāīs begin here, and this is the line almost every Hindu child knows. “Cālīsā” simply means “a forty”, a hymn of forty verses; the form was common in North Indian devotion, and there are Cālīsās to Śiva, Durgā, Gaṇeśa and many others, most modelled on this one.

📜 Looking deeper

The opening praises wisdom before strength, as Vālmīki did. Kapīśa, lord of the vānaras, is a title of honour: Sugrīva was king, but Hanumān is lord in the sense of the greatest. The three worlds are earth, sky and heaven, or in later reading the waking, dreaming and deep-sleep states. Ujāgara means one who makes things bright and famous: Hanumān’s deeds light up every world where they are told.

🌱 For today

Start with praise. Saying what is good in someone, even God, changes the one who says it.

↑ CAUPĀĪ 1 · Ocean of wisdom, light of the three worlds.

CAUPĀĪ 2 · DEEP DIVE

RĀMA’S MESSENGER

🔎 In context

Rāmadūta is the title Hanumān gives himself in Laṅkā: when Rāvaṇa asks who he is, he answers “I am the messenger of Rāma”. Every other name follows from that one. The verse also gives his two parents, Añjanā and the Wind, matching Vālmīki.

📜 Looking deeper

Atulita means unweighable, beyond comparison: in the Sundara Kāṇḍa no rākṣasa, not even Rāvaṇa’s son Akṣa, can match him. But the verse puts the title of messenger first, before strength. A messenger has no agenda of his own; his greatness lies in carrying another’s word faithfully. That is the Cālīsā’s picture of Hanumān: a mighty being whose identity is service.

🌱 For today

Whatever your strength, ask whose message you carry. The answer gives the strength its point.

↑ CAUPĀĪ 2 · First Rāma’s messenger, then the strong one.

CAUPĀĪ 3 · DEEP DIVE

THE HERO WITH LIMBS OF THUNDER

🔎 In context

Bajaraṅgī (vajrāṅgī) gave rise to the popular name Bajrangbalī, “the strong one with thunderbolt limbs”. It recalls Indra’s vajra that struck the infant Hanumān and could not harm him, and the boon that it never would.

📜 Looking deeper

The second line turns from outer to inner battle. Kumati, a twisted mind, is the real enemy for most of us; sumati, good understanding, is the friend. Tulasīdāsa says elsewhere in the Rāmacaritamānas that where sumati lives, prosperity follows, and where kumati lives, disaster. Hanumān’s strength is thus pointed inwards: the same force that crushed rākṣasas can crush our worst impulses.

🌱 For today

Notice the bad thought before you act on it. That pause is where Hanumān’s help begins.

↑ CAUPĀĪ 3 · He drives off bad thinking.

CAUPĀĪ 4 · DEEP DIVE

GOLDEN AND RADIANT

🔎 In context

Here begins a short dhyāna, a description to hold in the mind while chanting. Many hymns have one: before worship, the devotee pictures the deity in detail. This verse and the next give Hanumān’s form.

📜 Looking deeper

The golden colour matches Vālmīki, who often compares Hanumān to gold, to the rising sun, or to a mountain of gold such as Meru when he grows huge. Sindūr has made most temple images orange, but the texts see him as golden-tawny. Earrings were a mark of nobility and learning; curly hair, a mark of beauty. The picture is of a prince, not a wild animal, which matches the Rāmāyaṇa’s view of the vānaras as a noble people.

🌱 For today

Picture what you pray to. A clear image steadies the mind.

↑ CAUPĀĪ 4 · A golden prince of the forest.

CAUPĀĪ 5 · DEEP DIVE

MACE, FLAG AND SACRED THREAD

🔎 In context

Bajra here is usually read as the mace or a thunderbolt-like weapon, and the flag as a banner of victory, perhaps Rāma’s own. In many North Indian shrines Hanumān holds both, and saffron flags fly over every Hanumān temple.

📜 Looking deeper

The muñja-grass thread is a detail that tells a lot. The janeū, sacred thread, marks a student who has been initiated into Vedic learning; muñja grass is the material prescribed for the girdle of a brahmacārī in the Gṛhya Sūtras. So the verse quietly calls Hanumān a lifelong celibate student, a scholar as well as a warrior. Some ask how a vānara wears a sacred thread; the devotional answer is that learning and devotion, not birth, make the twice-born.

🌱 For today

Carry both: the weapon for the fight and the flag for what you fight for.

↑ CAUPĀĪ 5 · Warrior’s mace, student’s thread.

CAUPĀĪ 6 · DEEP DIVE

ŚIVA’S OWN, KESARĪ’S JOY

🔎 In context

Some editions read “saṅkara svayaṃ”, Śiva himself, instead of “saṅkara suvana”, Śiva’s son. Either way the line brings in the Śaiva tradition that Hanumān is Śiva’s portion, born to serve Rāma, and puts it beside his family name: joy of Kesarī.

📜 Looking deeper

Tulasīdāsa was a great bridge-builder between Śaivas and Vaiṣṇavas. In his Rāmacaritamānas, Śiva tells the Rāma story to Pārvatī, and Rāma worships Śiva at Rāmeśvaram. By calling Hanumān Śiva’s own, he makes Hanumān the place where both traditions meet: Śiva serving Viṣṇu. Teja is inner fire or brilliance; pratāpa is the power that makes others bow; bandana (vandana) means the salutation of all the world.

🌱 For today

Great people often belong to more than one family. Let them.

↑ CAUPĀĪ 6 · Śiva’s own, in Rāma’s service.

CAUPĀĪ 7 · DEEP DIVE

LEARNED, GOOD AND QUICK

🔎 In context

This verse moves from his form to his character. It echoes Vālmīki’s Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa, where Rāma marvels at Hanumān’s learning after hearing him speak once.

📜 Looking deeper

Ātura is a strong word: impatient, almost restless. Hanumān does not wait to be told twice; in the Sundara Kāṇḍa he refuses even to rest on the mountain Maināka. Cātura, clever, is shown again and again: his choice of ordinary speech for Sītā, his trick with Surasā, his decision to let Indrajit bind him so he could see Rāvaṇa. Learning, goodness and cleverness are all put to one use, Rāma’s work. Devotees say that is what turns talent into virtue.

🌱 For today

Use your gifts for something worth doing, and do it without being asked twice.

↑ CAUPĀĪ 7 · Always eager for Rāma’s work.

CAUPĀĪ 8 · DEEP DIVE

IN LOVE WITH THE LORD’S STORY

🔎 In context

This verse is the root of the belief that Hanumān is present wherever the Rāmāyaṇa is told. A rasiyā is a connoisseur, someone who savours: Hanumān never tires of hearing what he himself witnessed.

📜 Looking deeper

Hearing (śravaṇa) is the first of the nine forms of devotion listed in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, and Hanumān is often named its great example. The second line anticipates the later story of the torn chest: Rāma, Lakṣmaṇa and Sītā live inside him. Tulasīdāsa’s own life fits the verse: by tradition he found Hanumān at a Rāmāyaṇa reading in Vārāṇasī, the first to come and the last to leave.

🌱 For today

What you love to hear about, you become. Choose your stories.

↑ CAUPĀĪ 8 · He never tires of Rāma’s story.

CAUPĀĪ 9 · DEEP DIVE

TINY FOR SĪTĀ, HUGE FOR LAṄKĀ

🔎 In context

Now the hymn turns to his deeds, retelling the Sundara Kāṇḍa in two lines. Both forms come from Vālmīki: Hanumān appears small to Sītā so as not to frighten her, and grows vast when he burns the city.

📜 Looking deeper

The pairing is the point. The same being who can shrink to a whisper for a frightened woman can grow terrible for a city of tyrants. Power that knows when to be small is the rarest kind. Commentators also read sūkṣma as subtle in the spiritual sense: to reach Sītā, who stands for the soul or for Śakti, one must become subtle and humble; to burn the city of ego, one must be fierce.

🌱 For today

Be gentle with the frightened and firm with the bully. Know which moment is which.

↑ CAUPĀĪ 9 · Small for Sītā, huge for Laṅkā.

📜 Looking deeper

Some traditions extend his presence to the first age too, as Śiva’s portion or as Vāyu’s power since creation. The verse prepares for the next ones: if he is present in every age, he is present now, and the devotee of today can call on him as directly as Rāma did. Ujiyārā, light, recalls the first caupāī’s ujāgara: the hymn keeps returning to Hanumān as a light.

🌱 For today

Some help never goes out of date. Courage and loyalty are among them.

↑ CAUPĀĪ 29 · His glory spans all four ages.

CAUPĀĪ 30 · DEEP DIVE

GUARDIAN OF THE GOOD

🔎 In context

This verse echoes the Gītā’s promise of the Lord’s descent: to protect the good and destroy the wicked (4.8). Hanumān carries out that very task for Rāma.

📜 Looking deeper

Dulāre is a tender, everyday word, used for a much-loved child. After the grand titles of messenger, hero and gatekeeper, Tulasīdāsa calls Hanumān Rāma’s pet, his darling. The contrast is deliberate: terrible to demons, tender to Rāma. The sādhus and santas he guards include, by tradition, Tulasīdāsa himself, Samartha Rāmadāsa and Rāghavendra Tīrtha, saints who credited Hanumān with their protection.

🌱 For today

Be fierce in defending good people, and gentle with those you love.

↑ CAUPĀĪ 30 · Guard of the good, Rāma’s darling.

CAUPĀĪ 31 · DEEP DIVE

SĪTĀ’S BOON

🔎 In context

In the Rāmacaritamānas, Sītā in the Aśoka grove blesses Hanumān: may he be ageless, immortal, full of virtue and dear to Rāma. Later tradition extends the blessing: he may give the siddhis and nidhis to others.

📜 Looking deeper

The eight siddhis of yoga are aṇimā (becoming tiny), mahimā (becoming huge), garimā (heavy), laghimā (light), prāpti (reaching anything), prākāmya (getting what one wishes), īśitva (lordship) and vaśitva (control). Hanumān shows several in the Rāmāyaṇa: tiny for Sītā, huge for the leap, too heavy for Bhīma to lift his tail. The nine nidhis are Kubera’s treasures, such as padma and mahāpadma. Note the giver: not Rāma but Sītā, Jānakī, “daughter of Janaka”, called Mother.

🌱 For today

The best gifts come from people we have helped in their darkest hour.

↑ CAUPĀĪ 31 · Sītā’s blessing: powers to give.

CAUPĀĪ 32 · DEEP DIVE

THE ELIXIR OF RĀMA

🔎 In context

Rasāyana is an Āyurvedic term: a rejuvenating tonic that restores youth and long life. Tulasīdāsa uses it for Rāma’s name: the medicine that heals the soul.

📜 Looking deeper

The second line can be read two ways. As a blessing: “may you always be Rāma’s servant”, what Hanumān wants most. Or as a description: “you are always Rāma’s servant”. Either way, the verse links the healer of verse 27 with this deeper medicine: the herb saved Lakṣmaṇa’s body, the name saves the self. Many sādhus call japa of Rāma’s name rāma-rasāyana, a lifelong tonic.

🌱 For today

Find the thing that renews you, and keep it close.

CAUPĀĪ 33 · DEEP DIVE

THROUGH YOU, RĀMA

🔎 In context

This is the heart of Hanumān’s place in theology: he is not an end but a way. Worshipping him leads to Rāma, just as meeting him led Sugrīva and Vibhīṣaṇa to Rāma.

📜 Looking deeper

Bhajana means worship, especially devotional singing. The second line speaks of rebirth: sorrows accumulated over many lives are not paid off one by one but forgotten, dissolved. In Vaiṣṇava teaching this is grace again: the soul is freed not by the arithmetic of karma but by the Lord’s love. The guru-like role of Hanumān returns: the teacher’s worth is the place he leads to.

🌱 For today

Good teachers point beyond themselves. Follow where they point.

↑ CAUPĀĪ 33 · Through him, to Rāma.

CAUPĀĪ 34 · DEEP DIVE

AT LIFE’S END

🔎 In context

The verse speaks of death, and of what follows. Raghubara pura, Rāma’s city, is Sāketa, the heavenly Ayodhyā of Vaiṣṇava theology.

📜 Looking deeper

The second line covers both possibilities: if the devotee is not freed at once, they will be born again as a devotee, keeping their love across lives. The Gītā says something similar of the yogī who falls short (6.41–43): he is born in a good family and takes up the path again. For many families this verse is chanted at the bedside of the dying, as a comfort that the journey continues in good hands.

🌱 For today

Live so that the way you are remembered is the way you would want to begin again.

↑ CAUPĀĪ 34 · At the end, Rāma’s city.

CAUPĀĪ 35

CAUPĀĪ 35 · DEEP DIVE

NO OTHER GOD NEEDED

🔎 In context

This verse is sometimes misread as dismissing other gods. In context it is the language of devotion, which always speaks of its beloved as enough.

📜 Looking deeper

Tulasīdāsa honoured Śiva, Gaṇeśa, Durgā and many others in his works; he was not exclusive. The verse means that devotion to Hanumān alone is sufficient: one need not run from temple to temple. Indian devotion calls this ekānta bhakti, one-pointed love, and it does not deny that other doors exist. Some editions read “citta na dharaī” as “need not worry”, giving the same sense.

🌱 For today

Depth beats breadth. One faithful practice does more than many scattered ones.

↑ CAUPĀĪ 35 · He alone is enough.

CAUPĀĪ 36 · DEEP DIVE

TROUBLES CUT AWAY

🔎 In context

The promise of verses 27 and 28 returns, framing the last section of the hymn. Repetition is part of the Cālīsā’s design: it is meant to be chanted, and the refrain-like returns steady the chanter.

📜 Looking deeper

Sumirai (smaraṇa) is remembrance, the gentlest form of devotion: not ritual, not even chanting aloud, just keeping him in mind. Smaraṇa is the third of the nine forms of bhakti. The verse ends the list of benefits with the simplest condition of all. Balabīrā joins bala, strength, and bīra (vīra), hero: the strong one who protects.

🌱 For today

Remember who you can call on. Half of courage is memory.

↑ CAUPĀĪ 36 · Remember him and troubles fall away.

CAUPĀĪ 37 · DEEP DIVE

BE KIND, LIKE A GURU

🔎 In context

The triple jai marks the start of the closing section, the phalaśruti, which tells the benefits of reciting the hymn. Gosāīṃ (gosvāmī), “master of the senses”, is a title of honour; Tulasīdāsa himself is often called Gosvāmī Tulasīdāsa.

📜 Looking deeper

The hymn began with the guru’s feet; it ends asking Hanumān to be the guru. The request is not for favours but for the kind of grace a teacher gives: correction, patience and guidance, not just gifts. In the guru tradition, the teacher’s kṛpā is the one thing that cannot be earned. Asking Hanumān to be that teacher completes the circle the first dohā opened.

🌱 For today

Find someone who will teach you, not just please you. Then listen.

CAUPĀĪ 38 · DEEP DIVE

A HUNDRED RECITATIONS

🔎 In context

A hundred recitations is a traditional vow. Some do it in a single day, some as 108 for the rosary’s count, and some read “sata” as “sat”, truly, meaning “whoever recites it truly”.

📜 Looking deeper

Bandi means bondage: literally prison, and some traditions say a prisoner chanting it was freed. Spiritually it is the bondage of karma and saṃsāra. A popular story tells that Tulasīdāsa, jailed by a ruler, composed or recited the Cālīsā and that an army of vānaras besieged the jail until he was freed; this is legend, but it shows how the verse was understood. Both readings of “sata”, a hundred or truly, point to commitment.

🌱 For today

Repeat what matters until it changes you. Habit is a kind of prayer.

ŚIVA IS WITNESS

🔎 In context

The only place the hymn names itself. Gaurīśa, “lord of Gaurī (Pārvatī)”, is Śiva, called to witness the promise. In Tulasīdāsa’s Rāmacaritamānas, Śiva is the first narrator of the Rāma story, so he is the natural witness.

📜 Looking deeper

Calling Śiva as witness also closes the loop with verse 8, “Śaṅkara suvana”: if Hanumān is Śiva’s own, Śiva vouches for him. A witness (sākṣī) in Indian law made a promise binding. Siddhi here means success or fulfilment in general, not just the yogic powers of verse 33.

🌱 For today

Make your promises in front of someone who will hold you to them.

CAUPĀĪ 40 · DEEP DIVE

LIVE IN MY HEART

🔎 In context

Indian poets sign their work in the last verse, a convention called the bhaṇitā or chāp. Tulasīdāsa signs as Hari’s cerā, a humble word for a servant or disciple. This signature is the main basis for attributing the hymn to him.

📜 Looking deeper

Some scholars note that the Cālīsā does not appear in early manuscript lists of Tulasīdāsa’s major works and question whether he wrote it; tradition holds firmly that he did. Either way the request is the same: “make your ḍerā, your camp, your home, in my heart.” A ḍerā is a simple dwelling, a tent: the poet asks for nothing grand, only that Hanumān stay.

🌱 For today

Ask for presence, not presents.

THE CLOSING PRAYER

🔎 In context

The closing dohā mirrors the opening two. It is usually followed in recitation by shouts of “bolo siyāvara rāmacandra kī jaya, pavanasuta hanumāna kī jaya”, and then an ārati.

📜 Looking deeper

The hymn ends where verse 10 placed Rāma, Lakṣmaṇa and Sītā: in a heart. But now it is the devotee’s heart, not Hanumān’s. The request is that Hanumān come in and bring his household with him, the reverse of the torn-chest image. Sura bhūpa, king of the gods, is the highest title in the hymn, given at the very end. Maṅgala mūrati, the form of auspiciousness, is how many temples greet him.

🌱 For today

Let the people you admire live in you. Their courage becomes yours.

THE HYMN ON ONE PAGE

The Cālīsā in five lines
Invocation (dohās 1–2). Clean the mirror of the mind; ask for strength, wisdom and learning.
Who he is (1–8). Ocean of wisdom, Rāma’s messenger, thunder-limbed hero, golden, Śiva’s own, eager to serve, lover of Rāma’s story.
What he did (9–19). Small for Sītā, huge for Laṅkā; the herb for Lakṣmaṇa; Rāma’s embrace; Sugrīva and Vibhīṣaṇa made kings; the sun; the ring and the sea.
What he gives (20–36). Hard things made easy; guard of Rāma’s door; no fear, no ghosts, less pain; freedom from trouble; siddhis by Sītā’s boon; the way to Rāma.
Blessing (37–40, closing dohā). Be my guru; recite and be free; Śiva is witness; live in my heart with Rāma, Lakṣmaṇa and Sītā.
