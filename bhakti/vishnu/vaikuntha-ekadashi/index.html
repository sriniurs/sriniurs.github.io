VAIKUṆṬHA EKĀDAŚĪ

FESTIVALS · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The day the gate of Vaikuṇṭha opens · 20 December 2026

FOREWORD

Once a year, before dawn, a gate that stays shut all year is opened on the north side of every great Viṣṇu temple. Pilgrims who have waited all night walk through it into the Lord’s presence. This is Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī, the day the gate of Vaikuṇṭha opens.

In 2026 it falls on Sunday 20 December, and it is also Gītā Jayantī, the day the Bhagavad Gītā was spoken. This guide explains the day: what Ekādaśī is, why this one is special, how Srirangam and Tirumala keep it, how to fast and break the fast, and the stories behind it.

ZAPPY: Why is this Ekādaśī so special?

PROF. BOLT: Of 24 Ekādaśīs in a year, this is the one when Vaikuṇṭha's gate is opened.

Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī is the biggest single day in the Viṣṇu temple year in South India. Millions queue before dawn at Tirumala, Srirangam and smaller temples to walk through a gate that is opened only now.

In 2026 it coincides with Mokṣadā Ekādaśī of the northern calendar and with Gītā Jayantī, so the whole country marks the same day in different ways: fasting, darśan through the gate, and reading the Gītā.

The names show how one day is shared across regions: Mukkoṭi in Telugu and Kannada homes, Svargavāsal in Tamil Nadu and Kerala, Mokṣadā in the Hindi belt and in ISKCON calendars. What unites them is the idea of liberation, mokṣa, made present for a day.

WHAT IS EKĀDAŚĪ?

The eleventh day
TithiThe eleventh lunar day of each fortnight: two a month, about 24 a year
Viṣṇu’s dayEach Ekādaśī is sacred to Viṣṇu, with its own name and story
FastGrains, especially rice, are avoided; many fast fully
The goddessThe Padma Purāṇa tells of a goddess born from Viṣṇu who killed the demon Mura: Viṣṇu named her Ekādaśī
PāraṇaThe fast is broken on Dvādaśī, the twelfth day, within a set window

ZAPPY: A goddess called Ekādaśī?

PROF. BOLT: Born from Viṣṇu's body while he slept. She saved him, and he made her day holy.

Ekādaśī is the most widely kept fast in Vaiṣṇava tradition. Each of the year’s Ekādaśīs has a name, such as Nirjalā, Devaśayanī, Prabodhinī and Mokṣadā, and a story in the Ekādaśī māhātmyas.

The Mura story gives the fast its origin: while Viṣṇu rested in a cave, a radiant goddess sprang from his body and destroyed the demon Mura. Viṣṇu named her Ekādaśī and promised that those who fast on her day would be freed from sin.

Ekādaśī is also kept by Śaiva and Smārta families, and by many who keep no other fast. Its popularity owes much to the Vaiṣṇava saints: the Haridāsas of Karnataka, the Vārkarīs of Maharashtra walking to Paṇḍharpur on Āṣāḍhī Ekādaśī, and the Gauḍīya tradition, which made strict Ekādaśī a mark of devotion.

THE BEST OF MONTHS

Mārgaśīrṣa and Mārgaḻi
Gītā 10.35Kṛṣṇa says: of months, I am Mārgaśīrṣa
Dhanur māsaIn the solar calendar this is Dhanus, Mārgaḻi in Tamil, the month of dawn worship
The gods’ dawnTradition: the six months of the sun’s southward journey are the gods’ night; Mārgaḻi is their dawn
Two calendarsVaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī is set by the Mārgaḻi month in the Tamil tradition, and by lunar Mārgaśīrṣa elsewhere; usually the same day

bṛhatsāma tathā sāmnāṃ gāyatrī chandasām aham
māsānāṃ mārgaśīrṣo ’ham ṛtūnāṃ kusumākaraḥ

Of chants I am the Bṛhat Sāman, of metres the Gāyatrī; of months I am Mārgaśīrṣa, of seasons the flowering spring. (Gītā 10.35)

The solar month of Dhanus, Mārgaḻi in Tamil, runs from mid-December to mid-January. It is considered the dawn of the gods’ day, the brahma muhūrta of the year, and is given over to early-morning worship.

Kṛṣṇa’s words in the Gītā, “of months I am Mārgaśīrṣa”, give the month special sanctity. Mārgaśīrṣa may once have begun the year, which may be why it is called the best.

In Karnataka and Andhra, Dhanurmāsa is also a month when weddings and house-warmings are traditionally avoided, since the month is given to God. Rangoli at dawn, temple bells before sunrise and the smell of hot pongal define it in many towns.

THE NORTHERN GATE

Vaikuṇṭha dvāra
WhatA gate on the north side of the temple, kept shut all year
OpenedBefore dawn on Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī
MeaningPassing through it is passing through the gate of Vaikuṇṭha, Viṣṇu’s heaven
NamesParamapada vāsal (Srirangam), Svargavāsal, Vaikuṇṭha dvāram (Tirumala)
CrowdsDevotees queue through the night for this one passage

ZAPPY: Walking through a door gets you to heaven?

PROF. BOLT: It's a picture of what the day means: the gate of liberation opens.

The Vaikuṇṭha dvāra is a symbol made physical. Viṣṇu temples are built as images of Vaikuṇṭha, and the northern gate stands for the passage from this world to his abode. Walking through it on this one day is taken as a foretaste of liberation.

Many smaller temples set up a decorated temporary gate on the north side for the day so that devotees everywhere can take part.

In Śrīvaiṣṇava thought, the gate also recalls a famous verse of Nammāḻvār describing the welcome the soul receives in Vaikuṇṭha: the gods line the way and the doors are thrown open. The temple rite stages that welcome for the living.

WHY THE GATE OPENS

The story told in temples
Madhu and KaiṭabhaA popular South Indian telling: two asuras, defeated by Viṣṇu, asked that whoever saw him through a gate on this day reach Vaikuṇṭha
GrantedViṣṇu agreed; so the northern gate opens on this day
MukkoṭiAnother name: the “three crore” gods come to Vaikuṇṭha to see Viṣṇu on this day
SourceThese are temple and regional traditions; Purāṇic support is indirect

The Madhu–Kaiṭabha telling is common in South Indian temple lore: the two asuras born from Viṣṇu’s ear during the cosmic sleep fought him, were defeated, and asked for a boon, that the gate of Vaikuṇṭha should open for all who come through it on this day.

The name Mukkoṭi, “three crores”, refers to the traditional 33 crore gods, who are said to throng to Vaikuṇṭha to see Viṣṇu on this day; devotees on earth join them.

Other tellings link the day to the end of Viṣṇu’s cosmic sleep or to the gods’ dawn. Whatever the origin story, the practice is ancient in Tamil temples; the opening of the Paramapada Vāsal at Srirangam is described in temple chronicles centuries old.

SRIRANGAM

The greatest celebration
Twenty-one daysThe Adhyayana Utsavam: ten days of Pagal Pattu (day) and ten of Rāppattu (night), around the Ekādaśī
HymnsThe 4,000 verses of the Āḻvārs (Divya Prabandham) are recited before Raṅganātha
MohinīOn the day before, Namperumāḷ (the festival image) appears as Mohinī
The gateBefore dawn on the Ekādaśī, Raṅganātha passes through the Paramapada Vāsal, and devotees follow
Nammāḻvār’s mokṣaOn the last day, the poet-saint Nammāḻvār’s liberation is enacted

ZAPPY: Twenty-one days for one day?

PROF. BOLT: The day is the peak. The festival is the mountain.

See: Triraṅga: Srirangam

The Adhyayana Utsavam at Srirangam is one of the great festivals of South India. Over twenty-one days the Nālāyira Divya Prabandham, the four thousand Tamil verses of the Āḻvārs, is recited in order before Raṅganātha, with the Tiruvāymoḻi of Nammāḻvār at its heart during the Rāppattu nights.

The opening of the Paramapada Vāsal before dawn on the Ekādaśī, with Namperumāḷ carried through it in procession, draws enormous crowds. The festival closes with the enactment of Nammāḻvār’s attaining of Vaikuṇṭha.

The Pagal Pattu days cover the first thousand verses (Periyāḻvār, Āṇḍāḷ, Kulaśekhara and others) and the Rāppattu nights the Tiruvāymoḻi. Each day Namperumāḷ is carried in a different splendid adornment, and pilgrims from across the South plan the year around it.

THE ARAIYAR

Dance and song for the Lord
Araiyar sevaiA hereditary art of singing and enacting the Āḻvārs’ hymns before the deity
WhereSrirangam, Śrīvilliputtūr and Āḻvār Tirunagari
WhenDuring the Adhyayana Utsavam
NāthamuniTradition credits the 10th-century ācārya Nāthamuni with collecting the hymns and starting the festival

Araiyar sevai is a rare living art: the Araiyars sing and enact the Āḻvārs’ hymns in a mix of music, gesture and commentary, wearing tall caps, before the deity. It survives in only a few temples.

Tradition tells that Nāthamuni, the first great Śrīvaiṣṇava ācārya, recovered the lost hymns of Nammāḻvār and set them to music, and that the Srirangam festival grew from his work.

TIRUMALA

The Vaikuṇṭha Dvāram
The passageA passage around the sanctum, normally shut, opened for darśan
Ten daysTTD now keeps it open for ten days from Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī, so more pilgrims can pass through
DvādaśīOn the next day, the Sudarśana Cakra is bathed in the Svāmi Puṣkariṇī tank
MukkoṭiThe Telugu name of the day
BhadrācalamSītā Rāmacandra Svāmī’s gate opening is the other great Telugu event

At Tirumala the Vaikuṇṭha Dvāram is a passage that circles the sanctum. In recent years TTD has kept it open for ten days so that more pilgrims can pass through, issuing tokens in advance.

On Dvādaśī, the Cakrasnānam bathes the Sudarśana Cakra in the temple tank. At Bhadrācalam in Telangana, Rāma’s temple holds a famous gate-opening, the Uttara Dvāra Darśanam.

Tirumala’s Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī crowds are among the largest single-day gatherings at any temple. Advance tokens, special queue lines and the ten-day opening were introduced to manage them; the details change from year to year, so pilgrims check TTD’s announcements.

IN KARNATAKA

Mukkoṭi Ekādaśī
GatesMany Veṅkaṭeśvara and Viṣṇu temples set up a decorated northern gate for the day
BengaluruLong queues at Vaikuṇṭha-dvāra temples across the city
DhanurmāsaViṣṇu temples hold dawn pūjās all month, with pongal (huggi) as prasāda
HomesFasting, Viṣṇu Sahasranāma, Gītā reading and night bhajans
Chant: Viṣṇu Sahasranāma

In Karnataka the day is widely called Mukkoṭi Ekādaśī. Temples of Veṅkaṭeśvara and Viṣṇu build a festive northern gate, and city temples see queues through the night.

Dhanurmāsa pūjā at dawn, with huggi (pongal) as prasāda, is a long tradition in Mysuru and across the state.

Karnataka’s Haridāsa poets, Purandaradāsa and Kanakadāsa among them, sang many songs on Ekādaśī and on fasting with devotion rather than for show. Their kīrtanas are still sung in night vigils across the state.

MOKṢADĀ EKĀDAŚĪ

The northern story
Name“The Ekādaśī that gives liberation”
King VaikhānasaA king dreamt his father suffered in hell; a sage told him to keep this Ekādaśī and give its merit to his father
ResultHis father was freed
MeaningThe fast can help not only oneself but one’s ancestors
SourceTold in the Ekādaśī māhātmyas ascribed to the Brahmāṇḍa and Padma Purāṇas

The Mokṣadā story is typical of the Ekādaśī māhātmyas: a king, King Vaikhānasa of Campaka city, troubled by a dream of his father in hell, is told by the sage Parvata to keep this Ekādaśī and offer its merit to his father, who is then freed.

It reflects a strong Indian belief that a descendant’s good acts can help ancestors, also seen in śrāddha and in offerings at places like Gayā.

Every Ekādaśī of the year has such a māhātmya, a glorification story, often set as a dialogue between Kṛṣṇa and Yudhiṣṭhira. Together they form a popular literature that families read on each Ekādaśī.

ZAPPY: Read 700 verses in one day?

PROF. BOLT: Many do. Or read one chapter well. Our 18 chapters are online.

Read: Bhagavad Gītā, all 18 chapters · Ch 12, Bhakti · Ch 15

The tradition that the Gītā was spoken on Mārgaśīrṣa Śukla Ekādaśī is widely held and is the basis of Gītā Jayantī. Kurukṣetra in Haryana hosts a large Gītā Mahotsav around the date.

Many families read the whole Gītā aloud that day, often in a group, or a single chapter with care. Our series covers all 700 ślokas with meanings and deep dives.

For first-time readers, teachers often suggest beginning with Chapter 2 (the soul and duty), Chapter 12 (devotion, only twenty verses) or Chapter 15 (the inverted tree, also twenty verses). Reading one chapter slowly with its meaning is better than rushing through all eighteen.

HOW THE FAST IS KEPT

Levels of the vow
NirjalaNo food or water, from sunrise to the next sunrise
PhalāhāraFruit, milk and nuts only
EkabhuktaOne meal without grains
No grainsRice and other grains, and often beans, are avoided by all
HealthChildren, the elderly, the sick, and pregnant women are traditionally excused; a light fast is fine

ZAPPY: Can diabetics fast?

PROF. BOLT: Not without their doctor's advice. Tradition excuses the sick. Devotion matters more than hunger.

The fast can be kept at many levels, and tradition is flexible: the ill, the elderly, children and pregnant women are excused or keep a light fast. Devotion, not strain, is the point.

Anyone with diabetes, heart disease or on regular medication should check with their doctor before fasting.

Fasting in Indian tradition is also a discipline of the senses and tongue: less food, less talk, more prayer. Many keep a vow of silence for part of the day, or avoid screens and entertainment, giving the time to reading and chanting.

WHY NO RICE?

A belief and a reason
BeliefA popular saying: on Ekādaśī, sin itself takes refuge in grains
MedhaA story: the sage Medhas’s body became rice and barley, which are thus treated as living on this day
PracticalA day of light eating rests the body; old texts saw fasting as cleansing
SubstitutesSābudāna, potato, rājgirā, fruits and milk

The belief that sin resides in grains on Ekādaśī is popular rather than scriptural in a strict sense; it is often told with the Medhas story, in which a sage’s body became rice and barley.

Whatever the reason, a grain-free day of light eating is the defining practice of Ekādaśī across India, and regional fasting foods have grown up around it.

THE NIGHT VIGIL

Jāgaraṇa
Stay awakeMany stay awake through the night of Ekādaśī
HowBhajans, the Viṣṇu Sahasranāma, Gītā reading, Divya Prabandham, stories
Paramapada sopānamIn Tamil homes, the snakes-and-ladders board game of “steps to Vaikuṇṭha” is played to stay awake
Gate at dawnVigil ends with the gate’s opening or dawn darśan

ZAPPY: Snakes and ladders?

PROF. BOLT: An old Indian game. Ladders are virtues, snakes are vices. The last square is Vaikuṇṭha.

Jāgaraṇa, keeping awake, is an old part of Ekādaśī. It turns the night into worship and prepares the devotee for the dawn darśan.

The Paramapada sopānam game, an Indian ancestor of snakes and ladders, is still played in many Tamil homes on this night: its ladders are virtues and its snakes vices, and its last square is Vaikuṇṭha.

In Andhra and Karnataka homes, night vigils are filled with Haridāsa songs and Annamācārya kīrtanas; in the North, with kīrtan and Hanumān Cālīsā; in Tamil Nadu, with the Divya Prabandham. The vigil looks different everywhere, but its purpose is the same: to welcome the dawn awake.

BREAKING THE FAST

Pāraṇa on Dvādaśī
WhenOn the morning of Dvādaśī, Monday 21 December 2026, within the window in your pañcāṅga
HowWith tulasī water, then a simple sāttvika meal
Feed firstMany first feed a guest or the needy
Don’t miss itThe window matters: breaking too late is said to spoil the vow

Breaking the fast correctly is part of the vow. The pāraṇa window on Dvādaśī morning is given in almanacs; many first offer tulasī water to Viṣṇu and feed a guest or the needy.

A traditional Dvādaśī meal in the South includes simple dishes such as agathi keerai (a leaf), amla and sundaikkai, chosen to restore the body gently.

If the pāraṇa window is very early or very short, families often wake before dawn, offer prayers and break the fast with a sip of water and a tulasī leaf, then eat properly later in the morning.

KING AMBARĪṢA

A Dvādaśī story
The kingA great devotee who kept Ekādaśī all his life
DurvāsāThe sage came as a guest on Dvādaśī, then went to bathe and was late
DilemmaThe pāraṇa window was passing; the king sipped water to keep the vow without eating before his guest
WrathDurvāsā raged and sent a demon; Viṣṇu’s discus Sudarśana destroyed it and chased the sage through the worlds
LessonViṣṇu told Durvāsā to seek the king’s forgiveness: the devotee’s humility protects him
SourceBhāgavata Purāṇa, Book 9

The Ambarīṣa story, in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa’s ninth book, is the classic tale of Dvādaśī. Faced with breaking the vow or insulting his guest, the king sips water, which counts as both eating and not eating.

Durvāsā’s fury and Sudarśana’s chase through all the worlds end when Viṣṇu tells the sage that he is bound to his devotees, and that only Ambarīṣa can save him. The king prays for the sage, and the discus stops.

ĀṆḌĀḶ’S TIRUPPĀVAI

Mārgaḻi mornings
ĀṇḍāḷThe woman Āḻvār of Śrīvilliputtūr
TiruppāvaiThirty Tamil verses, one for each morning of Mārgaḻi
SungAt dawn in Viṣṇu temples and homes all month, including Tirumala, where it replaces the Suprabhātam
Opening“mārgaḻi tiṅgaḷ”: “in the month of Mārgaḻi, on this fine full-moon day”
See: Veṅkaṭeśa Suprabhātam

Āṇḍāḷ, the only woman among the twelve Āḻvārs, wrote the Tiruppāvai as a vow kept by young women in Mārgaḻi. Its thirty verses are sung at dawn throughout the month, and in Tirumala during Mārgaḻi it replaces the Suprabhātam.

Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī falls within this month of dawn songs, so many devotees read the Tiruppāvai alongside the Gītā and the Sahasranāma.

Āṇḍāḷ’s story is loved: she wore the garlands meant for the god before they were offered, and Viṣṇu declared he preferred them so. She is said to have merged with Raṅganātha at Srirangam, linking her month of songs to the temple of the gate.

WHAT THE DAY TEACHES

Gates, hunger and the Gītā
GateLiberation is a door: it can be opened
FastHunger for a day reminds us what we really need
VigilStay awake to what matters
GītāThe same day the Lord gave the Gītā
AmbarīṣaHumility protects better than power

The day gathers several teachings: the door to the highest can be opened; fasting clears the mind; staying awake is a kind of devotion; and the Gītā, spoken on this day, explains how to live and how to be free.

The Ambarīṣa story adds the last lesson: humility protects the devotee more surely than power.
