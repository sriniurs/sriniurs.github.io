WHERE WE ARE

Alongside the Awadhī hymns, Hanumān has a set of short Sanskrit verses that people learn by heart and recite before travel, study, work or sleep. Some come from known works, like the Rāmarakṣā Stotra and the Rāmacaritamānas; others are traditional verses gathered in stotra books.

Here are eight of the best loved, and the Hanumān Gāyatrī, with their metres, sources where known, and the stories behind their images.

ŚLOKA 1

SWIFT AS THE MIND
manojavaṃ mārutatulyavegaṃjitendriyaṃ buddhimatāṃ variṣṭhamvātātmajaṃ vānarayūthamukhyaṃśrīrāmadūtaṃ śaraṇaṃ prapadye ‖1‖
I take refuge in Rāma’s messenger: swift as the mind, fast as the Wind, master of his senses, best of the wise, son of the Wind, chief of the vānara hosts.
WordMeans
manojavaṃ mārutatulyavegaṃswift as thought, speed equal to the Wind
jitendriyaṃ buddhimatāṃ variṣṭhamsenses conquered, best of the intelligent
vātātmajaṃ vānarayūthamukhyaṃWind’s son, leader of the vānara hosts
śrīrāmadūtaṃ śaraṇaṃ prapadyeI take refuge in Rāma’s messenger
Swift as thought, master of himself.

ŚLOKA 1 · DEEP DIVE

SWIFT AS THE MIND

🔎 In context

This is the best-known Sanskrit verse to Hanumān. It comes from the Rāmarakṣā Stotra, a protective hymn to Rāma attributed to the sage Budha Kauśika, and is recited on its own before travel, study or any task.

📜 Looking deeper

The verse packs Hanumān’s whole character into eight titles in the upajāti metre. The first two are about speed: mind and Wind are the two fastest things in Indian thought. The next two are about control and wisdom, so speed never becomes recklessness. The last two give his birth and rank, and the final line names him by his service. Śaraṇaṃ prapadye, “I take refuge”, is the language of surrender used for Viṣṇu himself, applied here to his messenger.

🌱 For today

Be quick, but in charge of yourself. Speed without control is just falling.

↑ ŚLOKA 1 · Swift as thought, master of himself.

ŚLOKA 2

WHAT REMEMBERING HIM GIVES
buddhir balaṃ yaśo dhairyaṃnirbhayatvam arogatāajāḍyaṃ vākpaṭutvaṃ cahanūmat smaraṇād bhavet ‖2‖
Intelligence, strength, fame, courage, fearlessness, good health, alertness and skill in speech: all these come from remembering Hanumān.
WordMeans
buddhir balaṃ yaśo dhairyaṃintellect, strength, fame, steadiness
nirbhayatvam arogatāfearlessness, health
ajāḍyaṃ vākpaṭutvaṃ caalertness and eloquence
hanūmat smaraṇād bhavetcome from remembering Hanumān
Eight gifts from remembering him.

ŚLOKA 2 · DEEP DIVE

WHAT REMEMBERING HIM GIVES

🔎 In context

A popular verse, said by students before exams and by speakers before talks. Its source is not certain; it appears in traditional collections of Hanumān ślokas and is quoted everywhere.

📜 Looking deeper

Each gift matches a moment in the Rāmāyaṇa: buddhi in his choice of speech for Sītā; bala in the leap; yaśas in his fame across three worlds; dhairya when he despaired in Laṅkā and kept looking; fearlessness in Rāvaṇa’s court; health, through the sañjīvanī; ajāḍya, freedom from dullness, the opposite of the forgetting curse; and vākpaṭutva, the perfect speech Rāma praised. Smaraṇa, remembering, is enough: no ritual is required.

🌱 For today

Pick one of these eight to work on this week. Hanumān had all of them.

↑ ŚLOKA 2 · Eight gifts from remembering him.

ŚLOKA 3

THE SUNDARA KĀṆḌA INVOCATION
atulitabaladhāmaṃ hemaśailābhadehaṃdanujavanakṛśānuṃ jñānināmagragaṇyamsakalaguṇanidhānaṃ vānarāṇām adhīśaṃraghupatipriyabhaktaṃ vātajātaṃ namāmi ‖3‖
I bow to the son of the Wind: home of matchless strength, his body like a golden mountain, a fire to the forest of demons, first among the wise, treasury of all virtues, lord of the vānaras, Rāma’s beloved devotee.
WordMeans
atulitabaladhāmaṃabode of incomparable strength
hemaśailābhadehaṃbody shining like a golden mountain
danujavanakṛśānuṃfire to the forest of demons
raghupatipriyabhaktaṃ vātajātaṃ namāmiI bow to the Wind-born, Rāma’s dear devotee

ŚLOKA 3 · DEEP DIVE

THE SUNDARA KĀṆḌA INVOCATION

🔎 In context

Tulasīdāsa wrote this Sanskrit verse as the third invocation of the Sundara Kāṇḍa of his Rāmacaritamānas. Every recitation of that book begins with it, so it is heard in homes and temples every Tuesday.

📜 Looking deeper

The metre is mālinī, a graceful 15-syllable line, unusual for Tulasīdāsa, who wrote mostly in Awadhī. The images are carefully chosen: a golden mountain for his size and colour, a forest fire for what he did to Laṅkā’s demons and its Aśoka grove. Jñānināmagragaṇyam, “counted first among the wise”, again puts wisdom beside strength. The Cālīsā’s “atulita bala dhāmā” in caupāī 2 echoes this verse’s first words.

🌱 For today

Strength, wisdom and devotion together make the complete person.

↑ ŚLOKA 3 · A golden mountain of strength.

ŚLOKA 4

WHEREVER RĀMA IS SUNG
yatra yatra raghunāthakīrtanaṃtatra tatra kṛtamastakāñjalimbāṣpavāriparipūrṇalocanaṃmārutiṃ namata rākṣasāntakam ‖4‖
Wherever Rāma’s praises are sung, there, with joined hands raised to his head and eyes full of tears, is Māruti, destroyer of rākṣasas. Bow to him.
WordMeans
yatra yatra raghunāthakīrtanaṃwherever Rāma’s praise is sung
tatra tatra kṛtamastakāñjalimthere, hands joined above his head
bāṣpavāriparipūrṇalocanaṃeyes filled with tears
mārutiṃ namata rākṣasāntakambow to Māruti, slayer of demons
Wherever Rāma is sung, he is there.

ŚLOKA 4 · DEEP DIVE

WHEREVER RĀMA IS SUNG

🔎 In context

This verse is the scriptural basis for leaving a seat for Hanumān at Rāmāyaṇa recitals. It is traditional, found in many stotra collections.

📜 Looking deeper

The picture is precise: hands not just joined but raised to the forehead, the highest salute; eyes not just moist but brimming. The great warrior, slayer of rākṣasas, weeps at a song. That contrast, strength and tenderness in one person, is the core of Hanumān devotion. The metre is rathoddhatā, an eleven-syllable line.

🌱 For today

Let yourself be moved. Strong people cry too.

↑ ŚLOKA 4 · Wherever Rāma is sung, he is there.

ŚLOKA 5

ENDER OF SĪTĀ’S SORROW
añjanānandanaṃ vīraṃjānakīśokanāśanamkapīśam akṣahantāraṃvande laṅkābhayaṅkaram ‖5‖
I worship the hero, Añjanā’s joy, who ended Jānakī’s sorrow; lord of the vānaras, slayer of Akṣa, the terror of Laṅkā.
WordMeans
añjanānandanaṃ vīraṃAñjanā’s joy, the hero
jānakīśokanāśanamdestroyer of Sītā’s grief
kapīśam akṣahantāraṃlord of the vānaras, killer of Akṣa

ŚLOKA 5 · DEEP DIVE

ENDER OF SĪTĀ’S SORROW

🔎 In context

A short anuṣṭubh verse, often recited at the start of the Sundara Kāṇḍa and in daily prayer. Each title points to a scene of that book.

📜 Looking deeper

Akṣa, or Akṣa Kumāra, was Rāvaṇa’s young son, killed by Hanumān in single combat in the Aśoka grove; Vālmīki describes him as brilliant and brave, and Hanumān admires his skill and hesitates before killing one so young. To name him here is to recall the hardest fight of the Sundara Kāṇḍa. Laṅkābhayaṅkara, terror of Laṅkā, recalls the burning. The verse begins with his mother’s joy and ends with his enemies’ fear: the same deeds read from two sides.

🌱 For today

What brings joy to some will frighten others. Be sure which side you are on.

↑ ŚLOKA 5 · He ended Sītā’s grief.

ŚLOKA 6

THE OCEAN AS A HOOFPRINT
goṣpadīkṛtavārāśiṃmaśakīkṛtarākṣasamrāmāyaṇamahāmālā-ratnaṃ vande ’nilātmajam ‖6‖
I worship the son of the Wind, who made the ocean like a cow’s hoofprint and the rākṣasas like mosquitoes: the jewel in the great garland of the Rāmāyaṇa.
WordMeans
goṣpadīkṛtavārāśiṃwho made the ocean a hoof-puddle
maśakīkṛtarākṣasamwho made the demons mosquitoes
rāmāyaṇamahāmālāratnaṃjewel of the Rāmāyaṇa’s great garland
vande ’nilātmajamI worship the Wind’s son

ŚLOKA 6 · DEEP DIVE

THE OCEAN AS A HOOFPRINT

🔎 In context

Goṣpada is the little pool of water left in a cow’s hoofprint; to make the ocean a goṣpada is to cross it as easily as stepping over a puddle. The verse is traditional and widely recited.

📜 Looking deeper

The image of the Rāmāyaṇa as a great garland with Hanumān as its central jewel is lovely and apt: in a garland, the jewel hangs at the centre, at the heart. The Sundara Kāṇḍa is the fifth of seven books, near the centre, and it is Hanumān’s. Maśaka, mosquito, also recalls his own size-change: he became as small as a cat to enter Laṅkā, and in some tellings as small as a mosquito.

🌱 For today

Big problems become small when you have the strength and the reason to cross them.

↑ ŚLOKA 6 · The ocean, a hoofprint.

ŚLOKA 7

WITH FOLDED HANDS
ullaṅghya sindhoḥ salilaṃ salīlaṃyaḥ śokavahniṃ janakātmajāyāḥādāya tenaiva dadāha laṅkāṃnamāmi taṃ prāñjalir āñjaneyam ‖7‖
I bow with joined palms to Āñjaneya, who playfully leapt across the waters of the ocean, took the fire of Janaka’s daughter’s grief, and with that very fire burned Laṅkā.
WordMeans
ullaṅghya sindhoḥ salilaṃ salīlaṃleaping the ocean’s water playfully
yaḥ śokavahniṃ janakātmajāyāḥwho, the fire of Sītā’s grief

ŚLOKA 7 · DEEP DIVE

WITH FOLDED HANDS

🔎 In context

This verse, in upajāti metre, is famous for its central conceit, and for its pun: salilaṃ salīlam, “the water, playfully”.

📜 Looking deeper

The fire that burned Laṅkā, says the poet, was not really from Hanumān’s tail: it was the fire of Sītā’s grief, which he carried away from her and set loose on the city that caused it. It is a poetic way of saying that injustice brings its own destruction. Vālmīki himself hints at this: Hanumān realises the fire did not burn him because of Sītā’s power, and that her suffering, not his tail, doomed Laṅkā.

🌱 For today

Grief carried by a friend can become a force for change.

↑ ŚLOKA 7 · Sītā’s grief burned Laṅkā.

I meditate on the Wind’s delight, Āñjaneya: with a rosy face, a form lovely as a golden mountain, dwelling at the foot of the pārijāta tree.
WordMeans

kāñcanādrikamanīyavigrahamform lovely as a golden mountain
pārijātatarumūlavāsinaṃdwelling at the foot of the pārijāta

ŚLOKA 8 · DEEP DIVE

UNDER THE WISHING TREE

🔎 In context

A dhyāna śloka, a verse for meditation, used at the start of Hanumān worship. Bhāvayāmi, “I visualise”, makes clear that this is a picture to hold in mind, not a story.

📜 Looking deeper

The pārijāta is the wish-fulfilling tree of Indra’s heaven, which rose from the churning of the ocean and was later brought to earth by Kṛṣṇa. To place Hanumān at its foot is to say that he grants wishes, and that his true home is a heavenly one, though he chose to stay on earth. Pāṭala, rose-pink, is the colour of the rising sun and of the pāṭalī flower.

🌱 For today

A clear image of calm strength is worth carrying through the day.

↑ ŚLOKA 8 · Rosy-faced, under the wishing tree.

HANUMĀN GĀYATRĪ

THE HANUMĀN GĀYATRĪ
oṃ āñjaneyāya vidmahevāyuputrāya dhīmahitanno hanumat pracodayāt ‖gāyatrī‖
Om. May we know Āñjaneya; may we meditate on the son of the Wind; may Hanumān inspire us.
WordMeans
oṃ āñjaneyāya vidmahemay we know Āñjaneya
vāyuputrāya dhīmahimay we meditate on the Wind’s son
tanno hanumatmay that Hanumān
pracodayāturge us on, inspire us
Know, meditate, be moved.

HANUMĀN GĀYATRĪ · DEEP DIVE

THE HANUMĀN GĀYATRĪ

🔎 In context

The Gāyatrī form, modelled on the great Vedic Gāyatrī mantra to the Sun, has been adapted for many deities. This one is popular and is often chanted 108 times on a rosary. It is a later devotional formula, not from the Vedas.

📜 Looking deeper

All deity gāyatrīs follow the same three steps: vidmahe, “may we know”; dhīmahi, “may we meditate”; pracodayāt, “may he impel”. Knowledge, meditation, then inspiration to act. For Hanumān, the third step fits perfectly: he is the deity of action and courage. The grammar is plain Sanskrit dative and imperative forms, which makes it easy to learn.

🌱 For today

Know, reflect, then act. A good order for anything.

↑ HANUMĀN GĀYATRĪ · Know, meditate, be moved.
