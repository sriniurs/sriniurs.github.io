BHAJA GOVINDAM

PART 2 · VERSES 11–20 · THE NOOSE OF DESIRE
Ādi Śaṅkara's "hammer for delusion" · explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: First Part 2 for chanting in one go. Then the story of the fourteen disciples and the hymn's background, and two pages for each verse: the chant, a picture, what it says and the key words; then a deeper look, background facts, the inner teaching and the one thing to remember. Part 1 covered verses 1–10; Part 3 covers 21–33.

FOREWORD

In Part 1, Śaṅkara shook the old grammarian awake: wealth, the body, family and life itself are fleeting; turn to Govinda.

Part 2 (verses 11–20) goes further. Time plays and life runs out; desire never ages; robes and austerities can be costumes; even pilgrimages are not enough without knowledge. Here Śaṅkara's own "garland of twelve" ends, and his disciples begin to speak.

And then the tone turns gentle: whoever delights in the highest rejoices anywhere, and a little Gītā, a drop of Gaṅgā water and one sincere prayer are enough to lose the fear of death.

THE STORY · THE FOURTEEN DISCIPLES

How the "garland of twelve" grew into a hymn
The story in 5 frames
The master's twelve. On the lane in Kāśī, Śaṅkara sings to the old grammarian. His verses, with the refrain, make the Dvādaśa-mañjarikā, "the garland of twelve".
The signature. A verse closes it: "thus this whole teaching was given to the grammarian by Śrī Śaṅkara Bhagavatpāda".
The disciples are moved. Tradition says fourteen of his disciples, hearing the master, each added a verse of their own.
The garland of fourteen. Their verses form the Caturdaśa-mañjarikā. Some lists name the disciple for each verse, including Padmapāda, Toṭaka, Hastāmalaka and Sureśvara; the lists differ.
One hymn. Together with a few closing verses, the two garlands became the Bhaja Govindam we sing today.

The master

Śaṅkara: twelve verses and the refrain.

The pupils

Fourteen disciples, a verse each.

The result

A hymn sung by everyone.

ZAPPY: The disciples wrote part of it?

PROF. BOLT: So tradition says. That's why some verses sound like monks warning monks: they were students taking the master's words to heart.

ABOUT BHAJA GOVINDAM

A Vedānta primer in song

✍️ The author

Ascribed to Ādi Śaṅkara, the 8th-century teacher of Advaita Vedānta, with verses traditionally credited to his disciples.

🎵 The sound

Short rhyming lines of sixteen beats (mātrās), in a metre called pādākulaka: easy to sing and remember.

Two garlands. Tradition groups the verses into the Dvādaśa-mañjarikā, "the garland of twelve" that Śaṅkara sang, and the Caturdaśa-mañjarikā, "the garland of fourteen" by his disciples. Versions differ in how they number and divide them.

For everyone. Its refrain names Govinda, Kṛṣṇa, so it is loved by Advaitins, Smārtas and Vaiṣṇavas alike. M. S. Subbulakshmi's recording made it famous across India.

Knowledge and devotion. Coming from the great teacher of knowledge (jñāna), its message is striking: knowledge must ripen into devotion (bhakti).

VERSE 11

TIME TAKES IT ALL
mā kuru dhanajanayauvanagarvaṃharati nimeṣātkālaḥ sarvam |māyāmayamidamakhilaṃ hitvābrahmapadaṃ tvaṃ praviśa viditvā ‖11‖
Do not be proud of wealth, of people around you, or of youth: Time takes them all away in the blink of an eye. Giving up all this, which is made of māyā, and knowing the truth, enter the state of Brahman.
Don't be proud of money, followers or youth: time steals them in a moment. Let go of what is passing and enter what is real.

VERSE 11 · GOING DEEPER

TIME TAKES IT ALL

Three prides. Wealth, a crowd of supporters, and youth: the three things people are proudest of, and the three that time takes most quickly.

Kāla. In Sanskrit kāla means both "time" and "death". Time is not neutral here; it is the great thief.

Māyā. Śaṅkara's key word: the world as it appears, real enough while it lasts, but not the final reality. Seeing it so is how one lets go.

A variant. Some texts read budhvā, "understanding", for hitvā, "giving up": understanding it as māyā, enter Brahman.

💡 Māyā

The power that makes the one reality appear as many changing things.

💡 Kāla

Time and death; also a name of Śiva as Mahākāla.

Be grateful for what you have, not proud of it. Time is only lending it to you.

ZAPPY: So enjoying youth is wrong?

PROF. BOLT: Enjoying it isn't. Being proud of it is. Time takes it in a blink, says Śaṅkara.

Wealth, followers, youth: time takes them in a blink.
The source text marks a variant reading, budhvā ("understanding"), for hitvā ("giving up").

VERSE 12

THE SEASONS COME AND GO
dinayāminyau sāyaṃ prātaḥśiśiravasantau punarāyātaḥ |kālaḥ krīḍati gacchatyāyuḥtadapi na muñcatyāśāvāyuḥ ‖12‖
Day and night, dusk and dawn, winter and spring come round again and again. Time plays, and life slips away; yet the wind of desire does not let go.
Days and seasons keep returning, while our life keeps running out. Even so, desire never lets us go.

VERSE 12 · GOING DEEPER

THE SEASONS COME AND GO

Cycles and a line. Days and seasons go in circles; they always come back. Our life goes in a straight line; it doesn't. That contrast is the whole verse.

Time at play. Kālaḥ krīḍati: time is pictured as a child playing, carelessly using up our years.

The wind of hope. Āśā is hope, desire, expectation. Śaṅkara calls it a wind that keeps blowing us about long after the reasons for it have gone.

💡 Āśā

Hope, expectation, desire: neither good nor bad, but it never tires.

💡 Ṛtu

The six Indian seasons, from spring (vasanta) to winter (śiśira).

The seasons will return; your years will not. Spend them on what matters.

ZAPPY: Spring comes back every year, though.

PROF. BOLT: Spring does. Your springs don't. That's the point of the verse: the world circles, life runs straight.

Seasons circle back, life runs out, and desire never tires.

VERSE 13 & 13A

THE BOAT ACROSS

Stop worrying about family and money as if no one is in charge. The only boat across life's ocean is the company of the good. And so ends the "garland of twelve", Śaṅkara's own teaching to the grammarian.

VERSE 13 & 13A · GOING DEEPER

THE BOAT ACROSS

Someone is in charge. Kiṃ tava nāsti niyantā? "Is there no one who governs you?" We worry as if everything depended on us. Śaṅkara reminds us of the Lord who holds the world.

The boat. Saṃsāra is often called an ocean (bhavasāgara). Verse 9 made good company the first rung of a ladder; here it is the only boat.

13a: the end of the twelve. The next verse closes the Dvādaśa-mañjarikā, the garland of twelve: "thus this teaching was given to the grammarian by Śrī Śaṅkara". From verse 14, tradition says, his disciples speak.

💡 Bhagavatpāda

"Revered feet of the Lord": the honorific for Śaṅkara.

💡 Vātula

"Windy": the old Indian idea of madness as disturbed wind in the body.

You are not the only one holding up your world. Find good people and get in the boat.

ZAPPY: Why call him crazy?

PROF. BOLT: Because he worries as if no one were in charge of the world. Śaṅkara's answer: trust, and keep good company.

The only boat across the ocean: the company of the good.

VERSE 14 · GOING DEEPER

COSTUMES FOR THE BELLY

The disciples speak. Tradition says the next fourteen verses, the Caturdaśa-mañjarikā, were added by Śaṅkara's disciples, each inspired by the master's song.

Outer and inner. Matted locks, a shaved head, plucked hair (as some ascetics did), ochre robes: all signs of renunciation. But a costume is not renunciation. Some wear it to be fed.

A sharp word to monks. This verse was spoken by monks to monks: a warning to themselves first. Real renunciation is inside.

💡 Kāṣāya

Ochre or saffron cloth worn by renunciates.

💡 Caturdaśa-mañjarikā

"The garland of fourteen": verses traditionally credited to Śaṅkara's disciples.

Wearing the uniform is easy. Being what it stands for is the work.

ZAPPY: Is this against sādhus?

PROF. BOLT: Against fake renunciation. It was monks warning monks: the robe is not the realisation.

A robe is not renunciation: look past the costume.

Body weak, hair white, teeth gone, walking with a stick: and still he clutches his bundle of desires.

VERSE 15 · GOING DEEPER

THE OLD MAN AND HIS STICK

A portrait. The verse paints old age plainly: weak limbs, white hair, a toothless mouth, a stick. Desire alone does not age.

Back to the story. It is a picture of the old grammarian, and of all of us if we don't wake up: the body gives up long before the wanting does.

Piṇḍa. A lump or ball: desires are pictured as a solid lump clutched to the chest.

💡 Vārdhakya

Old age: in Indian thought, a time meant for turning inward.

💡 Vānaprastha

The "forest-dweller" stage: stepping back from worldly life after the householder years.

The body lets go in time; the mind has to be taught to. Start letting go before the stick.

ZAPPY: Even very old people still want things?

PROF. BOLT: The verse says desire doesn't age with the body. Letting go has to be practised; it doesn't come automatically.

The body lets go; desire holds on, unless we learn to release it.

VERSE 16

Fire in front, the sun at his back; at night, knees drawn up to the chin; alms taken in the palm of the hand; living under a tree. Even so, the noose of desire does not let him go.
He sits by a fire, sun on his back, hugs his knees at night, begs from his palm, lives under a tree: a perfect ascetic. And still desire holds him in its noose.

VERSE 16 · GOING DEEPER

STILL THE NOOSE OF DESIRE

A hard life. The verse describes extreme austerity: a fire in front for heat, the sun behind, no blanket at night, eating only what fits in the palm, no house but a tree.

Yet bound. Outer hardship, the verse says, does not free the mind. The noose of desire can tighten around a hermit as easily as around a king.

Two portraits. Verse 15 showed the old householder; verse 16 shows the ascetic. Both, says the disciple, can be equally bound.

💡 Pañcāgni

A severe austerity of sitting among fires with the sun overhead.

💡 Karapātra

"Hand as bowl": monks who eat only from their palms.

Freedom is not measured by how little you own, but by how little owns you.

ZAPPY: He gave up everything and still isn't free?

PROF. BOLT: On the outside, yes. The noose of desire was still inside. That's the work that matters.

Outer austerity is not inner freedom: desire can bind a hermit too.

Pilgrimages, vows and charity are good, but without knowing the truth of the Self, no one is freed, not even in a hundred births.

VERSE 17 · GOING DEEPER

PILGRIMAGE WITHOUT KNOWLEDGE

Śaṅkara's central teaching. In Advaita Vedānta, liberation comes from knowledge (jñāna) of the Self. Good deeds, pilgrimages and vows purify the mind, but they do not by themselves free it.

Gaṅgāsāgara. The holy place where the Gaṅgā enters the Bay of Bengal, a great pilgrimage at Makara Saṅkrānti. Even that, without knowledge, is not enough.

Not against rituals. This doesn't mean "skip them": rituals prepare the ground. Knowledge is the seed that grows freedom.

💡 Jñāna

Knowledge of the Self: in Advaita, the direct means to liberation.

💡 Citta-śuddhi

Purification of the mind: what good actions give, preparing for knowledge.

Good deeds clean the window; knowledge lets the light in.

ZAPPY: So pilgrimages are useless?

PROF. BOLT: No. They purify. But Śaṅkara says freedom itself comes from knowing who you are.

Pilgrimage, vows, charity: good, but freedom needs knowledge.
The source text records a variant word order, bhajati na muktiṃ.

VERSE 18

THE JOY OF LETTING GO

Living in a temple or at the foot of a tree; the bare ground for a bed; a deerskin for clothing; giving up every possession and pleasure: whom does such dispassion not make happy?
Living in a temple or under a tree, sleeping on the ground, owning almost nothing: true dispassion brings real happiness.

VERSE 18 · GOING DEEPER

THE JOY OF LETTING GO

The other side. Verse 16 showed an ascetic still bound by desire. Verse 18 shows the real thing: one who has let go inside as well as outside, and is happy.

Sukha. The surprising word is "happiness". Renunciation is not misery; it is freedom from the weight of wanting.

For householders. Most of us won't live under a tree. But the taste of vairāgya is available to anyone who enjoys something without needing to own it.

💡 Vairāgya

Dispassion: one of the two wings of yoga, with practice (abhyāsa).

💡 Aparigraha

Non-possessiveness: one of the yamas of yoga.

The less you need, the lighter you walk. Real letting go feels like relief, not loss.

ZAPPY: Owning nothing makes you happy?

PROF. BOLT: Not owning nothing, but needing nothing. That freedom, says the verse, makes anyone happy.

Inner letting go is happiness, not loss.

Meditating or enjoying, with people or alone, it doesn't matter: the one whose mind rests in the highest is always full of joy.

VERSE 19 · GOING DEEPER

JOY, JOY, JOY

The surprising verse. After so many verses on renouncing, this one says: outer life doesn't decide it. Yogī or enjoyer, in a crowd or alone, what matters is where the mind rests.

Three times joy. Nandati nandati nandaty eva: the repetition is the joy spilling over. It's one of the happiest lines in Sanskrit.

For householders too. This is good news for those of us in the world: the inner delight in the highest is possible anywhere.

💡 Ānanda

Bliss: one of the three words for Brahman, sat-cit-ānanda.

💡 Ramaṇa

"Delighting": the same root as Rāma, "the one in whom all delight".

It isn't where you are but where your mind rests. Rest it in the highest, and joy follows you everywhere.

ZAPPY: So you don't have to become a monk?

PROF. BOLT: Not according to this verse. Wherever you are, if your mind delights in the highest, you rejoice, rejoice, rejoice.

Yogī or enjoyer, alone or in company: a mind resting in Brahman rejoices.

VERSE 20 · GOING DEEPER

A LITTLE GĪTĀ, A DROP OF GAṄGĀ

Gentleness after hammering. After verses of warning, this one comforts: a little is enough. Not the whole Gītā, a little; not a bath, a drop; not a lifetime of rituals, once.

Three practices. Study (the Gītā), purification (the Gaṅgā), and devotion (worship of Murāri). Knowledge, purity, love: a whole spiritual life in miniature.

Murāri. "Enemy of Mura": Kṛṣṇa, who slew the demon Mura. The hymn returns to Govinda by another name.

💡 Gītā

The 700-verse dialogue of Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna; Śaṅkara wrote a famous commentary on it.

💡 Yama

The lord of death and of dharma, who judges the departed.

Don't wait to do it all. Start with a little, sincerely, and it is enough to change everything.

ZAPPY: Just a little Gītā is enough?

PROF. BOLT: Sincerely, yes, says the verse. A little study, a drop of Gaṅgā, one real act of worship. The door opens with very little.

A little Gītā, a drop of Gaṅgā, one sincere prayer: death loses its terror.

Verses 17–18. Pilgrimages without knowledge don't free us; true dispassion is happiness.
Verses 19–20. A mind resting in Brahman rejoices anywhere; a little Gītā, a drop of Gaṅgā and one prayer are enough.
