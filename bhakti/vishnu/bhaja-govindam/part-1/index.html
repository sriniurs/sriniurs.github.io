Ādi Śaṅkara's "hammer for delusion" · explained and illustrated

Then the story of the old grammarian at Kāśī and the hymn's background, and two pages for each verse: the chant, a picture, what it says and the key words; then a deeper look, background facts, the inner teaching and the one thing to remember. Parts 2–3 cover the rest of the hymn.

FOREWORD

"Bhaja govindaṃ, bhaja govindaṃ…" Few Sanskrit songs are as widely sung. Many of us know its tune long before we know its meaning: a gentle melody carrying some of the bluntest words in all of Indian spiritual writing.

Ādi Śaṅkara, the great teacher of Advaita Vedānta, is said to have sung it on a lane in Kāśī, on seeing an old man cramming grammar rules. In a few short verses he asks the questions we avoid: what is wealth for, what is the body, who are our family, who are we, and what will save us at the end?

Its old name is Moha Mudgara, "the hammer for delusion". It hammers, but it also comforts: its answer to every question is the same gentle refrain, turn to Govinda. We take it in three parts; this first part covers verses 1–10.

THE STORY · THE OLD MAN AND THE GRAMMAR

How the hymn is said to have been born, in Kāśī
The story in 5 frames
A walk in Kāśī. Ādi Śaṅkara is walking through the lanes of Vārāṇasī with his disciples.
An old man studying. They pass an elderly scholar loudly repeating rules of Pāṇini's grammar: "ḍukṛñ karaṇe, ḍukṛñ karaṇe…"
Pity. Śaṅkara is moved: this man is near the end of his life, and he is spending it memorising the roots of verbs.
The song. Śaṅkara sings to him: "Worship Govinda, O foolish mind! When death comes, ḍukṛñ karaṇe will not save you." Then twelve more verses follow.
The disciples join in. Tradition says his fourteen disciples each added a verse, and more verses were added later; together they make the hymn we sing.

The place

Kāśī, Śiva's city on the Gaṅgā.

The cue

A grammar rule, ḍukṛñ karaṇe.

The point

Wake up before the end.

ZAPPY: Was Śaṅkara making fun of the old man?

PROF. BOLT: No. He felt for him. He saw a wise man wasting his last years on rules, and sang to wake him, and us.

ABOUT BHAJA GOVINDAM

A Vedānta primer in song

✍️ The author

Ascribed to Ādi Śaṅkara, the 8th-century teacher of Advaita Vedānta, with verses traditionally credited to his disciples.

🎵 The sound

Short rhyming lines of sixteen beats (mātrās), in a metre called pādākulaka: easy to sing and remember.

Two garlands. Tradition groups the verses into the Dvādaśa-mañjarikā, "the garland of twelve" that Śaṅkara sang, and the Caturdaśa-mañjarikā, "the garland of fourteen" by his disciples. Versions differ in how they number and divide them.

For everyone. Its refrain names Govinda, Kṛṣṇa, so it is loved by Advaitins, Smārtas and Vaiṣṇavas alike. M. S. Subbulakshmi's recording made it famous across India.

Knowledge and devotion. Coming from the great teacher of knowledge (jñāna), its message is striking: knowledge must ripen into devotion (bhakti).

VERSE 1 · GOING DEEPER

WORSHIP GOVINDA, O FOOLISH MIND

The refrain. This verse is the chorus of the whole hymn. In singing, it is repeated after every verse, so the listener hears "bhaja govindaṃ" again and again.

Ḍukṛñ karaṇe. A line from Pāṇini's list of verb roots: "the root ḍukṛñ (kṛ), in the sense of doing". The old man in the story was cramming exactly this. Śaṅkara uses it to stand for all learning that is only memorised, never lived.

Mūḍhamate. "O fool": a harsh word, but tender too. Śaṅkara is speaking to the mind, ours and the old man's, the way a teacher shakes a student awake.

Govinda. A name of Kṛṣṇa: "the one who finds and tends the cows", also read as "the one known through the Vedas (go)".

💡 Pāṇini

The great grammarian of Sanskrit, whose rules and root lists the old man was learning.

💡 Moha Mudgara

The hymn's older name: "the hammer that breaks delusion".

Knowledge you only memorise is a load to carry. Knowledge you live is a boat to cross with.

ZAPPY: Is Śaṅkara against learning grammar?

PROF. BOLT: Not at all: he was a great scholar himself. He's against spending your last years on rules while forgetting what life is for.

When the time comes, no grammar rule will save you; turn to Govinda.

VERSE 2

ENOUGH IS A FEAST
mūḍha jahīhi dhanāgamatṛṣṇāṃkuru sadbuddhiṃ manasi vitṛṣṇām |yallabhase nijakarmopāttaṃvittaṃ tena vinodaya cittam ‖2‖
O fool, give up the thirst to pile up wealth. Make your mind good and free of craving. Whatever you gain by your own work, let that content your mind.
Stop chasing more money. Earn honestly, and be happy with what your own effort brings.

VERSE 2 · GOING DEEPER

ENOUGH IS A FEAST

Not "don't earn". Śaṅkara doesn't tell the householder to stop working. He says: earn by your own effort (nija-karma), and let that be enough. The poison is the tṛṣṇā, the thirst that grows with every coin.

Thirst. Tṛṣṇā is literally thirst: drinking salt water makes it worse. So with wealth chased for its own sake.

Contentment. Santoṣa, contentment, is one of the niyamas of yoga. The mind that is content is free to think of higher things.

💡 Tṛṣṇā

"Thirst": craving, which the Buddha also named as the root of suffering.

💡 Santoṣa

Contentment: one of the five niyamas in Patañjali's Yoga Sūtras.

Work hard, earn honestly, then stop counting. Contentment is the only wealth that never runs out.

ZAPPY: So is money bad?

PROF. BOLT: Earning is fine, says Śaṅkara. It's the endless thirst for more that robs your peace.

Earn honestly, and let it be enough.

VERSE 3

VERSE 3 · GOING DEEPER

THE BODY IS NOT THE PERSON

Who is it for? Śaṅkara was a monk, speaking to monks and seekers in a society where the seekers were mostly men. The point is not about women; it is about any body, including one's own. Infatuation mistakes the outer form for the person.

Vikāra. A "modification": the body is food turned into flesh, always changing, always ageing. The Self that looks through the eyes does not change.

Not hatred. Vedānta does not ask us to despise the body, only to see it clearly. A body is a house, not the one who lives in it.

💡 Moha

Delusion, infatuation: the "moha" that the Moha Mudgara hammers.

💡 Deha and dehī

The body and the one who has the body: a key Gītā distinction.

Love the person, not the packaging. The packaging changes; the one inside does not.

ZAPPY: Isn't this verse a bit harsh?

PROF. BOLT: It was said to monks in Śaṅkara's time. Its real point is for all of us: don't mistake a body, anyone's, for the person.

The body is a changing form; the Self within is what is real.
Some modern editions adapt this verse for all readers; we keep the traditional text and read it as a teaching on bodily attachment in general.

VERSE 4 · GOING DEEPER

A DROP ON A LOTUS LEAF

The image. A drop of water on a lotus leaf never soaks in. It trembles, rolls, and at the slightest breeze is gone. That is life: bright, beautiful and uncertain.

Another lesson in the same image. The Gītā (5.10) uses the lotus leaf differently: the wise act in the world untouched by it, like a lotus leaf by water. The leaf teaches both fragility and freedom.

Not gloom. Seeing how fleeting life is isn't meant to depress us; it is meant to wake us, so we don't waste the time we have.

💡 Padmapatram

"Like a lotus leaf in water": Gītā 5.10, acting without being stained.

💡 Abhimāna

Pride, ego: listed with disease as what grips the world.

Because the drop may fall any moment, make this moment count.

ZAPPY: That's a sad picture.

PROF. BOLT: It's a true one, and a beautiful one. A drop on a lotus shines. Life is like that: brief, so live it well.

Life trembles like a drop on a lotus leaf: don't waste it.

VERSE 5 · GOING DEEPER

WHILE YOU EARN

A hard truth. Śaṅkara is not attacking families; he is warning against putting all our hope in what is temporary. Love that depends on usefulness fades when the usefulness does.

Then what? If even family attention depends on what we can give, then lasting security has to come from within, from our relationship with the Lord and the Self.

For us as children. The verse also quietly asks us: do we ask after our own elders?

💡 Parivāra

Family, household: here those who gather round a provider.

💡 Gṛhastha

The householder stage of life: earning, raising family; honoured, but not the final goal.

Build a life that does not depend on being useful to others. And make sure the elders in your home are never left without a kind word.

ZAPPY: Is this really true of families?

PROF. BOLT: Not of every family. But Śaṅkara is warning: don't rest your whole peace on others' attention. Find it in God and in yourself.

Love that depends on your earnings fades with them; seek what lasts.

VERSE 6 · GOING DEEPER

WHEN THE BREATH LEAVES

Who were they asking after? The family loved the person, not the body. When the breath leaves, the body that was so dear becomes something to fear and to cremate. So, Śaṅkara asks, which was the real you?

Prāṇa. The breath, prāṇa, is the sign of life. Its leaving shows that the body was never the self: something left, and that something was what everyone loved.

A pointer to the Self. These verses (5 and 6) are not cynical; they push us to ask: what am I, really?

💡 Prāṇa

The life-breath: in Vedānta, the vital force that animates the body.

💡 Antyeṣṭi

The last rites, performed soon after death: the body is returned to the elements.

What everyone loved in you was never the body. Find out what it was.

ZAPPY: Even the wife is afraid?

PROF. BOLT: Of the lifeless body, yes. It shows what she really loved was the person, the life inside. Śaṅkara wants us to find that inner life.

When the breath leaves, the body is feared: so what were we, really?

VERSE 7 · GOING DEEPER

CHILD, YOUTH, OLD MAN

A whole life in four lines. Childhood goes in play, youth in love, old age in worry. Each stage has its own attachment, and each is natural. The problem is that there is never a stage when we turn to the highest.

Saktaḥ. The same word ends each line: "attached". Śaṅkara shows that attachment simply changes its object as we age.

The fourth line. If attachment is our nature, he hints, attach it to the right thing: parame brahmaṇi, the supreme reality.

💡 Āśramas

The four stages of life: student, householder, forest-dweller, renunciate.

💡 Brahman

The supreme reality in Vedānta: being, consciousness, bliss.

Don't wait for "later" to seek the highest. Every stage of life has its own excuse.

ZAPPY: So when should we think of God?

PROF. BOLT: Śaṅkara's answer: now. Childhood plays, youth loves, age worries. If we wait for a free stage, it never comes.

Play, love, worry: every age has its attachment, none chooses the highest.

VERSE 8 · GOING DEEPER

WHO ARE YOU, REALLY?

The great questions. This is the heart of Vedānta in two lines: kas tvam, "who are you?"; kuta āyātaḥ, "where have you come from?" Śaṅkara's whole philosophy is an answer: you are not this body or these roles; you are the Self.

Brother. After calling us "fool" in verse 1, here he calls us bhrātaḥ, "brother". The teacher is gentle again.

Not unloving. Asking "who is your son?" is not a rejection of family; it is a reminder that every relationship is a meeting on a journey, not the end of the journey.

💡 Ko'ham

"Who am I?": the question Ramaṇa Maharṣi made the centre of his teaching.

💡 Saṃsāra

The flowing round of birth, death and rebirth.

Every relationship is a meeting of travellers. Love them on the road, and remember where the road leads.

ZAPPY: Who am I, then?

PROF. BOLT: That's exactly the question Śaṅkara wants us to sit with. His answer, across his whole work: not the body, not the mind, but the Self.

"Who are you? Where did you come from?" Think on it now, brother.

Keep good company, and you slowly let go. Letting go clears delusion; a clear mind settles in the truth; and that is freedom, here and now.

VERSE 9 · GOING DEEPER

THE LADDER OF GOOD COMPANY

A chain reaction. The most quoted verse of the hymn is a ladder. Each rung rests on the one below: satsaṅga, then detachment, then clarity, then steadiness, then freedom.

Why company first? We become like those we spend time with. Being near the wise, good books and devotional singing slowly changes what we want.

Jīvanmukti. Liberation is not only after death. Śaṅkara's Vedānta speaks of the jīvanmukta, one who is free while still living in the body.

💡 Satsaṅga

"Company of truth": gatherings for hearing scripture and singing bhajans take this name.

💡 Jīvanmukta

One liberated while alive; the Gītā's "sthitaprajña" is a close picture.

Choose your company carefully: it is the first rung of the ladder to freedom.

ZAPPY: Freedom starts with friends?

PROF. BOLT: With good company. Spend time with the wise and the good, and the rest follows step by step.

Good company → detachment → clarity → steadiness → freedom, here and now.

VERSE 10

When youth has gone, where is lust? When the water has dried, where is the lake? When wealth is spent, where is the family circle? When the truth is known, where is saṃsāra?
No youth, no lust; no water, no lake; no money, no crowd of relatives. And when you know the truth, the whole round of worldly bondage simply disappears.

VERSE 10 · GOING DEEPER

WHEN THE WATER DRIES UP

Three and then one. The first three lines are things everyone sees: things depend on their causes and vanish when the causes go. The fourth line uses the same logic for the deepest point: saṃsāra depends on ignorance; when the truth is known, it is gone.

A teacher's method. Śaṅkara moves from the obvious to the profound: if you accept the first three, you have to accept the fourth.

End of Part 1. The first ten verses have shaken us awake about wealth, the body, life's shortness and family. Part 2 continues the hammering of delusion.

💡 Kārya and kāraṇa

Effect and cause: when the cause goes, the effect goes.

💡 Ajñāna

Ignorance: in Advaita, the cause of saṃsāra, removed by knowledge.

Bondage has a cause. Remove the cause, ignorance, and bondage dries up like a lake without water.

ZAPPY: Saṃsāra just disappears?

PROF. BOLT: When the truth is known, says Śaṅkara. Like a lake without water, it can't continue without its cause.

No water, no lake; no ignorance, no bondage.
