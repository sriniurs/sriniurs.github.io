Ādi Śaṅkara's "hammer for delusion" · explained and illustrated

Then the end of the grammarian's story and the hymn's background, and two pages for each verse: the chant, a picture, what it says and the key words; then a deeper look, background facts, the inner teaching and the one thing to remember. Parts 1–2 covered verses 1–20.

FOREWORD

Parts 1 and 2 hammered at delusion: wealth, the body, family, time and desire. Part 3 (verses 21–33) turns from warning to the way across.

It begins with the most-sung line after the refrain, "punarapi jananaṃ punarapi maraṇaṃ", and a cry for grace. Then come the great teachings of oneness: one Viṣṇu in you, in me, in all; see the Self in friend and foe; ask "who am I?"; sing, meditate, keep good company, give.

It ends at the guru's feet, with the Lord seen in one's own heart, and with the old grammarian of Kāśī awakened at last.

THE STORY · THE GRAMMARIAN AWAKENED

How the Kāśī story ends, and how the hymn lives on
The story in 5 frames
The song goes on. Śaṅkara's twelve verses and his disciples' fourteen fill the lane in Kāśī.
The old man listens. The grammarian, cramming "ḍukṛñ karaṇe", stops and hears.
Awakened. Verse 32 says he was awakened by Śaṅkara's disciples, his mind purified.
The Name. The last verse repeats the refrain and gives the final answer: remembering the Lord's Name is the way across the ocean.
Still sung. Twelve centuries later it is sung in homes, temples and concert halls, most famously in M. S. Subbulakshmi's recording.

ABOUT BHAJA GOVINDAM

A Vedānta primer in song

✍️ The author

Ascribed to Ādi Śaṅkara, the 8th-century teacher of Advaita Vedānta, with verses traditionally credited to his disciples.

🎵 The sound

Short rhyming lines of sixteen beats (mātrās), in a metre called pādākulaka: easy to sing and remember.

Two garlands. Tradition groups the verses into the Dvādaśa-mañjarikā, "the garland of twelve" that Śaṅkara sang, and the Caturdaśa-mañjarikā, "the garland of fourteen" by his disciples. Versions differ in how they number and divide them.

For everyone. Its refrain names Govinda, Kṛṣṇa, so it is loved by Advaitins, Smārtas and Vaiṣṇavas alike. M. S. Subbulakshmi's recording made it famous across India.

Knowledge and devotion. Coming from the great teacher of knowledge (jñāna), its message is striking: knowledge must ripen into devotion (bhakti).

VERSE 21 · GOING DEEPER

AGAIN BIRTH, AGAIN DEATH

The best-known verse after the refrain. "Punarapi jananaṃ punarapi maraṇaṃ": the rolling repetition makes you feel the endless wheel of rebirth, saṃsāra.

A prayer, not a lecture. For the first time the hymn turns into a direct cry: pāhi murāre, "save me, Murāri". The teacher of knowledge asks for grace.

Knowledge and grace. Śaṅkara's Advaita says knowledge frees; this verse says grace makes that knowledge possible. The two are not enemies.

💡 Saṃsāra

The flowing round of birth, death and rebirth, driven by karma and ignorance.

💡 Murāri

"Enemy of Mura": Kṛṣṇa, who destroyed the demon Mura.

When you feel trapped in the same circles, it is all right to ask for help. Grace is part of the way out.

ZAPPY: Even Śaṅkara asks to be saved?

PROF. BOLT: In this verse, yes. The great teacher of knowledge turns to grace. That's why this hymn touches everyone.

Born again, dying again: save me by Your grace, Murāri.

VERSE 22

THE YOGĪ IN RAGS

His only cloak is stitched from rags picked up on the street; his path is beyond merit and sin; with his mind joined in yoga, the yogī delights like a child, or like one who is mad.
The real yogī wears rags from the street, has gone beyond "good deeds" and "bad deeds", and plays happily like a child or a madman, his mind in the Lord.

VERSE 22 · GOING DEEPER

THE YOGĪ IN RAGS

The opposite of verse 14. There, people wore holy costumes for the belly. Here is the real thing: a yogī in a patched cloak (kanthā) who cares nothing for appearances.

Beyond merit and sin. The jñānī is not bound even by good karma; he acts without the sense "I am the doer". This is a high state, not permission to do wrong.

Child and madman. Sacred texts often compare the free sage to a child (innocent, playful) and a madman (indifferent to opinion). Think of Śuka, or of Rāmakṛṣṇa.

💡 Kanthā

A patched cloak; also the famous Bengali stitched quilt.

💡 Avadhūta

"One who has shaken off" all conventions: the free, wandering sage.

Freedom often looks foolish from outside. A heart at peace doesn't need the world's approval.

ZAPPY: He acts like a madman?

PROF. BOLT: To the world, maybe. Inside, he's at perfect peace, playing like a child in the Lord.

Rags outside, bliss inside: the real yogī plays like a child.

Ask: who am I, where did I come from, who are my parents really? See that it is all passing, like a dream, and let it go.

VERSE 23 · GOING DEEPER

LIKE A DREAM

The questions return. Verse 8 asked them in the middle of the hymn; verse 23 asks again, deeper: not "who is your wife?" but "who are you, who am I?"

The dream. In a dream we have parents, homes, joys and fears, all quite real until we wake. Vedānta says the waking world is like that before the Self is known.

Not cold. Seeing the world as dream-like does not mean not caring. It means not drowning: loving fully, but without clinging.

💡 Svapna

Dream: one of the three states, with waking and deep sleep; see Oṃ in our Mahimna Part 3.

💡 Vicāra

Inquiry: careful reflection, the method of Self-knowledge.

Hold the world the way you hold a dream as you wake: gently, gratefully, and without grasping.

ZAPPY: If it's a dream, why bother?

PROF. BOLT: Because waking up is the point. You play your part well, and you don't mistake the dream for the waking.

"Who am I?" Ask it, and see the world as a dream you can wake from.

VERSE 24 · GOING DEEPER

ONE VIṢṆU IN ALL

Ethics from oneness. If one Lord lives in all, anger at another is anger at Him. Śaṅkara's philosophy of oneness becomes a rule for daily life: patience.

Sama-citta. Even-mindedness: the Gītā's great word (samatvaṃ yoga ucyate, "evenness is yoga", 2.48).

Viṣṇutvam. To become like Viṣṇu, all-pervading, is to see Him in everyone.

💡 Viṣṇu

"The all-pervader": the name itself means the one who is everywhere.

💡 Samatva

Evenness of mind: Gītā 2.48, "yoga is evenness".

When you are angry with someone, remember who lives in them.

ZAPPY: So I can never be angry?

PROF. BOLT: The verse says anger at others is anger at the same Lord in them. Patience comes from seeing that.

One Viṣṇu in you, in me, in all: so why be angry?

VERSE 25 · GOING DEEPER

FRIEND AND FOE

Politics of the heart. Vigraha and sandhi, war and treaty, are terms from statecraft. Śaṅkara uses them for family and friends: don't make life a game of forming camps.

Bheda. The ignorance that sees separateness is, for Advaita, the root problem. Seeing the one Self in all dissolves it.

Carrying on from 24. If there is one Viṣṇu in all, there are no true enemies, only the same Self in different forms.

💡 Advaita

"Non-duality": the teaching that the Self in all is one.

💡 Ātman

The Self: in Advaita, identical with Brahman.

Stop dividing people into camps. The same light looks out of every face.

ZAPPY: Treat enemies like friends?

PROF. BOLT: Don't spend your life fighting or plotting. See the same Self in both. That's Śaṅkara's cure for division.

See the one Self in friend and foe: drop the ignorance of difference.

VERSE 26 · GOING DEEPER

THE FOUR THIEVES

The inner enemies. Indian thought lists six inner enemies (ariṣaḍvarga): lust, anger, greed, delusion, pride and jealousy. This verse names the first four.

Ko'ham. "Who am I?" is the inquiry at the heart of Advaita, later made famous by Ramaṇa Maharṣi. A variant reading adds paśyati so'ham: "he sees, I am That".

Hell now. "Cooked in hidden hell" can be read as our inner state: a mind ruled by these four is already burning.

💡 Ariṣaḍvarga

The six inner enemies: kāma, krodha, lobha, moha, mada, mātsarya.

💡 So'ham

"I am That": the variant line, and a famous mantra of the breath.

Lust, anger, greed and confusion are the four thieves of peace. The question "who am I?" turns on the light.

ZAPPY: What's the hidden hell?

PROF. BOLT: Many read it as the mind ruled by desire and anger: already a kind of hell. Self-knowledge is the way out.

Drop the four thieves and ask: who am I?
The source text records a variant line, paśyati so'ham ("he sees: I am That").

VERSE 27 · GOING DEEPER

FOUR THINGS TO DO

A daily rule in one verse. Four short commands, each starting with a verb form ending in -yam: sing, meditate, lead the mind, give. Many people memorise this verse as a life plan.

The Thousand Names. The Viṣṇu Sahasranāma, from the Mahābhārata; Śaṅkara wrote a commentary on it as well as on the Gītā.

From the head to the hand. The list moves from speech (sing) to mind (meditate) to company (lead the mind) to action (give). Spiritual life ends in generosity.

💡 Viṣṇu Sahasranāma

The thousand names of Viṣṇu, taught by Bhīṣma in the Mahābhārata.

💡 Dāna

Giving: one of the foundations of dharma.

Sing, remember, choose your company, give. A whole spiritual life fits in these four.

ZAPPY: This one's practical!

PROF. BOLT: Very. Four things anyone can do, starting today. Many families teach children this verse first.

Sing, meditate, keep good company, give to those in need.

VERSES 28–29 · GOING DEEPER

PLEASURE AND WEALTH

Two portraits. Verse 28 shows pleasure followed by illness; verse 29 shows wealth followed by fear. Both are about what happens when we chase things for their own sake.

Artham anartham. A play on words: artha (wealth, purpose) becomes anartha (trouble, meaninglessness) when it rules us.

For balance. Indian tradition honours artha and kāma as legitimate goals of life, within dharma. These verses warn against letting them take over.

💡 Puruṣārthas

The four aims of life: dharma, artha, kāma, mokṣa.

💡 Anartha

Trouble, harm: the shadow side of wealth.

Enjoy what comes, earn what you need, but don't let pleasure or money become the master.

ZAPPY: Afraid of their own sons?

PROF. BOLT: Over inheritance and money, sadly, it happens. Śaṅkara says wealth brings worry as often as joy.

Pleasure brings illness, wealth brings fear: neither is the goal.

VERSE 30 · GOING DEEPER

WITH GREAT CARE

A yoga syllabus. The verse lists steps from Patañjali's yoga (prāṇāyāma, pratyāhāra, samādhi) with Vedānta's viveka, discrimination between the eternal and the passing, and devotion's japa.

Avadhāna. Attention, care. The repetition, "with care, with great care", is the teacher's final emphasis: spiritual practice must be done attentively, not mechanically.

Not only feeling. After all the talk of devotion, the hymn insists on disciplined practice too.

💡 Aṣṭāṅga yoga

Patañjali's eight limbs, including prāṇāyāma, pratyāhāra and samādhi.

💡 Viveka

Discrimination: Śaṅkara's first qualification for a seeker.

Practice done carelessly is a habit. Practice done with great care becomes a path.

ZAPPY: Why say "care" twice?

PROF. BOLT: It's the teacher underlining it. Breath, senses, thought, mantra: all of them need real attention.

Breath, senses, discernment, japa, absorption: with great care.

VERSE 31 · GOING DEEPER

AT THE GURU'S FEET

The guru. In Indian tradition, the teacher is the bridge to the truth: Bhaja Govindam itself began with a teacher stopping to help an old man.

Where God is found. After all the warnings, the last teaching verse ends with a promise: drakṣyasi nijahṛdayasthaṃ devam, "you will see the Lord in your own heart". Not far away; within.

Two wings. Devotion (to the guru) and discipline (of senses and mind): the verse joins bhakti and yoga, and the result is jñāna, seeing.

💡 Guru

"The one who removes darkness": the teacher of the path.

💡 Hṛdaya

The heart: in Vedānta, the seat of the Self.

The journey ends where it began: at home, in your own heart.

ZAPPY: So God is inside us?

PROF. BOLT: That's the promise of the last teaching verse: discipline the mind, love the teacher, and you'll see the Lord in your own heart.

Serve the guru, steady the mind: see the Lord in your own heart.

THE GRAMMARIAN AWAKENED
mūḍhaḥ kaścana vaiyākaraṇoḍukṛñkaraṇādhyayanadhurīṇaḥ |śrīmacchaṅkarabhagavacchiṣyaiḥbodhita āsīcchodhitakaraṇaḥ ‖32‖· · ·bhajagovindaṃ bhajagovindaṃgovindaṃ bhaja mūḍhamate |nāmasmaraṇādanyamupāyaṃnahi paśyāmo bhavābdhitaraṇe ‖33‖
(32) A certain foolish grammarian, absorbed in studying "ḍukṛñ karaṇe", was awakened by the disciples of Śrī Śaṅkara Bhagavatpāda, and his mind was purified. (33) Worship Govinda, worship Govinda, worship Govinda, O foolish mind! Other than remembering the Name, we see no other way to cross the ocean of becoming.
So the old grammarian was awakened by Śaṅkara's disciples, his mind made pure. Worship Govinda: the remembrance of His Name is the only way across.

VERSES 32–33 · GOING DEEPER

THE GRAMMARIAN AWAKENED

The story ends. Verse 32 closes the frame opened in Part 1: the old man cramming "ḍukṛñ karaṇe" is awakened. The hymn tells us its own outcome: it worked.

The refrain returns. Verse 33 repeats "bhaja govindaṃ" and adds the hymn's final answer: nāma-smaraṇa, remembering the Lord's Name, is the way across.

From grammar to the Name. A beautiful circle: the old man was obsessed with words; he is saved by one word, the Name of God.

💡 Nāma-smaraṇa

Remembering the divine Name: the heart of bhakti practice in every Indian tradition.

💡 Bhavābdhi

"The ocean of becoming": saṃsāra pictured as a sea to be crossed.

He spent his life on words, and was saved by one Name. Whatever your life has been, it is never too late to turn.

ZAPPY: So the old man was saved?

PROF. BOLT: The hymn says so: awakened, his mind purified. And the last word is the same as the first: bhaja govindaṃ.

Not grammar but the Name: bhaja govindaṃ, O mind.
Verse 33's last word reads bhavābdhitaraṇe in the main text, with bhavataraṇe as a recorded variant.
