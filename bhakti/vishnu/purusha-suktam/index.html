PURUṢA SŪKTAM

THE HYMN OF THE COSMIC PERSON
“Sahasraśīrṣā puruṣaḥ” · explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: First all 16 verses for chanting in one go. FOREWORD

“Sahasraśīrṣā puruṣaḥ…” If you have stood near a temple during an abhiṣeka, you have heard it: the steady Vedic chant as water, milk and honey pour over the deity.

The Puruṣa Sūktam is one of the most famous hymns of the Ṛg Veda. In sixteen verses it pictures God as a Cosmic Person, with a thousand heads and a thousand eyes, who is everything that was and will be, and still stands beyond it all. From His sacrifice come the seasons, the creatures, the Vedas, the sun and moon, the sky and the earth.

For Vaiṣṇavas this Person is Nārāyaṇa, and the hymn is the Vedic foundation of Viṣṇu worship. Its seer is even named Nārāyaṇa.

Hari Oṃ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Is it really 3,000 years old?

PROF. BOLT: The Ṛg Veda is usually dated to the second millennium BCE, and scholars place this hymn in its later layer. Either way, it is among the oldest hymns still chanted every day.

THE STORY · THE PERSON WHO BECAME THE WORLD

A seer’s vision of how everything began
The story in 5 frames
The seer. The Ṛg Veda names the seer of this hymn as Nārāyaṇa, and its deity as Puruṣa, the Person.
The vision. He sees one vast Being, with countless heads, eyes and feet, filling the world and rising beyond it.
The sacrifice. The gods perform the first sacrifice, and the offering is the Person Himself. The seasons are its ghee, fuel and oblation.
The world is born. From that offering come animals, the Vedas, society, the moon from His mind, the sun from His eye, sky, air and earth.
The pattern. That first sacrifice becomes the model, the “first dharma”, for all worship after it.

ZAPPY: The gods sacrificed God? That sounds strange!

PROF. BOLT: It is a picture, not a crime scene! It means the world exists because the Lord gives Himself. Creation is an act of self-giving.

ABOUT THE PURUṢA SŪKTAM

One hymn, found in all the Vedas

📜 Where it comes from

Ṛg Veda 10.90. Seer: Nārāyaṇa. Deity: Puruṣa. 15 verses in anuṣṭubh (4 × 8 syllables), the last in triṣṭubh (4 × 11).

🔁 In other Vedas

With variations in the Atharva Veda (19.6), the Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā of the Śukla Yajur Veda, and the Taittirīya Āraṇyaka of the Kṛṣṇa Yajur Veda.

Link to ViṣṇuWhere
Seer NārāyaṇaThe Ṛg Veda names him as the seer; later texts identify the Puruṣa with Nārāyaṇa
Lakṣmī’s LordThe Taittirīya “uttara” section ends: Hrī and Lakṣmī are Your consorts
Sahasranāma“sahasramūrdhā viśvātmā sahasrākṣaḥ sahasrapāt” echoes verse 1
Bhagavad GītāKṛṣṇa as Puruṣottama, the Supreme Person (15.18); the universal form (ch. 11)
WorshipChanted at abhiṣeka; in many traditions the 16 verses go with the 16 offerings (ṣoḍaśopacāra) of pūjā

Old and new. Many scholars consider the hymn part of the later layer of the Ṛg Veda, on grounds of its language and ideas. Tradition, for its part, calls it the heart of the Vedas: vedeṣu pauruṣaṃ sūktam, “among Vedic hymns, the Puruṣa Sūktam”.

VERSE 1

A THOUSAND HEADS
sahasraśīrṣā puruṣaḥsahasrākṣaḥ sahasrapāt |sa bhūmiṃ viśvato vṛtvāaty atiṣṭhad daśāṅgulam ‖1‖
The Puruṣa has a thousand heads, a thousand eyes, a thousand feet. Covering the earth on every side, he stood ten fingers’ breadth beyond it.
The Cosmic Person has countless heads, eyes and feet. He wraps the whole earth, and still reaches beyond it.
WordMeans
sahasraa thousand: here, countless
śīrṣa · akṣa · pādhead · eye · foot
puruṣaḥthe Person, the Cosmic Being
viśvataḥ vṛtvācovering on every side
ati atiṣṭhatstood beyond
daśāṅgulamten fingers’ breadth
Everyone’s heads are His. “A thousand” means beyond counting: every head that thinks, every eye that sees, every foot that walks belongs to the one Person. And He is not used up by the world: He fills it and stands beyond. “Ten fingers” is read by Sāyaṇa as “exceeding it”; some later teachers read it as the space of the heart, where He dwells in each of us.
He fills the whole world and still stands beyond it.

VERSE 2

ALL THIS IS HE
puruṣa evedaṃ sarvaṃyad bhūtaṃ yac ca bhavyam |utāmṛtatvasyeśānoyad annenātirohati ‖2‖
The Puruṣa alone is all this: whatever has been and whatever will be. He is the lord of immortality, and of all that grows by food.
Everything is the Cosmic Person: the past, the future, all of it. He rules over the deathless, and over everything that lives and grows by eating.
WordMeans
puruṣa evathe Person alone
idaṃ sarvamall this
yad bhūtamwhat has been
yac ca bhavyamand what will be
amṛtatvasya īśānaḥlord of immortality
annena atirohatigrows up by food
The most quoted line. “Puruṣa evedaṃ sarvam” is the seed of the Upaniṣadic “sarvaṃ khalv idaṃ brahma”, “all this is Brahman”. The hymn holds two worlds in one Lord: the mortal world that lives by food, and the immortal beyond it.
Past, present, future: all of it is He.

VERSE 3

ONE QUARTER IS THE WORLD
etāvān asya mahimāato jyāyāṃś ca pūruṣaḥ |pādo’sya viśvā bhūtānitripād asyāmṛtaṃ divi ‖3‖
Such is His greatness, and the Puruṣa is greater still. All beings are one quarter of Him; three quarters of Him are the immortal in heaven.
All this vast universe is only a small part of Him. Most of Him is beyond, deathless, in the highest heaven.
WordMeans
etāvānso great
mahimāgreatness
jyāyāngreater
pādaḥa quarter (literally a foot)
viśvā bhūtāniall beings
tripād amṛtamthree quarters, immortal
The world is the smaller part. All the galaxies are one “foot” of the Lord; the rest is beyond time and death. Later Vaiṣṇava teachers took this up: the material world is one part, the eternal realm (tripād-vibhūti, “the three-quarter glory”) the greater.
The whole universe is only a quarter of Him.

VERSE 4

SPREADING OUT
tripād ūrdhva ud ait puruṣaḥpādo’syehābhavat punaḥ |tato viṣvaṅ vy akrāmatsāśanānaśane abhi ‖4‖
With three quarters the Puruṣa rose up on high; one quarter of Him came to be here again and again. From there He spread out in every direction, into what eats and what does not eat.
Most of Him rose above; one part became this world, over and over. From it He spread everywhere: into living things that eat and into things that do not.
WordMeans
ūrdhvaḥ ud aitwent up on high
iha abhavat punaḥcame to be here again
viṣvaṅeverywhere, in all directions
vy akrāmatstrode out, spread
sāśanawith eating: living beings
anaśananot eating: the non-living
“Again and again.” The world is not made once; it comes and goes in cycles, and each time the Lord’s one quarter becomes it. Vy akrāmat, “He strode out”, uses the verb of Viṣṇu’s famous three strides.
One part of Him became everything, living and non-living.

VERSE 5

VIRĀJ, THE SHINING VASTNESS
tasmād virāḍ ajāyatavirājo adhi pūruṣaḥ |sa jāto aty aricyatapaścād bhūmim atho puraḥ ‖5‖
From Him Virāj was born; from Virāj, the Puruṣa. Once born, He reached beyond the earth, behind and in front.
From the Person came Virāj, the shining universe; and within Virāj the Person was born again, overflowing the earth on every side.
WordMeans
tasmātfrom Him
virāṭVirāj: the shining, vast one
ajāyatawas born
adhifrom, upon
aty aricyataexceeded, overflowed
paścāt · puraḥbehind · in front
Born from each other. The Puruṣa gives birth to Virāj, the cosmic body, and then is born within it as its living spirit. Sāyaṇa reads Virāj as the universe as one body, the “egg of Brahmā”, and the second Puruṣa as the soul that enlivens it.
He makes the universe, then lives inside it.

VERSE 6

THE SEASONS AS OFFERINGS
yat puruṣeṇa haviṣādevā yajñam atanvata |vasanto asyāsīd ājyaṃgrīṣma idhmaḥ śarad dhaviḥ ‖6‖
When the gods spread out the sacrifice with the Puruṣa as the offering, spring was its ghee, summer its firewood, autumn its oblation.
The gods held a great fire-ritual, and the offering was the Cosmic Person Himself. The seasons became its materials: spring the ghee, summer the fuel, autumn the offering.
WordMeans
puruṣeṇa haviṣāwith the Puruṣa as the offering
yajñam atanvataspread out the sacrifice
vasantaḥ · ājyamspring · ghee
grīṣmaḥ · idhmaḥsummer · firewood
śarad · haviḥautumn · oblation
Creation as a sacrifice. This is the hymn’s great image: the world comes from an act of giving. Time itself, the turning year, is the ritual. Every season “burns” to keep the world going.
The world began as a great act of giving.

VERSE 7

ON THE SACRED GRASS
taṃ yajñaṃ barhiṣi praukṣanpuruṣaṃ jātam agrataḥ |tena devā ayajantasādhyā ṛṣayaś ca ye ‖7‖
That sacrifice, the Puruṣa born in the beginning, they sprinkled on the sacred grass. With Him the gods sacrificed, and the Sādhyas, and the seers.
They consecrated the first-born Person on the sacred grass, and with Him the gods, the Sādhyas and the sages performed the sacrifice.
WordMeans
barhiṣion the sacred grass (barhis, kuśa)
praukṣanthey sprinkled, consecrated
jātam agrataḥborn first, in the beginning
ayajantathey sacrificed
sādhyāḥthe Sādhyas, an ancient class of gods
ṛṣayaḥthe seers
The sacrifice is the Person. Here yajña and puruṣa are the same: “that sacrifice, the Puruṣa”. Later texts say it outright: yajño vai viṣṇuḥ, “the sacrifice is Viṣṇu”. Every later ritual on kuśa grass re-enacts this first one.
The first sacrifice was the Lord Himself.

VERSE 8

THE CREATURES ARE MADE
tasmād yajñāt sarvahutaḥsambhṛtaṃ pṛṣadājyam |paśūn tāṃś cakre vāyavyānāraṇyān grāmyāś ca ye ‖8‖
From that sacrifice, offered whole, the clotted ghee was gathered. He made the creatures of the air, and those of the forest and of the village.
From that all-offering sacrifice came the rich ghee, and from it He made the birds, the wild animals and the tame.
WordMeans
sarvahutaḥfully offered, all-offered
sambhṛtamwas gathered
pṛṣad-ājyamclotted ghee, butter mixed with curd
vāyavyānof the air: birds
āraṇyānof the forest: wild
grāmyāḥof the village: domestic
Life from the offering. The ghee of the cosmic sacrifice becomes living creatures. Air, forest, village: the hymn sorts animals as the Vedic world saw them, wild and tame, and all of them come from the same Lord.
Every creature came from the same offering.

VERSE 9

THE VEDAS ARE BORN
tasmād yajñāt sarvahutaṛcaḥ sāmāni jajñire |chandāṃsi jajñire tasmādyajus tasmād ajāyata ‖9‖
From that sacrifice, offered whole, the verses (ṛc) and the chants (sāman) were born. The metres were born from it; from it the ritual formulas (yajus) were born.
The Vedas themselves came from that sacrifice: the hymns, the songs, the metres and the ritual formulas.
WordMeans
ṛcaḥverses: the Ṛg Veda
sāmānichants: the Sāma Veda
chandāṃsithe metres
yajuḥritual formulas: the Yajur Veda
jajñirewere born
Even the Veda is born. The sacred word is not separate from the Lord; it comes out of Him like everything else. The tradition takes this to mean the Vedas are not man-made (apauruṣeya), but “breathed out” by the Supreme.
The sacred word itself was born from Him.

VERSE 10

HORSES, COWS, GOATS, SHEEP
tasmād aśvā ajāyantaye ke cobhayādataḥ |gāvo ha jajñire tasmāttasmāj jātā ajāvayaḥ ‖10‖
From it were born horses, and all animals with teeth in both jaws. Cows were born from it; from it were born goats and sheep.
Horses and all animals with two rows of teeth, cows, goats and sheep: all came from that sacrifice.
WordMeans
aśvāḥhorses
ubhayādataḥwith teeth in both jaws
gāvaḥcows
ajāvayaḥgoats and sheep (ajā + avi)
ajāyanta · jajñirewere born
The animals of Vedic life. Horse, cow, goat, sheep: the wealth of the Vedic household. The verse sees even the herd and the stable as gifts from the one source.
The animals that sustain us are His gift.

VERSE 11

THE GREAT QUESTION
yat puruṣaṃ vy adadhuḥkatidhā vy akalpayan |mukhaṃ kim asya kau bāhūkā ūrū pādā ucyete ‖11‖
When they divided the Puruṣa, into how many parts did they arrange Him? What was His mouth? What were His two arms? What are His thighs and feet called?
When the gods shared out the Cosmic Person, how did they arrange Him? What became His mouth, His arms, His thighs, His feet?
WordMeans
vy adadhuḥthey divided, distributed
katidhāin how many ways?
vy akalpayanthey arranged
mukhammouth
bāhūthe two arms
ūrū · pādauthighs · feet
A riddle, Vedic style. The hymn now asks questions it will answer in the next three verses. One body, divided: the answers will map the parts of the Person onto society (verse 12) and the cosmos (verses 13–14).
One body, divided into many parts: which part became what?

VERSE 12

MOUTH, ARMS, THIGHS, FEET
brāhmaṇo’sya mukham āsīdbāhū rājanyaḥ kṛtaḥ |ūrū tad asya yad vaiśyaḥpadbhyāṃ śūdro ajāyata ‖12‖
The brāhmaṇa was His mouth; the rājanya was made His arms; His thighs became the vaiśya; from His feet the śūdra was born.
The four social orders of the Vedic world, priest, ruler, trader and worker, are pictured as mouth, arms, thighs and feet of one body.
WordMeans
brāhmaṇaḥthe priest-teacher
rājanyaḥthe ruler-warrior (later: kṣatriya)
vaiśyaḥthe farmer-trader
śūdraḥthe worker
mukham · bāhūmouth · arms
ūrū · padbhyāmthighs · from the feet
The most discussed verse in the Vedas. It names the four orders (varṇa) as parts of one body. It has been read in very different ways: as a picture of unity, where every part is needed; as a charter for hierarchy; and by many scholars as a later addition. The next page sets out the main views.
Four orders, pictured as one body: see the next page for how it is read.
This is the only verse of the Ṛg Veda that names all four orders together.

READING VERSE 12

The most debated verse in the Vedas: the main views, side by side
ViewHow it reads the verse
The plain senseFour social orders, pictured as mouth, arms, thighs and feet of one cosmic body. It is the only Ṛg Veda verse that names all four together.
One bodyMany traditional readers stress unity: every part belongs to the same Person and the body needs all of them. The same body gives the moon, the sun and the earth (verses 13–14).
Quality and workThe Gītā says the four orders were made “by division of qualities and work” (guṇa-karma-vibhāgaśaḥ, 4.13); many teachers read varṇa as nature and calling rather than birth.
A later additionMax Müller and many later scholars saw the hymn as late in its language and ideas, and the varṇa verse as inserted later. Some describe it as a “charter myth” for social order.
Original to the hymnOther scholars hold that the verse belongs to the hymn as composed. The question is still argued.

What is widely agreed. The later caste system, with fixed birth-groups and rules on marriage and eating together, grew far more elaborate and rigid than anything the Ṛg Veda describes. The Ṛg Veda itself never hints at such rules.

ZAPPY: So which reading is right?

PROF. BOLT: Scholars and teachers still disagree. What everyone can see: the hymn's main message is that all of it, every part, is one Person.

Read in many ways; one body, one Person is the hymn’s own frame.

VERSE 13

MOON FROM MIND, SUN FROM EYE
candramā manaso jātaścakṣoḥ sūryo ajāyata |mukhād indraś cāgniś caprāṇād vāyur ajāyata ‖13‖
The moon was born from His mind; from His eye the sun was born. From His mouth came Indra and Agni; from His breath Vāyu was born.
His mind became the moon, His eye the sun, His mouth Indra and fire, His breath the wind.
WordMeans
candramāḥ · manasaḥthe moon · from the mind
sūryaḥ · cakṣoḥthe sun · from the eye
indraḥ · agniḥIndra, king of gods · Agni, fire
vāyuḥ · prāṇātthe wind · from the breath
We and the cosmos are made alike. Mind and moon, eye and sun, breath and wind: the inner world mirrors the outer. The Upaniṣads build on exactly this matching of body (adhyātma) and universe (adhidaiva).
Your mind, eye and breath are kin to moon, sun and wind.

VERSE 14

THE THREE WORLDS
nābhyā āsīd antarikṣaṃśīrṣṇo dyauḥ sam avartata |padbhyāṃ bhūmir diśaḥ śrotrāttathā lokāṃ akalpayan ‖14‖
From His navel came the mid-air; from His head the sky came forth; from His feet the earth, from His ear the directions. So they fashioned the worlds.
His navel became the space between, His head the heavens, His feet the earth, His ears the four quarters. That is how the worlds were shaped.
WordMeans
nābhyāḥ · antarikṣamfrom the navel · mid-air
śīrṣṇaḥ · dyauḥfrom the head · the sky
padbhyām · bhūmiḥfrom the feet · the earth
śrotrāt · diśaḥfrom the ear · the directions
lokān akalpayanthey fashioned the worlds
The universe is His body. Sky, air and earth, the three Vedic worlds, are head, navel and feet. This is the root of the later viśvarūpa, the “universal form” that Kṛṣṇa shows Arjuna in the Gītā, and of Vaiṣṇava teaching that the world is the body of the Lord.
Sky, air and earth are the body of the Lord.

VERSE 15

SEVEN AND TWENTY-ONE
saptāsyāsan paridhayastriḥ sapta samidhaḥ kṛtāḥ |devā yad yajñaṃ tanvānāabadhnan puruṣaṃ paśum ‖15‖
Seven were its enclosing sticks; thrice seven fuel sticks were made, when the gods, spreading out the sacrifice, bound the Puruṣa as the victim.
The altar had seven boundary sticks and twenty-one sticks of firewood, when the gods performed the sacrifice with the Cosmic Person as its offering.
WordMeans
sapta paridhayaḥseven enclosing sticks
triḥ saptathree times seven
samidhaḥfuel sticks
tanvānāḥspreading out, performing
abadhnanthey bound
paśumas the victim, the offering
Numbers in ritual. Seven and twenty-one are sacred Vedic numbers. Sāyaṇa reads the seven enclosures as the seven metres, and the twenty-one fuel sticks as the twelve months, five seasons, three worlds and the sun. The point: the whole cosmos is the altar.
The whole cosmos is the altar.
In the Taittirīya recitation this verse comes earlier, right after verse 6.

VERSE 16

THE FIRST DHARMAS
yajñena yajñam ayajanta devāstāni dharmāṇi prathamāny āsan |te ha nākaṃ mahimānaḥ sacantayatra pūrve sādhyāḥ santi devāḥ ‖16‖
With the sacrifice the gods sacrificed to the sacrifice: these were the first dharmas. These mighty ones reached the vault of heaven, where the ancient Sādhyas, the gods, dwell.
The gods worshipped the Lord through the Lord Himself. That was the first law of the world. By it they reached heaven, where the first gods live.
WordMeans
yajñena yajñamby sacrifice, to sacrifice
dharmāṇi prathamānithe first dharmas, the first laws
nākamthe vault of heaven
mahimānaḥthe mighty ones
sacantareached, attained
pūrve sādhyāḥthe ancient Sādhyas
The circle closes. The offering, the offerer and the one offered to are all the same Lord. And that first sacrifice became the pattern, the dharma, for every act of worship since. Note the change of metre: this last verse is in the longer triṣṭubh, a grand closing.
Worship is the Lord, offered to the Lord, by the Lord.

THE TAITTIRĪYA VERSES

The version chanted in South Indian temples and homes

The Taittirīya Āraṇyaka (3.12–13) version changes the order a little (verse 15 follows verse 6) and adds verses. Two of the best-loved come before the final verse:

vedāham etaṃ puruṣaṃ mahāntamādityavarṇaṃ tamasas tu pāre |sarvāṇi rūpāṇi vicitya dhīraḥnāmāni kṛtvābhivadan yad āste ‖
I know this great Person, bright as the sun, beyond all darkness: the wise One who shaped every form, gave each its name, and abides, calling them.
dhātā purastād yam udājahāraśakraḥ pravidvān pradiśaś catasraḥ |tam evaṃ vidvān amṛta iha bhavatinānyaḥ panthā ayanāya vidyate ‖
Him whom the Creator proclaimed of old, and Indra, knowing, in all four directions: whoever knows Him thus becomes immortal here. There is no other path to go.

THE PURUṢA SŪKTAM ON ONE PAGE

Sixteen verses in five lines
Verses 1–5. The Person: countless heads, eyes, feet; all that was and will be; the world only a quarter of Him; from Him Virāj, and He within it.
Verses 6–7. The gods perform the first sacrifice with the Person as offering; the seasons are its ghee, fuel and oblation.
Verses 8–10. From it come the creatures, the Vedas and the animals of the home.
Verses 11–14. His body divided: society, moon and sun, Indra, Agni and wind, sky, air and earth.
Verses 15–16. The cosmos is the altar; the first sacrifice is the first dharma, the pattern of all worship.
