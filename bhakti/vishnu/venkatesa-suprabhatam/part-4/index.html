FROM PRAISE TO SURRENDER

So far, the Suprabhātam woke the Lord and the Stotram praised Him and pleaded with Him.

Part 4 is the third section, the Prapatti: sixteen verses of surrender. It begins with Lakṣmī, the Mother who brings us to Him; then twelve verses gaze only at His feet: their fragrance, colour, marks, softness, their place in the Veda and the Gītā. It ends with the poet's own teacher, and a prayer to serve the Lord forever, never for oneself.

Every verse from 2 to 15 ends with the same words: "śrī-veṅkaṭeśa-caraṇau śaraṇaṃ prapadye", "I take the feet of Śrī Veṅkaṭeśa as my refuge."

Govindā Govindā,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Twelve verses just about His feet?

PROF. BOLT: When you bow, the feet are what you see! The poet looks at them from every side, and each verse ends with the same surrender.

THE SHAPE OF THE PRAPATTI

Surrender in four steps
Verse 1: to the Mother. Surrender begins with Śrī, who lives on His chest and makes Him forgiving.
Verse 2: His qualities. All-knowing, all-powerful, loving, master, gracious, easy to reach: every doubt answered.
Verses 3–14: His feet. Fragrance (3), beyond lotuses (4), holy marks (5), colour (6), tenderness (7–8), the gods' crowns (9), the Veda (10), the Gītā (11), where they have stood (12), the hill's jewel (13), a mother's nectar (14).
Verses 15–16: teacher and service. My ācārya showed me these feet; may I serve You with Śrī, never for myself.

🔑 The Dvaya mantra

The refrain echoes the Śrīvaiṣṇava mantra of surrender: "śrīman nārāyaṇa caraṇau śaraṇaṃ prapadye · śrīmate nārāyaṇāya namaḥ".

🎵 The sound

Verse 1 in stately śārdūla-vikrīḍita; verses 2–16 in flowing vasantatilakā, like the Suprabhātam.

ZAPPY: What does prapatti actually mean?

PROF. BOLT: Falling at His feet: giving up the idea that we save ourselves, and taking the Lord alone as our way. The Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition calls it surrender.

VERSE 1 · GOING DEEPER

FIRST, TO THE MOTHER

Surrender begins with Her. Before surrendering at the Lord's feet, the devotee surrenders to Lakṣmī. In Śrīvaiṣṇava teaching she is the puruṣakāra, the one who brings the soul to the Lord (see Suprabhātam verse 3).

She makes Him patient. Tat-kṣānti-saṃvardhinī: "she who increases His forbearance." When the Lord might judge, the Mother pleads; His patience with us grows because of Her.

A grander metre. This one verse is in śārdūla-vikrīḍita, "the tiger's play", with long 19-syllable lines, fit for the Queen of the universe.

💡 Puruṣakāra

"The one who recommends": Lakṣmī's role in bringing souls to the Lord.

💡 Śārdūlavikrīḍita

A stately 19-syllable metre, "the tiger's play".

Go to the Lord through the Mother. Her love opens His mercy.

ZAPPY: Why start the surrender with Lakṣmī?

PROF. BOLT: She's the mother who speaks for us. She even makes the Lord more patient with our faults!

Surrender starts with the Mother, who makes Him forgiving.

VERSE 2

I TAKE REFUGE AT HIS FEET
śrīman kṛpājalanidhekṛtasarvalokasarvajña śakta natavatsalasarvaśeṣin |svāmin suśīla sulabha-āśritapārijātaśrīveṅkaṭeśacaraṇauśaraṇaṃ prapadye ‖2‖

VERSE 2 · GOING DEEPER

I TAKE REFUGE AT HIS FEET

The refrain of surrender. From here every verse ends śrī-veṅkaṭeśa-caraṇau śaraṇaṃ prapadye, "I take the feet of Śrī Veṅkaṭeśa as my refuge". It echoes the first half of the Śrīvaiṣṇava Dvaya mantra, "śrīman nārāyaṇa caraṇau śaraṇaṃ prapadye".

Why these qualities? In the tradition's reading, they answer our doubts: He knows our need (sarvajña), can help (śakta), loves us despite faults (vatsala), owns us (svāmin), mixes with the lowly (suśīla) and is easy to reach (sulabha).

Prapatti. The word means "falling at" (the feet): surrender.

💡 Dvaya mantra

The two-line Śrīvaiṣṇava mantra of surrender to Śrīman Nārāyaṇa.

💡 Prapatti

Surrender: taking the Lord alone as the means of salvation.

Every doubt about surrender is answered by one of His qualities.

ZAPPY: Why list His qualities before surrendering?

PROF. BOLT: Each one answers a worry: does He know? Can He help? Will He mind my faults? Is He too far? No, no, no, no!

"Śaraṇaṃ prapadye": I take His feet as my refuge.

VERSE 3 · GOING DEEPER

FEET THAT SCENT THE FLOWERS

The feet begin. Verses 3–14 describe only the Lord's feet, from many angles: their fragrance, colour, marks, softness, glory in the Vedas. It is like a devotee bowing and looking only at the feet.

Fragrance to flowers. Usually flowers scent what they touch; here the feet give their fragrance to the flowers.

Ever new. Sadā anubhavane'pi navānubhāvyau: seen every day, yet new every time. Anyone who has stood before a loved image knows this.

💡 Pāda-sevā

Service at the Lord's feet: one of the nine forms of devotion.

💡 Nūpura

An anklet; the Lord's feet are adorned with them.

What is truly loved never grows old.

ZAPPY: Feet that make flowers smell better?

PROF. BOLT: That's the poet's joy: the flowers get their scent from Him, not the other way round.

Feet that make flowers sweeter: ever new, however often seen.
Some texts read ānūpurārpita for ānūpurārcita.

VERSE 4 · GOING DEEPER

NO LOTUS COMPARES

Beyond comparison. Indian poetry always compares beautiful feet to lotuses. The poet says: with these feet, that comparison is a "rash statement", an overreach.

Writing it down. Vilekhayantau, "causing to be written": the feet themselves enter the lotus-comparison into the list of rash claims. A witty, learned image.

Freshest lotus. Even the best lotus, just opened, deep red, fully fragrant, cannot match them.

💡 Upamā

Simile: the comparison at the heart of Sanskrit poetics.

💡 Vyatireka

A figure of speech where the subject surpasses its comparison.

Words fall short of the divine. Even the best comparison is only a pointer.

ZAPPY: A lotus isn't good enough?

PROF. BOLT: Not even the freshest, reddest one! Calling His feet "lotus-like" sells them short.

No lotus compares to His feet.

VERSE 5 · GOING DEEPER

THE MARKS ON HIS SOLES

Marks of the Supreme. Tradition holds that the Lord's soles bear auspicious signs (cihna), like the marks palmists look for, but divine. They prove that these are the feet of the Supreme Reality (para-tattva).

Each sign a promise. The banner of victory, the nectar of immortality, the parasol of protection, the goad that guides the mind, the wish-tree, and the conch and discus of Viṣṇu.

Seen in images. Marks of this kind are often shown on the Lord's footprints (pādukā) and on paintings of His feet.

💡 Pāda-cihna

Auspicious marks on a divine being's soles.

💡 Pādukā

Sandals or footprints of the Lord or a guru, worshipped in their place.

Every sign on His feet is a promise to the one who holds them.

ZAPPY: Marks on the soles of His feet?

PROF. BOLT: Holy signs: flag, nectar-pot, parasol, conch, discus. They show these are the Supreme's feet.

Banner, parasol, conch, discus: the signs of the Supreme on His soles.
Lists of the sole-marks differ between texts.

VERSE 6 · GOING DEEPER

BETTER THAN RUBIES AND SAPPHIRES

Three colours. The soles are red, the tops dark blue like the Lord's body, and the toenails white and shining. Each beats a precious thing: ruby, sapphire, moon.

Jewels and the Lord. The poet uses the greatest treasures of the world only to show they are less than His feet.

Nakha-candrikā. "Moonlight of the toenails" is a favourite image in Indian devotional poetry.

💡 Padmarāga

"Lotus-red": the Sanskrit name for ruby.

💡 Indranīla

"Indra's blue": sapphire.

The world's treasures are bright; the Lord's feet are brighter.

ZAPPY: Brighter than the moon?

PROF. BOLT: His toenails are! Red soles beat rubies, blue tops beat sapphires. The poet is clearly in love.

Ruby, sapphire, moon: all dim beside His feet.

VERSE 7

His feet are so soft that even Lakṣmī's gentle hands, pressing them with love and care, seem to tire them. Their tenderness is beyond words. I surrender at those feet.

VERSE 7 · GOING DEEPER

TOO TENDER EVEN FOR LAKṢMĪ'S HANDS

Love and fear. Lakṣmī presses His feet "with love and fear": love for Him, and fear that even her soft hands might hurt. A tender domestic picture.

Then how did He walk? The devotee's heart aches: these same feet walked forests as Rāma and herded cows as Kṛṣṇa. The tradition loves this paradox (see verse 12).

Beyond word and mind. The Upaniṣads use avāṅmanasa-gocara for the Absolute; here it describes the softness of His feet.

💡 Saukumārya

Delicacy, tenderness: one of the Lord's bodily beauties.

💡 Saṃvāhana

Gently pressing the feet: an act of loving service.

Serve gently. Even the Mother serves the Lord with love and care.

ZAPPY: Lakṣmī's hands are too rough?

PROF. BOLT: Not rough, just that His feet are so tender! She presses them with love and a little fear.

Feet so tender even Lakṣmī's hands are careful.

VERSE 8 · GOING DEEPER

RED FROM THE QUEENS' HANDS

A poet's explanation. Why are the soles so red? Because His queens' rosy hands have touched them so often that the colour has passed over. Kila, "it seems", marks this as a poetic fancy.

Three consorts. Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition names three principal consorts of Viṣṇu: Śrī (Lakṣmī), Bhū (Earth) and Nīlā, identified in the Tamil tradition with Nappinnai, Kṛṣṇa's beloved among the cowherds.

Connected to verse 7. There, Lakṣmī massaged His feet; here all the queens do.

💡 Nīlā Devī

The third consort of Viṣṇu in Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition; Nappinnai in Tamil.

💡 Utprekṣā

Poetic fancy: imagining a cause, here for the feet's colour.

Love leaves its colour on what it serves.

ZAPPY: Why are His feet red?

PROF. BOLT: The poet's fancy: the queens' rosy hands served them so often that the colour rubbed off!

Red with the touch of His queens' loving hands.

VERSE 9 · GOING DEEPER

THE GODS' CROWNS WAVE THE LAMP

An āratī of light. At the end of worship a lamp is waved before the Lord (nīrājana, āratī). Here the gods bow so constantly that the light from their crown-jewels becomes that āratī.

Nava-ratna. The nine gems: ruby, pearl, coral, emerald, yellow sapphire, diamond, blue sapphire, hessonite and cat's-eye, each linked with one of the nine planets.

Gods at His feet. As in Suprabhātam verses 14 and 16, the greatest gods are His devotees.

💡 Nīrājana

Waving a lit lamp before the deity: āratī.

💡 Nava-ratna

The nine gems, each tied to one of the nine planets.

When you bow fully, your best becomes an offering of light.

ZAPPY: The crowns do the āratī?

PROF. BOLT: The gods bow so often that their crown-jewels sparkle on His feet, like a waving lamp!

The gods' bowing crowns become a lamp of worship.

VERSE 10

PRAISED IN THE VEDA, SHOWN BY HIS HAND

Feet praised in the words "the highest step of Viṣṇu"; feet held to be sweet, as "a spring of honey"; and pointed out again by the palm of Your hand, as if to say "it is so": I take the feet of Śrī Veṅkaṭeśa as my refuge.
The Veda praises His feet as "Viṣṇu's highest step" and "a spring of honey"; and the Lord's own hand points to them, as if to say, "Yes, this is true." I surrender at those feet.

VERSE 10 · GOING DEEPER

PRAISED IN THE VEDA, SHOWN BY HIS HAND

Two Vedic quotations. Both phrases come from one hymn of the Ṛg Veda (1.154.5), which says that in the highest step of Viṣṇu there is a spring of honey. The poet reads this as the Lord's feet.

The pointing hand. The image at Tirumala holds the lower right hand pointing down to the feet. Tradition reads it as the Lord saying "surrender here"; the poet says it confirms the Veda: "it is so".

Waist-deep. A popular reading of the lower left hand on the hip: "the ocean of saṃsāra is only this deep for those who hold My feet".

💡 Ṛg Veda 1.154

The hymn to Viṣṇu's three strides, with "the highest step" and "a spring of honey".

💡 Varada-hasta

The hand gesture pointing down to the feet, at Tirumala.

The Lord Himself points the way: to His feet.

ZAPPY: His hand points to His feet?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, look at the image at Tirumala! The right hand points down: "Hold these. The Veda is right."

The Veda praises them; His own hand points to them.

VERSE 11

The very feet You showed to Arjuna, You who became his fitting charioteer, saying "take refuge"; those same feet of Yours You show to me again, here, with Your hand: I take the feet of Śrī Veṅkaṭeśa as my refuge.
On the battlefield, as Arjuna's charioteer, You told him: "Take refuge in Me." Here at Tirumala You point to the same feet for me. I surrender at those feet.

VERSE 11 · GOING DEEPER

"TAKE REFUGE": TO ARJUNA, AND TO ME

The Gītā's last word. In Bhagavad Gītā 18.66, Kṛṣṇa tells Arjuna: give up all other ways, take refuge in Me alone, and I will free you. The Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition calls it the Carama Śloka, the final verse.

Then and now. The poet joins Kurukṣetra and Tirumala: what Kṛṣṇa said in words, Veṅkaṭeśa says with His hand, pointing to His feet.

Fitting charioteer. Tat-sadṛśa: Kṛṣṇa became a humble driver worthy of His friend. The Lord of all serves His devotee.

💡 Carama Śloka

Gītā 18.66: "Take refuge in Me alone; I will free you."

💡 Pārtha

"Son of Pṛthā": Arjuna.

The promise made to Arjuna is made to every one of us.

ZAPPY: Like the Gītā?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly: "take refuge in Me". Kṛṣṇa said it to Arjuna; Veṅkaṭeśa shows it to us with His hand.

Told to Arjuna, shown to me: "take refuge".

VERSE 12

VERSE 12 · GOING DEEPER

WHERE HIS FEET HAVE STOOD

A journey of the feet. Kṛṣṇa danced on the hood of the serpent Kāliya; Rāma walked barefoot through the forests in exile; the Lord stands on Veṅkaṭa hill; the Upaniṣads ("head of the Vedas") rest on Him; and He lives in loving hearts.

"On my head" first. The devotee puts himself first in the list, alongside a serpent and the Vedas: grace treats all alike (samam).

Recalling verse 7. Feet too tender for Lakṣmī's hands walked rough forests: the tradition marvels at this love.

💡 Kāliya

The many-headed serpent in the Yamunā, subdued by young Kṛṣṇa dancing on his hoods.

💡 Śruti-śiras

"Head of the Vedas": the Upaniṣads.

His feet rest equally on the great and the small. Offer your head too.

ZAPPY: On a serpent and on my head, the same?

PROF. BOLT: That's His grace: Kāliya, the forests, the Vedas, our hearts. He treats them all alike.

On Kāliya, on the Vedas, and on my head.

VERSE 13

Your feet, which scatter unfading, joyful flowers over the earth; which are the jewel adorning the peak of Śrī Veṅkaṭa hill; which delight every mind and every eye: I take the feet of Śrī Veṅkaṭeśa as my refuge.
Wherever His feet step, fresh, happy flowers seem to spring up; they are the jewel on top of Veṅkaṭa hill, and every heart and eye rejoices in them. I surrender at those feet.

VERSE 13 · GOING DEEPER

THE HILL'S OWN ORNAMENT

Flowers where He walks. The ground blooms at the touch of His feet, and the flowers never fade.

The hill's jewel. A crown is the ornament of a head; His feet are the crown-jewel of the hill's peak. So the hill is honoured by Him, as Suprabhātam verse 15 honoured its names.

For all eyes. Ānandita-akhila-mano-nayanau: all minds and all eyes, with no exceptions.

💡 Ābharaṇa

An ornament, jewellery.

💡 Śikhara

A peak; also the tower crowning a temple.

Where the Lord stands, even rock becomes a garden.

ZAPPY: Flowers spring up where He steps?

PROF. BOLT: In the poem, yes, and they never fade. His feet are the hill's jewel.

Flowers where He steps: the jewel on the hill's crown.

VERSE 14

LIKE A MOTHER TO A CHILD

Feet which those who surrender reach for first, as a rule; which are like nectar to them, as a mother's breast is to her baby; matched only with each other, and with nothing else: I take the feet of Śrī Veṅkaṭeśa as my refuge.
Those who surrender go first to His feet, as a baby goes to its mother for milk. Only one foot can be compared to the other; nothing else compares. I surrender at those feet.

VERSE 14 · GOING DEEPER

LIKE A MOTHER TO A CHILD

The baby knows. A newborn needs no teaching to find its mother; the surrendered soul turns to His feet just as naturally. And as milk is life to the baby, the feet are nectar to the devotee.

Only each other. In verse 4 no lotus could compare; here the poet concludes: the right foot can be compared only to the left, and nothing else.

Two in all. The verses on the feet always say caraṇau, dual: the two feet, a pair, like the Lord and Lakṣmī.

💡 Prapanna

One who has surrendered to the Lord.

💡 Ananvaya

The figure of speech where something is compared only to itself.

Come to Him like a child to its mother: without reasons, without fear.

ZAPPY: Compared only to each other?

PROF. BOLT: Nothing else is good enough: not lotuses, not jewels. The left foot matches the right, and that's it!

Nectar to the surrendered, like a mother to her child.

My teacher, the gentle Maṇavāḷa Māmuni, whose kind glance carries people across the sea of life, showed me Your feet. I surrender at those feet.

VERSE 15 · GOING DEEPER

SHOWN TO ME BY MY TEACHER

The guru. Saumyopayantṛ-muni is a Sanskrit rendering of the Tamil name Maṇavāḷa Māmuni ("bridegroom-sage", Saumya Jāmātṛ Muni): the poet's own teacher, who asked him to compose this hymn.

Through the ācārya. In Śrīvaiṣṇava practice, one comes to the Lord's feet through one's teacher. Prapatti is usually performed under an ācārya's guidance.

A glance that saves. Dayārdra-dṛgañcala: the merest sidelong look of the compassionate teacher is enough to carry one across.

💡 Maṇavāḷa Māmuni

A 14th–15th-century Śrīvaiṣṇava teacher, revered as a later form of Rāmānuja.

💡 Ācārya

A spiritual teacher who guides the disciple to the Lord.

Thank the one who showed you the way. Gratitude to the teacher is part of the path.

ZAPPY: Who is this sage?

PROF. BOLT: The poet's own guru, Maṇavāḷa Māmuni. He's saying: my teacher showed me these feet.

My teacher showed me His feet.

VERSE 16 · GOING DEEPER

YOUR SERVANT, NEVER MY OWN

The whole teaching in one verse. Śrī brings the soul to Him (ghaṭikā, the one who unites); He alone is the means (upāya); He, with Śrī, is the goal (upeya). The poet's wish: to serve Him forever.

"Never for myself." The second half of the Dvaya mantra ends śrīmate nārāyaṇāya namaḥ; tradition reads namaḥ as na mama, "not mine". "Na jātu mahyam" says the same: service with no self in it.

The Prapatti's arc. It began with Śrī (verse 1), passed through His feet (2–14) and the teacher (15), and ends in selfless service: kaiṅkarya.

💡 Upāya · Upeya

The means and the goal: in prapatti, both are the Lord.

💡 Kaiṅkarya

Loving service, the Śrīvaiṣṇava's highest aim.

Serve without keeping anything back for yourself. That is surrender.

ZAPPY: "Never for myself"?

PROF. BOLT: Even our service should not be about us. Not "I gain", only "He is pleased". That's the end of the Prapatti.

"Syāṃ kiṅkaraḥ": may I serve You, never for myself.

The Prapatti in five lines
