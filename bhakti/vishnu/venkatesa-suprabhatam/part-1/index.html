FOREWORD

"Kausalyā suprajā rāma…" For millions of Indian homes, this is the sound of dawn. Before the milk boils and the day begins, M. S. Subbulakshmi's voice wakes Lord Veṅkaṭeśvara, and wakes the household with Him.

The Śrī Veṅkaṭeśa Suprabhātam is sung every morning at Tirumala, before the golden door of the sanctum, as the Lord is woken. It begins with a verse from the Rāmāyaṇa, wakes the Goddess, and then shows the whole world rising to greet the Lord of the Seven Hills: sages with lotuses, gods singing, parrots reciting, bees humming, the cowherd homes churning butter.

The full hymn has four sections and seventy verses. We take it in five parts. This first part covers verses 1–15 of the Suprabhātam proper.

THE STORY · A MORNING CALL, 600 YEARS OLD

From Viśvāmitra's call to the golden door of Tirumala
The story in 5 frames
The first morning call. In the Rāmāyaṇa, the sage Viśvāmitra wakes the young Rāma in the forest: "Kausalyā suprajā rāma…" Every suprabhātam begins from this one verse.
A disciple's task. In the early 15th century, the Śrīvaiṣṇava teacher Maṇavāḷa Māmuni asks his disciple Prativādi Bhayaṅkaram Aṇṇan of Kāñcīpuram to compose a dawn hymn for the Lord of Tirumala.
Tamil roots. Aṇṇan draws on the Tamil dawn songs of the Āḻvārs, Toṇḍaraḍippoḍi's Tiruppaḷḷiyeḻucci and Āṇḍāḷ's Tiruppāvai, and writes in Sanskrit.
Four sections. He writes the Suprabhātam (29 verses), the Stotram (11), the Prapatti (16) and the Maṅgalāśāsanam (14): 70 in all.
Every dawn since. It is sung daily at Tirumala before the golden door, and M. S. Subbulakshmi's recording carried it into homes everywhere.

ZAPPY: Six hundred years, every single morning?

PROF. BOLT: That's the tradition: sung at the Lord's door each dawn. And for most of us, MS Amma's voice is what we hear.

ABOUT THE SUPRABHĀTAM

A royal wake-up for the Lord of the Seven Hills

✍️ The author

Prativādi Bhayaṅkaram Aṇṇan (also Anantācārya), a Śrīvaiṣṇava scholar of Kāñcīpuram and disciple of Maṇavāḷa Māmuni; composed around 1420–1432.

🎵 The sound

Mostly in vasantatilakā, "ornament of spring": fourteen syllables a line, a flowing metre loved for dawn hymns.

The genre. A suprabhāta, "auspicious dawn", is a hymn sung to wake a deity, treating the Lord as a beloved king whose court gathers at sunrise. The Veṅkaṭeśa Suprabhātam is the most famous of all.

The refrains. Most verses end "śeṣādri-śekhara-vibho tava suprabhātam" or "śrī-veṅkaṭācala-pate tava suprabhātam": "Lord of Śeṣādri / Lord of Veṅkaṭa hill, a good morning to You!"

VERSES 1–2 · GOING DEEPER

WAKE UP, O RĀMA; WAKE UP, GOVINDA

From the Rāmāyaṇa. Verse 1 is not by Aṇṇan at all: it is Viśvāmitra waking the young Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa in the forest, in the Bālakāṇḍa of Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa. The whole suprabhāta genre grows from this one verse.

From Rāma to Veṅkaṭeśa. Verse 2 turns the same call to Govinda, the Lord of the Seven Hills. The tradition sees Śrīnivāsa as the same Viṣṇu who was Rāma and Kṛṣṇa.

Why wake God? The Lord never sleeps; His "yoga-nidrā" is a loving fiction. Waking Him is really waking ourselves into the day with Him.

💡 Viśvāmitra

The royal sage who took young Rāma to the forest and taught him divine weapons.

💡 Āhnika

The daily duties: morning prayer, sandhyā, worship.

Begin every day as if you were waking God, and you will find you have woken yourself.

ZAPPY: The first verse is from the Rāmāyaṇa?

PROF. BOLT: Yes! Viśvāmitra waking young Rāma. Every suprabhātam since has grown from that single morning call.

"Kausalyā suprajā rāma": a sage's morning call that became India's dawn prayer.

VERSE 3 · GOING DEEPER

GOOD MORNING, MOTHER

Mother first. Before waking the Lord, the hymn wakes the Goddess. In the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition, Lakṣmī is the puruṣakāra, the one who recommends the devotee to the Lord: we approach Him through Her.

On His chest. Lakṣmī lives on Viṣṇu's chest, the mark called Śrīvatsa. At Tirumala she is honoured as Padmāvatī, with her own temple at Tiruchanur below the hills.

Madhu and Kaiṭabha. Two demons born from Viṣṇu's ears during His cosmic sleep, slain by Him: a fitting reference in a hymn about waking.

💡 Puruṣakāra

"The one who intercedes": Lakṣmī's role in Śrīvaiṣṇava theology.

💡 Padmāvatī

Lakṣmī as the consort of Śrīnivāsa, worshipped at Tiruchanur.

Approach the Lord through the Mother: compassion opens the door that justice might keep shut.

ZAPPY: Why wake the Goddess first?

PROF. BOLT: In this tradition we go to the Lord through Lakṣmī. She is the mother who speaks for her children.

First, good morning to the Mother who speaks for us.

VERSE 4 · GOING DEEPER

LOTUS-EYED, MOON-FACED

A different rhythm. This verse is in a different metre from the rest, and it begins with the refrain the others end with: tava suprabhātam, "good morning to You".

The goddesses' goddess. Sarasvatī, Pārvatī and Śacī, the consorts of Brahmā, Śiva and Indra, come to worship Lakṣmī: she is honoured even by the other goddesses.

Vṛṣaśaila. "The bull hill", one of the seven names of Tirumala (see verse 15).

💡 Seven hills

Tirumala's seven peaks give Śrīnivāsa the name Saptagirīśa, "Lord of the seven hills".

💡 Dayā

Compassion: the quality the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition most associates with Lakṣmī.

The one who is honoured by all is the one who is kind to all.

ZAPPY: Other goddesses worship Lakṣmī?

PROF. BOLT: In this verse, yes. Sarasvatī, Pārvatī and Indra's queen all come to her at dawn.

Lotus-eyed, moon-faced, an ocean of compassion.

VERSE 5 · GOING DEEPER

THE SEVEN SAGES BRING LOTUSES

The refrain begins. From here every verse ends: śeṣādri-śekhara-vibho tava suprabhātam, "O Lord on the crest of Śeṣādri, a good morning to You!"

Two rivers. Ākāśa-sindhu is the heavenly Gaṅgā; it is also the Ākāśa Gaṅgā waterfall near Tirumala, from which water is brought for the Lord's daily bath. The poet means both.

Śeṣādri. The hill is said to be Ādiśeṣa, Viṣṇu's serpent, lying as the range; the Lord stands on its hood.

💡 Saptarṣi

The seven sages: in one list Atri, Bhṛgu, Kutsa, Vasiṣṭha, Gautama, Kaśyapa, Aṅgiras.

💡 Ādiśeṣa

The cosmic serpent on whom Viṣṇu rests; the hills are its body.

Bring your best flowers, but finish your own prayers first.

ZAPPY: Is Ākāśa Gaṅgā a real place?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, a waterfall near the temple. Its water is still carried for the Lord's bath, and the poet plays on both meanings.

The sages bring lotuses: good morning, Lord of Śeṣādri.

VERSE 6 · GOING DEEPER

THE GODS SING; THE ALMANAC IS READ

All the gods. Śiva, Brahmā, Kārttikeya and Indra stand at the Lord's door praising Him. The hymn is generous: other gods are not rivals but courtiers at His dawn.

Trivikrama. Viṣṇu as Vāmana, the dwarf who grew and measured the worlds in three steps.

The pañcāṅga. Vāsara-śuddhi: Bṛhaspati, "lord of speech", reads out the day's details, tithi, vāra, nakṣatra, yoga and karaṇa, just as the temple priests still read the almanac to the Lord each morning at Tirumala.

💡 Pañcāṅga

The Hindu almanac with five limbs: tithi, vāra, nakṣatra, yoga, karaṇa.

💡 Trivikrama

Viṣṇu's form of the three strides, after the Vāmana avatāra.

Every day is worth announcing: know what day it is, and offer it.

ZAPPY: Why read an almanac to God?

PROF. BOLT: It's like a king being told the day's news. At Tirumala the pañcāṅgam is still read to the Lord after the Suprabhātam.

The gods sing, the day is announced: good morning, Lord.

VERSE 7 · GOING DEEPER

THE MORNING BREEZE

Nature joins in. From verse 7 to 12, the whole of nature wakes up to greet the Lord: breeze, birds, bees, cowherd homes, drums.

Half-open. Īṣat-praphulla, "slightly opened": lotuses open with the sun, so at dawn they are only half-open. The poet catches the exact moment.

Temple gardens. Tirumala's groves of coconut and areca, and its lotus tanks, are part of the scene: this is a real place described lovingly.

💡 Sarasīruha

"Born in a lake": one of many Sanskrit words for lotus.

💡 Malaya breeze

Indian poetry loves the gentle, fragrant southern breeze of dawn.

Notice the morning: the breeze, the flowers, the light. Worship can begin with attention.

ZAPPY: Even the breeze says good morning?

PROF. BOLT: In this hymn, everything does: the wind, the flowers, the birds. The whole world wakes for Him.

The fragrant breeze of dawn wakes the Lord too.

VERSE 8 · GOING DEEPER

THE PARROTS RECITE

A homely scene. Parrots were kept in temples and palaces and taught to repeat sacred names. The poet shows them waking, having breakfast, and starting to "chant".

Prasāda. The bananas and pāyasam are left over from the previous night's offerings: even the parrots eat the Lord's prasāda.

Śuka. The parrot is also the sage Śuka, who recited the Bhāgavatam: a gentle hint that every voice, even a bird's, can praise God.

💡 Prasāda

Food offered to the Lord and then shared as a blessing.

💡 Śuka

The parrot-named sage who recited the Bhāgavata Purāṇa to King Parīkṣit.

Even a parrot that only repeats can praise God. Let your repetition become remembrance.

ZAPPY: Parrots chanting?

PROF. BOLT: Temple parrots really were taught names of God. Here they wake up, eat their pāyasam, and start their morning chant!

Even the temple parrots wake up chanting.

VERSE 9 · GOING DEEPER

NĀRADA'S VĪṆĀ

Nārada. The wandering sage-musician, always singing "Nārāyaṇa", carrying news between the worlds. He is the model of devotion through music.

Music as worship. Verse 8 had parrots reciting; verse 9 brings a master musician. From the simplest voice to the finest art, all praise the Lord.

Ananta. Ananta-carita can mean "endless deeds" or "the deeds of the Endless One": both fit.

💡 Nārada

The divine sage-musician, author of the Nārada Bhakti Sūtras.

💡 Vīṇā

India's classical stringed instrument; Nārada's is called Mahatī.

Use whatever gift you have, a voice, an instrument, a craft, as an offering.

ZAPPY: Nārada is there too?

PROF. BOLT: With his vīṇā, singing. The poet gathers every kind of praise: gods, sages, birds and bees.

Nārada's vīṇā sings the endless story of the Lord.

VERSE 10

THE BEES COME TO SERVE
bhṛṅgāvalī camakarandarasānuviddhājhaṅkāragītaninadaiḥsaha sevanāya |niryātyupāntasarasī-kamalodarebhyaḥśeṣādriśekharavibhotava suprabhātam ‖10‖
Swarms of bees, steeped in the nectar of the flowers, come out from the hearts of the lotuses in the nearby lakes with their humming songs, to serve You. O Lord of Śeṣādri's crest, good morning!
Bees full of nectar fly out of the lotuses humming, as if coming to serve You. Good morning, Lord!

VERSE 10 · GOING DEEPER

THE BEES COME TO SERVE

Trapped all night. Poets love the idea that bees, caught inside lotuses when they close at dusk, are freed when the flowers open at dawn. So the bees' appearance means sunrise.

Humming as song. Their buzzing (jhaṅkāra) becomes a hymn, and their flight becomes service (sevā).

Sevā. The word for service is the same used for the devotees' darśan at Tirumala today: every visit is "sevā".

💡 Sevā

Service, worship: at Tirumala, each ritual darśan is a sevā.

💡 Makaranda

Flower nectar: a favourite image of sweetness in devotional poetry.

Even our everyday hum of activity can become service, if we turn it toward the Lord.

ZAPPY: The bees are serving God?

PROF. BOLT: In the poet's eyes. They leave the lotuses humming at dawn, and he hears a hymn.

Humming bees at dawn come out to serve the Lord.
Some texts read anuviddha for anuviddhā; the meaning is the same.

VERSE 11

CHURNING IN THE COWHERD HOMES
yoṣāgaṇena varadadhnivimathyamāneghoṣālayeṣu dadhi-manthanatīvraghoṣāḥ |roṣātkaliṃ vidadhatekakubhaśca kumbhāḥśeṣādriśekharavibhotava suprabhātam ‖11‖
As groups of women churn the fine curd in the cowherd homes, the loud sound of the churning seems to make the pots quarrel in anger with the directions, which echo it back. O Lord of Śeṣādri's crest, good morning!
In the cowherd homes the women are churning curd, and the noise is so loud the pots seem to argue with the echoing hills. Good morning, Lord!

VERSE 11 · GOING DEEPER

CHURNING IN THE COWHERD HOMES

A Gokula morning. The scene is straight out of Kṛṣṇa's Vṛndāvana: gopīs churning butter at dawn. The poet brings that world to Tirumala.

A playful image. The pots make so much noise that the four directions shout back: the echo becomes an argument. A little comedy in the middle of prayer.

Śrīvaiṣṇava sources. Āṇḍāḷ's Tiruppāvai has a similar dawn verse about churning in the cowherd village: the hymn draws on the Tamil Āḻvārs.

💡 Ghoṣa

Both "cowherd village" and "loud sound": the poet plays on the word.

💡 Tiruppāvai

Āṇḍāḷ's Tamil dawn songs, a key inspiration for this Suprabhātam.

Ordinary work done at dawn, churning, cooking, sweeping, is part of the world waking to God.

ZAPPY: Why mention butter churning?

PROF. BOLT: It's Kṛṣṇa's world, the cowherd village. And the noise is so loud the hills echo back as if arguing!

The clatter of churning is part of the Lord's morning music.

VERSE 12

BEES LIKE DRUMS
padmeśamitraśatapatra-gatālivargāḥhartuṃ śriyaṃ kuvalayasyanijāṅgalakṣmyā |bherīninādamivabibhrati tīvranādaṃśeṣādriśekharavibhotava suprabhātam ‖12‖
The swarms of bees in the lotuses, friends of the sun, make a loud sound like the beating of drums, as if, with the dark beauty of their own bodies, they mean to steal the beauty of the blue lily. O Lord of Śeṣādri's crest, good morning!
Dark bees in the sun-opened lotuses buzz as loudly as drums, as if competing with the dark blue lilies in beauty. Good morning, Lord!

VERSE 12 · GOING DEEPER

BEES LIKE DRUMS

A riddle of beauty. The bees are dark; the blue lily (kuvalaya) is dark too. The poet imagines the bees jealous, beating drums as if going to war to steal the lily's beauty.

Sun and lotus. The sun is called padmeśa, "lord of the lotus", because lotuses open at sunrise. So the bees sit in the sun's "friends".

Temple drums. The bees' "drums" echo the real drums (bherī) of the temple at dawn.

💡 Kuvalaya

The blue water-lily, which opens at night and closes at dawn.

💡 Bherī

A large temple kettle-drum, beaten at dawn rituals.

Nature is full of small dramas. A poet sees them, and turns them into praise.

ZAPPY: Bees beating drums?

PROF. BOLT: Their buzzing is so loud it sounds like the temple drums. The poet makes it a little battle of beauty with the lilies.

Bees drum at dawn: even nature's little rivalries praise Him.

VERSE 13 · GOING DEEPER

ŚRĪNIVĀSA, FRIEND OF ALL

A new refrain. From here to verse 29, every verse ends: śrī-veṅkaṭācala-pate tava suprabhātam, "O Lord of the Veṅkaṭa hill, good morning".

Śrīnivāsa. The Lord's best-loved name: "the dwelling of Śrī". Lakṣmī lives on His chest; so the Lord and the Goddess are never apart, a central point of Śrīvaiṣṇava faith.

Friend of all. Akhila-loka-bandhu: not only of the pious; the verse sets no conditions.

💡 Śrīnivāsa

"Abode of Śrī": the Lord of Tirumala, also Veṅkaṭeśvara, Bālājī, Govinda.

💡 Vyūha Lakṣmī

At Tirumala Lakṣmī is worshipped on the image's chest.

The Lord who keeps Lakṣmī in His heart keeps us there too.

ZAPPY: Why is He called Śrīnivāsa?

PROF. BOLT: Because Śrī, Lakṣmī, lives on His chest. Where He is, she is: grace and power together.

Śrīnivāsa: Lakṣmī's home, the friend of all.

VERSE 14

THE GODS WAIT AT THE DOOR

VERSE 14 · GOING DEEPER

THE GODS WAIT AT THE DOOR

A Tirumala crowd. Anyone who has queued at Tirumala recognises this: devotees bathe in the Svāmi Puṣkariṇī, then wait at the golden door, pressed by the crowd and the guards. The poet says even the gods have to wait.

Humour and humility. Śiva and Brahmā being tapped on the head by the doorkeepers' canes is affectionately comic: before the Lord, all are equal devotees.

The golden door. The Suprabhātam is still recited every morning before the bangāru vākili, the golden door of the sanctum.

💡 Svāmi Puṣkariṇī

The sacred tank beside the temple at Tirumala.

💡 Bangāru vākili

"Golden door": the entrance to the sanctum where the Suprabhātam is sung.

Before the Lord, no one is too great to wait in line.

ZAPPY: The gods get hit by the guards' sticks?

PROF. BOLT: In the poem, yes, like everyone in the crowd. It's a lovely joke: at His door, all are just devotees.

Even the gods queue at His golden door.
Some texts read haraviriñca for haraviriñci.

VERSE 15 · GOING DEEPER

THE NAMES OF THE SEVEN HILLS

The seven hills. Tirumala is called Saptagiri, "seven hills". Traditional lists name Śeṣādri, Nīlādri, Garuḍādri, Añjanādri, Vṛṣabhādri, Nārāyaṇādri and Veṅkaṭādri; the verse names six and adds "and the rest".

Each a story. Śeṣa: Ādiśeṣa, Viṣṇu's serpent; Garuḍa: His eagle; Añjana: where Hanumān's mother did penance; Vṛṣabha: a demon devotee; Nārāyaṇa: where a sage did penance; Veṅkaṭa: "the hill that burns away sin", one explanation of the name.

Speaking the names. Just saying the hill's names is itself praise.

💡 Saptagiri

"Seven hills": Tirumala's peaks; Śrīnivāsa is Saptagirīśa.

💡 Veṅkaṭa

Often explained as "vem" (sin) + "kaṭa" (burning): the hill that destroys sin.

Even the names of holy places carry blessing. Say them with love.

ZAPPY: Seven hills, but six names?

PROF. BOLT: The verse ends "and the rest". Most lists include Nīlādri and Añjanādri too: seven in all.

Śeṣa, Garuḍa, Veṅkaṭa…: the seven hills of the Lord.
Lists of the seven hills vary slightly between sources.
