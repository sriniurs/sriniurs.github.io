Great shrines across India

FOREWORD

Hanumān lives at the edge of every village, but some of his shrines have become great pilgrim centres: Saṅkaṭ Mocan where Tulasīdāsa saw him, Hanumāngaṛhī guarding Ayodhyā, the bearded Bālājī of Sālāsar, the giant Āñjaneya of Nāmakkal and the birthplace hill of Añjanādri across from Hampi.

This part visits the best known, north to south, with their legends and what makes each special.

AT A GLANCE

Hanumān’s great shrines
NorthSaṅkaṭ Mocan (Vārāṇasī), Hanumāngaṛhī (Ayodhyā), Lete Hanumān (Prayāgrāj), Mahāvīr Mandir (Patna), Jakhu (Shimla)
WestSālāsar and Mehandipur Bālājī (Rajasthan), Añjaneri (Nashik), Samartha Rāmadāsa’s eleven Mārutis
SouthAñjanādri and Yantroddhāraka (Hampi), Nāmakkal, Suchindram, Koṇḍagaṭṭu, Pañcamukhi
BengaluruGāli Āñjaneya and Ragigudda
EverywhereA small Hanumān at almost every village edge
This guideFacts, legends and what makes each shrine special

ZAPPY: Which is the most important?

PROF. BOLT: Each region has its own. Devotees say: the one nearest you.

Hanumān has more shrines than almost any other Hindu deity, but most are small and local: a carved slab at a village gate, a roadside niche, a shrine in a police station or a wrestling ground. The great temples here are only the best known.

Northern shrines often call him Bālājī or Mahāvīr; southern ones Āñjaneya or Māruti; the image ranges from the beard of Sālāsar to the giant standing forms of Tamil Nadu.

SAṄKAṬ MOCAN, VĀRĀṆASĪ

Where Tulasīdāsa met Hanumān
FounderBy tradition Tulasīdāsa, in the 16th century, at the spot where he had a vision of Hanumān
ImageHanumān faces Rāma, as if gazing at his Lord
LaddusBesan laddus are the famous offering
MusicThe Saṅkaṭ Mocan Saṅgīt Samāroh, a classical music festival around Hanumān Jayantī
2006The temple was struck in the Vārāṇasī bombings of March 2006; worship resumed the next day

The temple stands near the Assi ghat area, in a grove. Its main image faces Rāma, as tradition says Hanumān always faces his master. Tuesdays and Saturdays see long queues; the evening ārati is famous.

The music festival, held for several nights in April, has hosted many of India’s greatest classical musicians, who perform as an offering. Musicians of different faiths have taken part.

The 2006 blast killed worshippers at the temple and elsewhere in Vārāṇasī. The temple reopened at once, and the mahant’s call for calm was widely noted.

HANUMĀNGAṚHĪ, AYODHYĀ

The fort of Hanumān
WhereOn a hillock in the heart of Ayodhyā; about 76 steps
ImageBāla Hanumān seated on his mother Añjanā’s lap
TraditionHanumān is said to guard Ayodhyā from here; pilgrims visit him before Rāma
HistoryThe present fortress-temple is generally dated to the 18th century, on land given by a Nawab of Awadh
SaintsA seat of the Nirvāṇī Akhāṛā of Rāmānandī sādhus

ZAPPY: Visit Hanumān before Rāma?

PROF. BOLT: He is the gatekeeper. You ask his permission first.

Hanumāngaṛhī is one of Ayodhyā’s oldest and busiest shrines. Its fortress-like walls enclose a courtyard, and the main image of the boy Hanumān on Añjanā’s lap is small and heavily decorated.

The temple is run by Rāmānandī sādhus of the Nirvāṇī Akhāṛā. Since the consecration of the Rāma temple in 2024, pilgrim numbers have grown steeply.

LETE HANUMĀN, PRAYĀGRĀJ

The reclining Hanumān
WhereNear the Saṅgam, by the Allahabad Fort
ImageA large Hanumān lying on his back, below ground level
The GaṅgāIn flood years the river reaches the image; devotees say Gaṅgā comes to bathe him
LegendHe rested here, exhausted after the war, by one telling

The reclining image is unusual: most Hanumāns stand or kneel. Local tradition says that after the war, or on his way back, Hanumān lay down here exhausted, and the image marks the spot.

Pilgrims to the Kumbha and Māgha Melas at the Saṅgam usually visit Lete Hanumān as part of the round.

MAHĀVĪR MANDIR, PATNA

A modern great shrine
WhereNear Patna Junction railway station
ImagePaired images of Hanumān, said to fulfil wishes and protect
NaivedyamIts sweet, made by South Indian cooks, is famous
CharityThe temple trust runs hospitals, including a cancer hospital

Mahāvīr Mandir is among the most visited temples in Bihar. It is also known for its social work: the trust runs hospitals and charitable kitchens.

Its laddu, called naivedyam, is made by cooks from Tirupati’s tradition and sold to pilgrims.

JAKHU, SHIMLA

On the highest hill
WhereJakhu hill above Shimla, about 2,450 m
LegendHanumān rested here on his way to the herb mountain; his footprint is shown
StatueA 108-foot orange Hanumān statue, unveiled in 2010, visible across Shimla
MonkeysThe hill is famous for its bold monkeys

The climb to Jakhu from the Ridge in Shimla is steep, and many pilgrims now take a ropeway. The statue, one of the tallest Hanumān images in the world when built, dominates Shimla’s skyline.

Monkeys on Jakhu are notorious for snatching glasses and bags; visitors are told to keep things in pockets.

SĀLĀSAR BĀLĀJĪ, RAJASTHAN

Hanumān with a beard
WhereSālāsar, Churu district
OriginBy tradition a farmer at Asota ploughed up the image in 1754; it was brought here
ImageHanumān with a moustache and beard, unique among his images
FairsHuge fairs on Caitra and Āśvin full moons
CoconutsDevotees tie coconuts with red thread to a tree for wishes

The Sālāsar Bālājī image, with its round face, moustache and beard, is said to have been ploughed up in a field at Asota village in 1754; the local thakur had a dream instructing him to send it to Sālāsar. The image of Hanumān with facial hair is very rare.

Twice-yearly fairs bring hundreds of thousands of pilgrims, many walking from distant towns with flags.

MEHANDIPUR BĀLĀJĪ, RAJASTHAN

The shrine of healing rites
WhereDausa district, between two hills
DeitiesBālājī (Hanumān), Pretrāj Sarkār and Bhairav
Known forRituals for those believed to be troubled by spirits
NoteThe rites draw crowds and also criticism; we describe, not endorse

Mehandipur Bālājī is widely known in North India for rites for people believed to be afflicted by spirits or black magic. Families come for days of prayer and rituals.

Mental-health professionals and rationalist groups have criticised the treatment of distressed people there. We note this as a well-known controversy, without taking sides on belief.

AÑJANERI, NASHIK

Añjanī’s mountain
WhereA hill fort about 20 km from Nashik, near Tryambakeśvar
ClaimOne of the places held to be Hanumān’s birthplace
ShrinesAñjanī Mātā temple and a cave linked to his birth
TrekA popular monsoon trek through grassland

Añjaneri has an old Jain cave group and Hindu temple ruins at its foot, and the Añjanī Mātā temple partway up. The hill is a common half-day trek from Nashik.

Its birthplace claim rests on the name and the local temple traditions.

AÑJANĀDRI, HAMPI

The hill across the river
WhereNear Ānegundi, across the Tuṅgabhadrā from Hampi
ClaimHeld by Karnataka tradition to be his birthplace, in Kiṣkindhā itself
ClimbAbout 575 whitewashed steps to the hilltop temple
ImageHanumān carved on a rock, with a small shrine to Añjanā
ViewA famous sunset over the boulders and paddy fields

ZAPPY: 575 steps!

PROF. BOLT: And monkeys watching every one of them.

Añjanādri is a short drive from Hampi via Ānegundi. The hilltop temple is simple: a carved image of Hanumān on the rock, with Rāma and Sītā shrines beside it. Pilgrims recite the Cālīsā on the climb.

Karnataka has been developing the hill as a major pilgrimage site, and Hanumān Jayantī brings large crowds. The view over the Tuṅgabhadrā, the boulders and the paddy fields at sunset is famous.

YANTRODDHĀRAKA, HAMPI

Hanumān in a yantra
WhereOn a rock by the river near the Vijaya Viṭhṭhala temple
ImageHanumān seated in meditation inside a six-pointed yantra, ringed by monkeys
VyāsatīrthaInstalled, by tradition, by Vyāsatīrtha, rājaguru of Kṛṣṇadevarāya
StoryHanumān appeared to him only after he drew the yantra to hold him

The image is unusual: Hanumān sits cross-legged in meditation, inside a hexagram, surrounded by a ring of small monkeys holding each other’s tails. Inscriptions on the rock carry the bīja syllables of the yantra.

The story is that Vyāsatīrtha drew Hanumān’s image on a rock each day, and each day it vanished, until he bound it within the yantra.

BENGALURU

Gāli Āñjaneya and Ragigudda
Gāli ĀñjaneyaOn Mysore Road at the Vṛṣabhāvatī river; one of Vyāsatīrtha’s images; “Gāli” is said to come from the wind
RagiguddaJayanagar; a hill temple with Āñjaneya, Rāma and Śiva; built in 1969 on a granite hillock
Mahālakṣmī LayoutA large Āñjaneya on a hillock
EverywhereĀñjaneya shrines in almost every old Bengaluru neighbourhood

Gāli Āñjaneya is one of Bengaluru’s oldest and busiest Hanumān temples, on the Mysore Road at the river crossing. It is linked by tradition to Vyāsatīrtha’s 732 images.

Ragigudda (“millet hill”) temple sits on a granite outcrop in Jayanagar; its name comes from a local story about a heap of millet that turned into a rock. Its Hanumān Jayantī celebrations are among the largest in the city.

NĀMAKKAL, TAMIL NADU

The 18-foot Āñjaneya under open sky
ImageA standing Āñjaneya about 18 feet tall, carved from a single stone
No roofHe stands in the open; by tradition he keeps growing and will not be roofed
FacingHe faces Narasiṃha in the cave temple across the square, with folded hands
OfferingsVaḍa-mālā garlands and butter coating

The Nāmakkal Āñjaneya is one of the tallest single-stone Hanumān images in South India. He faces Lakṣmī Narasiṃha in the rock-cut cave temple on the opposite side, with joined hands, as if in eternal worship.

The roofless shrine has a legend: attempts to build a roof failed because Hanumān keeps growing. The image is regularly coated with butter and garlanded with thousands of vaḍas.

SUCHINDRAM, TAMIL NADU

The giant in Thāṇumālayan’s temple
WhereNear Kanyakumari
ImageA tall standing Hanumān, said to be about 18 feet, inside the Thāṇumālayan (Śiva-Viṣṇu-Brahmā) temple
ButterDevotees coat him in butter
HistoryThe image was buried for safety in the 18th century and rediscovered in the 20th, by local tradition

The Thāṇumālayan temple is dedicated to Śiva, Viṣṇu and Brahmā as one (Sthāṇu–Māl–Ayan). Its Hanumān image is one of the largest in Tamil Nadu and attracts its own crowds.

Local tradition holds the image was buried to protect it during the wars of the 18th century and found again in the 1930s.

KOṆḌAGAṬṬU, TELANGANA

The dīkṣā hill
WhereJagtial district, on a hill
ImageĀñjaneya with two faces, his own and Narasiṃha’s
DīkṣāThousands end their 41-day Hanumān vow here on Jayantī
AlsoKarmanghat in Hyderabad, another great Telugu shrine

Koṇḍagaṭṭu, “hill” in Telugu, is the main destination for Telangana’s Hanumān dīkṣā pilgrims. Its image shows Āñjaneya with a Narasiṃha face beside his own.

Karmanghat in Hyderabad has a long history; a well-known local story tells how it survived an attempt to destroy it in the 17th century.

PAÑCAMUKHI, MANTRĀLAYAM

Where a saint meditated
WhereNear Mantrālayam, Raichur district side of the Tuṅgabhadrā
SaintRāghavendra Tīrtha is said to have meditated here and seen the five-faced Hanumān
ImageA rock image of Pañcamukha Hanumān
PilgrimsUsually visited with Mantrālayam

Pañcamukhi is a short drive from Mantrālayam, where the 17th-century Mādhva saint Rāghavendra Tīrtha entered samādhi. Tradition says he meditated at this rock for years and had a vision of the five-faced Hanumān.

Pilgrims usually combine it with Mantrālayam and nearby Bichāle.

MORE SHRINES

A short list across India
DelhiThe Connaught Place Hanumān temple; the 108-foot Saṅkaṭ Mocan statue at Jhandewalan
CitrakūṭaHanumān Dhārā, where a stream falls on the image: said to cool him after burning Laṅkā
GujaratSāraṅgpur Kaṣṭabhañjan Hanumān
AndhraParitala’s 135-foot Āñjaneya statue
Sri LankaRāmboḍa Hanumān temple in the hills, run by the Chinmaya Mission

These are only some of the great shrines. Each state has its own famous Hanumān: Kaṣṭabhañjan at Sāraṅgpur in Gujarat for those in trouble; Hanumān Dhārā at Citrakūṭa; giant statues at Paritala in Andhra and many places near Bengaluru.

In Sri Lanka, sites in the central hills, like Rāmboḍa and Seetha Eliya, are linked by local tradition to Hanumān’s search for Sītā.

VISITING A HANUMĀN SHRINE

What you will see
DaysTuesday and Saturday crowds
OfferingsSindūr, oil, flowers, laddus, vaḍas, betel leaves
RecitationThe Cālīsā, often 11 or 108 times
Rāma firstIn many shrines Rāma, Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa are worshipped too
RespectMany temples ask women not to touch the image; customs vary

A typical Tuesday visit: arrive at dusk, offer sindūr or oil, light a lamp, circle the shrine, recite the Cālīsā and take prasāda. Many devotees vow to visit for 11 or 21 Tuesdays in a row.
