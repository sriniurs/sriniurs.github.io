The meeting with Rāma

FOREWORD

Two princes in bark clothes walk up to a hill by a lake. On the hill a frightened monkey prince sends his minister down to see who they are. The minister is Hanumān, and when he speaks, Rāma knows he has found a friend.

This part follows Hanumān through the fourth book of the Rāmāyaṇa: the meeting, the alliance, the death of Vālī, the long monsoon, the search, the ring, and the moment on the southern shore when an old bear reminds him who he is.

In Karnataka this book is close to home: tradition sets it among the boulders of Hampi.

Jai Hanumān,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Is Kiṣkindhā really Hampi?

PROF. BOLT: By a tradition centuries old, yes. The places have names from the story.

AT A GLANCE

BookKiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa, the fourth book of Vālmīki
PlaceThe vānara kingdom on the Tuṅgabhadrā, placed by tradition at Hampi
MeetingHanumān, in disguise, meets Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa near Paṃpā lake
AllianceRāma and Sugrīva swear friendship before fire
VālīRāma kills Vālī; Sugrīva becomes king
SearchVānara armies go out in four directions
The ringRāma gives Hanumān his signet ring for Sītā
AwakeningJāmbavān reminds Hanumān of his power on the southern shore

ZAPPY: Why is this book important for Hanumān?

PROF. BOLT: It's where he meets Rāma. Everything after that is service.

The Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa is the hinge of the Rāmāyaṇa. Before it Rāma is alone, grieving and lost; after it he has an army, an ally, news of Sītā and, above all, Hanumān. Vālmīki makes Hanumān the first character Rāma meets in this book and the last one standing on its final page.

Hampi preserves this book on its landscape. Pilgrims are shown Ṛṣyamūka on the Tuṅgabhadrā, Sugrīva’s cave with streaks said to be from Sītā’s jewels, Mālyavanta where Rāma waited out the rains, Paṃpā Sarovar where he met Śabarī, and the hill of Añjanādri across the river.

TWO STRANGERS

Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa reach Paṃpā
After ŚabarīThe tribal devotee Śabarī directed the brothers to Paṃpā lake and Sugrīva
GriefRāma, mourning Sītā, wandered by the lake in spring
Sugrīva’s fearFrom Ṛṣyamūka, Sugrīva saw two armed warriors and feared Vālī had sent them
The planHe sent Hanumān to find out who they were

Śabarī’s welcome is one of the tenderest moments before this book: an old woman of a forest tribe who had waited years for Rāma, fed him and told him where to go. Rāma takes her advice and walks to Paṃpā.

Vālmīki’s spring at Paṃpā is the mirror of Rāma’s grief: everything blossoms, birds call in pairs, and every beautiful thing reminds him of Sītā. Lakṣmaṇa has to rouse him.

From his hilltop Sugrīva sees two tall men in bark garments carrying great bows, and assumes the worst. His panic is why Hanumān goes alone, in disguise.

THE DISGUISED MONK

Hanumān goes to look
DisguiseHanumān took the form of a wandering mendicant (bhikṣu)
ApproachHe praised the brothers’ noble looks and asked who they were and why they had come
OpennessThen he told them plainly who he was and why Sugrīva had sent him
OfferSugrīva sought their friendship

ZAPPY: Why the disguise?

PROF. BOLT: Caution. He had to find out if they were friends before he showed who he was.

The bhikṣu disguise was a natural choice: wandering mendicants could approach anyone without suspicion. Hanumān begins with courtesy, praising the strangers as if they were kings or gods in the forest, and only then asks who they are.

When they do not answer at once, he gives up the disguise in words before he does in form: he tells them who he is, who sent him and why. Vālmīki presents this as wise: trust offered first invites trust in return.

Lakṣmaṇa answers for Rāma, telling the whole story from the exile to Sītā’s loss, and asking for Sugrīva’s help.

“NOT ONE WRONG WORD”

Rāma’s praise of Hanumān
To LakṣmaṇaRāma said: he could not speak like this without knowing the Ṛg, Yajur and Sāma Vedas
GrammarHe has surely studied grammar well, for he spoke at length without a single error
DeliveryNo fault in his face, eyes, brow or limbs; his words were neither too long nor too short, clear and pleasing
A king’s needA king with a messenger like this will succeed in all he undertakes

ZAPPY: Rāma noticed his grammar first?

PROF. BOLT: Before his strength. Good speech showed a trained, steady mind.

This passage is Hanumān’s introduction to the world, and Vālmīki gives it to Rāma himself. It is still quoted as a model of good speech in Indian rhetoric: knowledge behind the words, correct grammar, a clear and steady face, words neither rushed nor slow, and meaning that goes straight to the heart.

The verses list specific faults he did not show: no excess, no ambiguity, no hesitation, no unnecessary length. Rāma concludes that such a messenger is a treasure to any king.

For devotees this is the first darśan: Rāma looks at Hanumān and recognises him. Many say the relationship of master and servant began right here.

ON HIS SHOULDERS

To Ṛṣyamūka
True formHanumān dropped the disguise and returned to his vānara form
The carryHe lifted both princes on his shoulders and leapt up to Ṛṣyamūka
ImageThis is the scene of many paintings and temple panels: Hanumān with Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa on his shoulders
A first serviceThe first thing he did for Rāma was carry him

The carry is short in Vālmīki, a single verse, but it caught the imagination of artists. Temple pillars at Hampi and paintings across India show Hanumān striding with a prince on each shoulder.

It is also the first instance of Hanumān’s size-changing in service: he makes himself large enough to carry two grown warriors uphill.

Devotees read it as Hanumān carrying the Lord himself, an image of bhakti in which the servant is honoured to bear the master’s weight.

FRIENDS BEFORE FIRE

Rāma and Sugrīva
The vowHanumān lit a fire and Rāma and Sugrīva walked around it, swearing friendship
The dealRāma would kill Vālī and restore Sugrīva; Sugrīva would find Sītā
Two exilesBoth had lost a wife and a kingdom; each saw himself in the other
WitnessAgni, the fire, as witness: the same as a wedding vow

The fire-vow (agni-sākṣī maitrī) made the alliance sacred. Hanumān, as minister, arranged the rite, rubbing sticks to make the fire and placing it between the two.

The parallel between Rāma and Sugrīva is deliberate: both are exiled, both have lost their wives, both depend on loyal brothers or ministers. The friendship is mutual need turned into a vow.

Sugrīva then tells his story, and Rāma promises to kill Vālī. The rest of the book follows from this promise.

SĪTĀ’S JEWELS

The bundle in a cloth
DroppedAs Rāvaṇa flew over Ṛṣyamūka, Sītā had dropped her upper cloth and ornaments to the vānaras below
ShownSugrīva brought them out; Rāma wept
Lakṣmaṇa“I do not know her armlets or earrings. I know only her anklets, because I bowed at her feet every day”
MeaningLakṣmaṇa’s reverence for his elder brother’s wife

ZAPPY: He only knew her anklets?

PROF. BOLT: He'd never looked higher than her feet. That's how much he respected her.

When Rāvaṇa carried Sītā through the sky, she saw five vānaras on a hilltop and threw down her ornaments wrapped in her upper cloth, hoping they would reach Rāma. They were the very proof she was alive and where she had gone.

Lakṣmaṇa’s answer about the anklets is one of the most quoted lines in the epic. It became a byword for respect within the family: a younger brother who never raised his eyes to his elder brother’s wife.

Some temples at Hampi show a rock with marks said to be from these jewels, near Sugrīva’s cave.

THE TEST OF STRENGTH

Seven trees and a skeleton
DoubtSugrīva feared no one could beat Vālī
DundubhiRāma kicked the huge skeleton of the buffalo-demon Dundubhi, killed by Vālī, far away with his toe
Seven sālasThen with one arrow he pierced seven great sāla trees in a row; the arrow passed into the earth and returned
ConvincedSugrīva fell at his feet

Dundubhi was a buffalo-shaped demon who challenged Vālī and was killed and flung far away; his blood fell on the sage Mataṅga’s hermitage, and Mataṅga cursed Vālī never to set foot on Ṛṣyamūka. That curse is why Sugrīva was safe there.

Sugrīva points out that Vālī could pierce one sāla tree; Rāma pierces seven with one arrow. The arrow passes through a hill as well and returns to his quiver, a sign of divine weaponry.

Only then does Sugrīva believe he can win, and agree to challenge his brother.

THE DEATH OF VĀLĪ

The most argued scene of the epic
First fightRāma could not tell the brothers apart and held back; Sugrīva was beaten
The garlandThe second time Sugrīva wore a flowering creeper so Rāma could know him
The arrowRāma shot Vālī from behind a tree
Vālī’s rebukeDying, Vālī asked what dharma allowed shooting a man fighting someone else
Rāma’s answerHe said Vālī had taken his brother’s wife, a crime a king must punish; vānaras could be hunted; and the land was Bharata’s to rule

ZAPPY: Wasn't that unfair?

PROF. BOLT: Readers have argued about it for two thousand years. Vālmīki lets Vālī speak his case in full.

The death of Vālī is the most debated scene in the Rāmāyaṇa. Vālī’s accusation is powerful: he was fighting someone else, he had done Rāma no harm, and Rāma shot him unseen. Vālmīki gives the speech in full.

Rāma’s reply rests on three grounds: Vālī had taken his brother’s wife while his brother lived, a grave sin; as a prince of the ruling line Rāma had the duty to punish it; and a hunter may strike from cover. Vālī, the text says, accepts the answer and dies at peace, commending Aṅgada to Rāma.

Later poets reopened the question. Kamban adds moving dialogue; some commentators defend Rāma, others admit unease. The scene remains one of Indian ethics’ great case studies.

TĀRĀ AND AṄGADA

After Vālī
TārāVālī’s wise queen grieved over him; Vālmīki gives her one of the great laments of the epic
AṅgadaVālī’s son, made crown prince under Sugrīva
Sugrīva crownedHe became king of Kiṣkindhā
Hanumān’s roleHe arranged the coronation and the rites

Tārā is one of the most intelligent figures in the epic. Before the fatal fight she warned Vālī that Sugrīva would not challenge him again without powerful help; he ignored her.

Her lament over Vālī’s body moves Rāma and Sugrīva both, and she later acts as a calm diplomat between the vānaras and the angry Lakṣmaṇa. Tradition counts her among the pañca-kanyā, five women whose names are remembered at dawn.

Aṅgada, young and impetuous, becomes leader of the southern search party, with Hanumān as his steadying adviser.

THE RAINS

Waiting on Prasravaṇa
The monsoonNo army could march in the rains; Rāma waited four months on Prasravaṇa hill (Mālyavanta at Hampi by tradition)
Rāma’s griefVālmīki’s descriptions of the rains are among the finest nature poetry in Sanskrit
Sugrīva forgetsRestored to his palace, Sugrīva lost himself in pleasure
Hanumān remindsIt was Hanumān who first reminded Sugrīva of his promise

ZAPPY: The minister reminded the king?

PROF. BOLT: That's what a good minister does. Even when the king doesn't want to hear it.

The rainy-season cantos are among the most admired passages in Sanskrit poetry: the clouds, the frogs’ chorus, swollen rivers, peacocks dancing, lovers apart. Rāma sees Sītā’s absence in everything.

Meanwhile Sugrīva, after years of hardship, gives himself to drink and pleasure. Hanumān, knowing a broken promise to Rāma would be disastrous, goes to the king and advises him plainly to begin the search.

This is Hanumān as a statesman: loyal, but willing to tell his own king an unwelcome truth.

LAKṢMAṆA’S ANGER

The promise kept
AngerWhen autumn came and no army marched, Lakṣmaṇa stormed into Kiṣkindhā twanging his bow
TārāTārā went out to calm him first
SugrīvaSugrīva apologised and called up the vānara hosts from every forest and mountain
ArmiesVast numbers of vānaras and bears gathered under their chiefs

Lakṣmaṇa’s fury when he arrives in Kiṣkindhā is a famous scene: the vānaras scatter, and Sugrīva hides. Tārā, sent out first, speaks to him so gently that his anger cools.

Sugrīva then explains that his messengers have already gone out to summon the armies. Vālmīki describes troops arriving from every mountain range in India, millions upon millions, in a long, rolling catalogue.

The army that will build the bridge and fight at Laṅkā is born here.

THE RING

Rāma’s trust
Four directionsSugrīva sent armies east, west and north, and the best of them south under Aṅgada
The southern partyAṅgada, Hanumān, Jāmbavān, Nala, Nīla and others
The ringRāma gave his signet ring, engraved with his name, to Hanumān alone, to show Sītā
Why himRāma said he was sure that Hanumān would succeed

ZAPPY: Why only Hanumān got the ring?

PROF. BOLT: Rāma had watched him. He knew who would find her.

Sugrīva gives each army precise instructions and a geography of the known world, a remarkable passage that maps rivers, mountains and peoples in all directions. The south is given the strongest party because Sampāti’s brother Jaṭāyu died fighting there.

Rāma’s gift of the ring is a quiet but crucial moment. It carries his name, and it will be the token by which Sītā recognises a true messenger. The Cālīsā remembers it: “prabhu mudrikā meli mukha māhīṃ”, he carried the Lord’s ring in his mouth over the sea.

The other armies return empty-handed after a month. Only the southern party is left.

THE CAVE AND THE DEADLINE

Lost in the south
ṚkṣabilaHungry and thirsty, the party entered a magic cave of golden trees and lakes
SvayamprabhāAn ascetic woman there fed them and, by her power, set them outside, on the shore of the southern sea
A month goneTheir deadline had passed
DespairAṅgada, fearing Sugrīva’s punishment, resolved to fast to death; the others sat with him

The cave episode is a strange interlude: a sealed underground world built by the architect Maya, guarded by the ascetic Svayamprabhā. Its beauty and abundance tempt the exhausted vānaras to stay.

Aṅgada’s despair has a political edge: Sugrīva had killed his father, and he fears failure will be punished with death. Some vānaras suggest rebellion; Hanumān speaks against it and keeps the party loyal.

The resolve to fast to death (prāyopaveśa) sets up the arrival of Sampāti.

SAMPĀTI

The old vulture’s news
The birdA huge wingless vulture, Sampāti, lived on the mountain; he heard them speak of Jaṭāyu’s death
BrothersJaṭāyu was his younger brother; they had flown at the sun, and Sampāti had shielded him and lost his wings
NewsWith his far sight he saw Sītā in Laṅkā, a hundred yojanas across the sea
WingsAs he helped Rāma’s cause, his wings grew back

ZAPPY: Another sun-leap story!

PROF. BOLT: Yes, Sampāti's ended badly. Hanumān's ended with boons.

Sampāti and Jaṭāyu were sons of Aruṇa, the Sun’s charioteer. In their youth they raced to the sun; Jaṭāyu flew too close, and Sampāti spread his wings over him and had them burned off. He fell on this southern mountain and lived there, wingless, for ages.

A sage had told him that his wings would return when he helped Rāma’s search. When he gives the vānaras the news of Sītā, his wings grow back and he flies away.

His far-seeing vision of Laṅkā, a hundred yojanas off, is the first sure news that Sītā is alive.

WHO CAN LEAP?

A hundred yojanas
The questionAṅgada asked each leader how far he could jump
AnswersEach named a shorter distance: ten, twenty, up to ninety yojanas
AṅgadaHe could reach Laṅkā, but was unsure he could return
JāmbavānToo old now, he said, but turned to the one who sat silent: Hanumān

The leap is a hundred yojanas, the usual measure given for the distance to Laṅkā. The yojana is an old unit of uncertain length; the point in the epic is that it is impossibly far.

Vālmīki lists the leaders and their claims in turn, building suspense. Each brave vānara falls short; Aṅgada could go but doubts his return; Jāmbavān, who once circled Vāmana as he spanned the worlds, says he is too old.

Hanumān sits apart, silent. He does not know what he can do.

JĀMBAVĀN REMINDS HIM

“Why are you silent?”
The speechJāmbavān told Hanumān of his birth, the sun leap, the boons and his power
The curse liftsReminded, Hanumān remembered his strength
He growsHe swelled to an enormous size, roaring
MahendraHe climbed Mount Mahendra on the shore to make the leap

ZAPPY: That's when the curse ended?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. A friend reminded him who he was.

Jāmbavān’s speech is Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa 66, the chapter recited to give courage. He recounts the boyhood and asks: why are you sitting silent, you who are equal to the Wind and Garuḍa?

Hanumān’s response is dramatic: he grows, roars, praises his father the Wind and declares that he will leap the ocean, find Sītā and even uproot Laṅkā and bring it back if needed.

He climbs Mount Mahendra, which shakes under his weight. The next book, the Sundara Kāṇḍa, begins with the leap.
