HANUMĀN · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The child who leapt at the sun

FOREWORD

Before he was Rāma’s servant he was a hungry baby in a mountain cave, who looked out at the dawn and saw a ripe red fruit. The fruit was the Sun.

This part tells Hanumān’s boyhood as Vālmīki gives it: his mother Añjanā, the Wind’s blessing, the leap, Indra’s thunderbolt and the broken jaw that gave him his name, the gods’ gifts and the sages’ curse that made him forget his own strength.

Every later deed of Hanumān goes back to these pages.

AT A GLANCE

The boyhood on one page
MotherAñjanā, a celestial apsaras born as a vānara under a curse
FathersKesarī, the vānara chief, and Vāyu, the Wind
BirthIn a mountain cave; the place is claimed by several regions
The leapAs a baby he took the rising sun for a ripe fruit and leapt for it
The woundIndra’s thunderbolt struck his jaw (hanu): hence “Hanumān”
The boonsThe gods, to appease the angry Wind, gave him protection and powers
The curseSages he teased made him forget his powers until someone reminded him
TeacherSūrya, the Sun

ZAPPY: A baby tried to eat the Sun?

PROF. BOLT: He thought it was a mango. Babies put everything in their mouth.

This part follows Vālmīki, who tells Hanumān’s boyhood twice. On the shore before the leap to Laṅkā, the old bear-king Jāmbavān recounts it to wake Hanumān’s memory. After the war, the sage Agastya tells it again to Rāma in Ayodhyā, with more detail about the boons and the curse.

The two tellings agree on the core: an apsaras mother, the Wind as father, the sun leap, Indra’s blow, the gods’ gifts. Everything else, from the birthplace to the reason for the curse, grows richer in later Purāṇas and folk Rāmāyaṇas.

The boyhood explains the adult Hanumān. His speed comes from the Wind, his learning from the Sun, his near-invincibility from the gods, and his famous modesty partly from a curse.

AÑJANĀ

The mother under a curse
WhoAn apsaras, often named Puñjikasthalā, cursed to be born on earth as a vānarī
ReleaseThe curse would end when she bore a son of great power
HusbandThe vānara chief Kesarī
Her tapasLater tellings say she prayed to Śiva for twelve years for a son
Names for her sonĀñjaneya, “Añjanā’s son”, is the name the South uses most

ZAPPY: Why was she cursed?

PROF. BOLT: Tellings differ. Most say she mocked a sage. The curse brought the world Hanumān.

In many tellings Puñjikasthalā was an apsaras in Indra’s heaven whose beauty made her careless; she mocked or disturbed a sage at his meditation, and was cursed to take the form of a she-monkey. The release clause, a son of great power, is typical of Indian curses: the punishment carries its own way out.

Añjanā is worshipped in her own right at Añjaneri near Nashik, at Gumla and at Añjanādri near Hampi, where the hilltop shrine has a small image of her beside her son. In folk songs she is a fierce, devoted mother who tells her son to serve Rāma faithfully.

Kesarī, her husband, is a brave vānara chief in his own right. Some tellings make him the slayer of a demon elephant called Śambasādana, and he fights in Rāma’s army in the war.

THE WIND’S BLESSING

How Vāyu became his father
VālmīkiAñjanā stood on a mountain in beautiful clothes; the Wind, enchanted, embraced her unseen
Her alarmShe cried out at being touched by no one
Vāyu’s answer“I have not harmed your vow. By my embrace of the mind you will have a son, brave, wise, and my equal in speed and leaping”
MeaningSo Hanumān is son of Kesarī by family and of the Wind by power

Vāyu, the Wind, is one of the oldest Vedic gods, and also prāṇa, the life-breath in every creature. To be his son is to share in the force that keeps all beings alive; that is why Mādhvas call Hanumān Mukhyaprāṇa, “the chief breath”.

Vālmīki is careful to keep Añjanā’s honour intact: the Wind’s touch is a blessing of the mind, not a seduction, and Añjanā’s vow to her husband is unbroken. Later commentators stress the same point.

The Wind’s two sons, Hanumān in the Rāmāyaṇa and Bhīma in the Mahābhārata, share his gifts: speed, enormous strength and the mace. Tradition pairs them as brothers across the ages.

WHERE WAS HE BORN?

Four hills, one baby
Añjanādri, HampiKarnataka: across the Tuṅgabhadrā from Hampi, in Kiṣkindhā itself
Añjaneri, NashikMaharashtra: a hill fort near Tryambakeśvar with an Añjanī temple
Añjanādri, TirumalaAndhra: one of Tirumala’s seven hills; Tirumala Tirupati Devasthanams declared it the birthplace in 2021
Gumla, JharkhandĀñjan village, with an Añjanī cave temple
GokarṇaSome Kannada tellings place Kesarī and Añjanā at Gokarṇa

ZAPPY: So which hill is right?

PROF. BOLT: Each has a tradition and a temple. None can be proved. Visit all four if you like.

Birthplace claims are a living issue. In 2021 Tirumala Tirupati Devasthanams set up a committee of scholars that declared Tirumala’s Añjanādri the birthplace; Karnataka’s Hampi tradition and a Hanumān trust at Kiṣkindhā disputed it, and the debate has not been settled.

Hampi’s claim rests on geography: Kiṣkindhā, where Sugrīva lived and Hanumān served, is placed on the Tuṅgabhadrā around Hampi, and the hill of Añjanādri rises directly opposite. Añjaneri’s claim rests on its name and an old Añjanī temple; Gumla’s on a cave shrine and local Adivasi traditions.

For readers the point is simple: every region wants Hanumān as its own child. That says more about his love in India than any proof could.

THE LEAP AT THE SUN

The first great leap
HungerLeft alone in the cave, the baby woke hungry
The fruitHe saw the rising sun, red as a ripe fruit, and leapt for it
SpeedHe flew as fast as his father the Wind; the Sun, knowing whose son he was, did not burn him
TulasīdāsaThe Cālīsā: “He swallowed the sun, thousands of leagues away, thinking it a sweet fruit”

ZAPPY: Didn't he get burned?

PROF. BOLT: The Sun knew he was a baby. Vālmīki says the Sun spared him.

In Vālmīki’s telling the leap is so fast and high that gods, dānavas and yakṣas look up in wonder: a baby flying like an arrow towards the morning sun. Jāmbavān uses this memory to remind the grown Hanumān that leaping the sea is nothing for one who leapt at the sun.

The Cālīsā’s line about the sun sits in the middle of a list of Hanumān’s deeds. Some modern writers point out that its “yug sahastra jojan” is a playful number; others try to read it as an estimate of the distance to the sun. Tulasīdāsa was writing devotion, not astronomy.

The Saṅkaṭmocan Aṣṭak opens with the same scene: the child swallows the sun, darkness falls on the three worlds, and the gods beg him to release it.

RĀHU COMPLAINS

An eclipse interrupted
That dayRāhu, the shadow planet who swallows the sun at eclipses, was coming to seize it
The rivalHe found a baby monkey already there
To IndraRāhu complained: “You gave me the Sun and Moon to eat; who is this other Rāhu?”
Indra comesIndra rode out on his white elephant Airāvata
Another fruitThe baby saw Airāvata and thought it a bigger fruit

Rāhu is the severed head of the demon who drank the nectar of immortality by stealth at the churning of the ocean. Viṣṇu cut him in two, but the head lived on, and in revenge it swallows the sun and moon at eclipses.

The meeting of Rāhu and Hanumān has a comic flavour in Vālmīki. Rāhu, the terror of the sky, flees to Indra complaining that someone has taken his food. When the baby turns towards Rāhu himself, the shadow planet runs again, crying for help.

Later tellings make this a parvan, a sacred eclipse day, and some say Hanumān actually grabbed Rāhu, which is why Hanumān worship is advised against the planets’ ill effects.

THE THUNDERBOLT

How Hanumān got his name
The blowIndra hurled his vajra; it struck the child on the jaw
The fallHe fell on a mountain, his left jaw broken
The name“Hanumān”: the one whose jaw was struck. Vālmīki makes Indra himself give the name
UnharmedThe vajra could not kill him; it left only the mark

ZAPPY: Indra hit a baby?

PROF. BOLT: Gods behave badly in old stories. And he paid for it, as you'll see.

The vajra is the most powerful weapon of the Vedic world, made, in one story, from the bones of the sage Dadhīci. That it could only mark a baby’s jaw shows how strong Hanumān already was.

Vālmīki makes the name a gift: Indra, ashamed, declares that since the child’s jaw was broken by the vajra he will be called Hanumān. The jaw becomes his identity, as the blue throat does for Śiva.

Many images show a slightly swollen left cheek or jaw, a quiet reminder of the story.

THE WIND STOPS

When Vāyu withdrew
GriefVāyu picked up his son and went into a cave
The world chokesThe Wind stopped moving: no breath in any creature, no digestion, no movement
Gods to BrahmāThe gods and sages ran to Brahmā, choking
BrahmāHe went to the cave and touched the child; Hanumān revived, and the Wind blew again

ZAPPY: Why did everything stop?

PROF. BOLT: Because Vāyu is prāṇa, the breath. Hurt his child and the whole world stops breathing.

This is one of the most striking scenes in the epic. Vālmīki describes the world without wind: creatures swell up, unable to breathe out; joints lock; the sacred fires die; the gods themselves start to suffocate.

The episode teaches something central about Hanumān’s father: Vāyu is not a passing breeze but the breath of life in every body, including the gods’. Hurt the Wind and the whole universe feels it.

Brahmā’s healing touch and the boons that follow are, in effect, the gods’ apology. From then on the child is protected by nearly every power in heaven.

THE GODS’ GIFTS

Boons to the child, part 1
BrahmāNo weapon of Brahmā could harm him; long life; the power to take any form
IndraHis vajra would never hurt him again; and the name Hanumān
SūryaA hundredth part of his own brilliance, and to teach him the śāstras himself
VaruṇaSafety from water and from Varuṇa’s noose
YamaFreedom from Yama’s rod and from disease

The boons are listed in the Uttara Kāṇḍa. Manuscripts differ in detail, but the pattern is clear: each god promises that his own weapon or power will never harm the child, and some add a gift.

Sūrya’s gift is the most important for the story: a share of his brilliance and the promise to teach him. Hanumān’s learning, prized by Rāma, comes from this.

Brahmā’s gift of taking any form explains Hanumān’s size changes in the Sundara Kāṇḍa, and his gift of protection from Brahmā’s weapon explains why Indrajit’s brahmāstra binds him only briefly.

THE GODS’ GIFTS

Boons to the child, part 2
KuberaHis mace would not harm him; he would never tire in battle
ŚivaSafety from Śiva’s own weapons
ViśvakarmāNo weapon he had made could hurt him
Brahmā againHe would strike fear into enemies and safety into friends
The upshotThe gods made Hanumān nearly unbeatable: the strongest of the vānaras

ZAPPY: Why give him so much?

PROF. BOLT: They were scared. And they knew he was meant for Rāma.

Śiva’s boon reflects the Śaiva view of Hanumān as his own portion. Kubera, the god of wealth, adds tirelessness in battle; Viśvakarmā, the gods’ craftsman, protection from every weapon he has forged.

Some later lists add icchā-mṛtyu, death only at his own wish, and freedom from old age. These ideas feed into his status as a cirañjīvī who chooses to stay on earth.

The boons come to a head in Laṅkā: Hanumān lets Indrajit’s weapon bind him on purpose, so that he can meet Rāvaṇa and see the city’s defences.

THE FORGETTING

The sages’ curse
The pranksterFull of power, the boy ran wild in the hermitages: tearing up sacrificial grass, breaking pots, pulling at the sages’ clothes
PatienceKnowing the gods’ boons, the sages bore it for a time
The curseFinally sages of the Bhṛgu and Aṅgiras lines cursed him to forget his own powers
The way outHe would remember them only when someone reminded him of his glory
Why it mattersSo in the epic he stands quiet until Jāmbavān reminds him on the shore

ZAPPY: He needed a reminder?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. It's why the leap to Laṅkā starts with a speech.

The curse is one of the cleverest devices in the epic. It explains why the strongest vānara stood by while Vālī exiled Sugrīva, and why Hanumān has to be told, on the shore, that he can leap the ocean.

The sages do not hate the child; they temper him. A being with every boon and no restraint would be dangerous. Forgetting his power until it is needed makes him gentle in ordinary life and terrible only for Rāma’s work.

Devotees read it as a lesson for everyone: we too have strength we have forgotten, and we need a good friend, a teacher or a crisis to remind us.

THE SUN AS TEACHER

Learning on the move
The promiseSūrya had promised to teach him
HowHanumān faced the Sun and moved backwards across the sky with him, from east to west, learning as he went
GrammarTradition says he mastered the nine grammars (nava-vyākaraṇa)
FeeA later story: the teacher’s fee was to serve Sugrīva, Sūrya’s son
ResultRāma’s praise of his perfect speech, years later

The picture of a student walking backwards across the sky, facing his teacher, is often used to explain the guru–śiṣya bond: the student keeps his eyes on the teacher at every step, whatever it costs.

Grammar was the most prestigious science in classical India, and “nava-vyākaraṇa-paṇḍita”, master of nine grammars, became one of Hanumān’s titles. The tradition of nine schools of grammar is later; the point is that he knew all there was to know.

The fee story ties the boyhood to the epic: because Sūrya asked him to look after his son Sugrīva, Hanumān was on Ṛṣyamūka when Rāma arrived.

SUGRĪVA’S MINISTER

Before Rāma came
KiṣkindhāThe vānara kingdom, ruled by Vālī, with his younger brother Sugrīva
The quarrelVālī drove Sugrīva out and took his wife
LoyaltyHanumān went with Sugrīva into exile on Ṛṣyamūka hill, where Vālī could not follow because of a sage’s curse
RoleSugrīva’s chief minister and adviser

ZAPPY: Why didn't Hanumān just beat Vālī?

PROF. BOLT: He'd forgotten his strength. And he was a minister, not a king.

Vālī and Sugrīva were sons of Indra and Sūrya respectively, born in the vānara line. Vālī had a boon that half his opponent’s strength would pass to him in a fight, which made him nearly unbeatable.

The quarrel began with a misunderstanding: Sugrīva sealed a cave believing Vālī dead inside it. Vālī came out, called him a traitor and drove him away. Sugrīva took refuge on Ṛṣyamūka, which Vālī could not climb because of the sage Mataṅga’s curse.

Hanumān stayed with the exiled prince. His loyalty to a friend in trouble, when his own strength was forgotten and the future dark, is the first sign of the servant he would become.

BĀLA HANUMĀN

The child in worship
ImagesBāla Hanumān shown crawling or leaping, often with the sun in his hand
For childrenParents pray to him for strong, fearless children
HymnsThe Cālīsā, Saṅkaṭmocan Aṣṭak and Bajrang Bāṇ all retell the sun leap
Saṅkaṭmocan AṣṭakIts first verse: as a child you swallowed the sun and the three worlds went dark

Bāla Hanumān images are popular in homes, and many children wear a small Hanumān pendant for protection. Hanumān is the patron of courage for children afraid of the dark.

Several temples keep Hanumān in a boyish form, and many depictions of the sun leap show a chubby orange baby reaching for a red sun. Comics and animated films have made this the best-known part of his story for children.

The three hymns that retell the leap are all in Avadhī or Braj; each will get its own verse-by-verse guide in this series.

WHAT THE BOYHOOD TEACHES

Power, pride and patience
InnocenceHis first great feat was a mistake made out of hunger
Every gift has a costHuge boons came with a curse that kept him humble
LearningHe studied with the Sun, at the Sun’s pace
WaitingHe waited years in exile with Sugrīva, not knowing what he could do

The boyhood has the shape of a fairy tale and the weight of a teaching. The child has no idea of his power, uses it by accident, is struck down, is raised up, receives everything, and then loses it until the right moment.

Indian tradition sees in it a model of spiritual life: innate power, a fall, divine grace, and the long wait until one’s purpose appears. For Hanumān that purpose arrives with two princes walking up to Ṛṣyamūka.
