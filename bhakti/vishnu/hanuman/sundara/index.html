The Sundara Kāṇḍa

FOREWORD

There is one book of the Rāmāyaṇa that belongs to Hanumān. It begins with him standing on a mountain by the sea and ends with him in Rāma’s arms. In between he crosses an ocean, beats a city’s guardian, searches a sleeping palace, despairs, finds Sītā, burns Laṅkā and comes home.

It is called the Sundara Kāṇḍa, the beautiful book, and many families read it on its own when they need hope.

This part follows it scene by scene.

Jai Hanumān,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why read it on its own?

PROF. BOLT: Because it is a whole story: from despair to hope in 68 chapters.

AT A GLANCE

The Sundara Kāṇḍa on one page
BookSundara Kāṇḍa, fifth book of Vālmīki, 68 sargas
HeroHanumān: this is his book
The leapFrom Mount Mahendra across the sea to Laṅkā
TestsMaināka, Surasā, Siṃhikā, Laṅkinī
FoundSītā in the Aśoka grove, under a śiṃśapā tree
TokensRāma’s ring to Sītā; her crest-jewel (cūḍāmaṇi) to Rāma
Laṅkā burnsHis tail set alight, he sets the city ablaze
Return“Seen Sītā!”: the news that saves Rāma

ZAPPY: Why is it called Sundara, beautiful?

PROF. BOLT: Many reasons are given. The simplest: it's the book where hope comes back.

The Sundara Kāṇḍa is the only book of the Rāmāyaṇa in which Rāma does not appear in person until its last pages. Its hero is Hanumān from the first verse to the last, and many readers treat it as a complete work on its own.

Commentators give many reasons for the name. Some say Hanumān was called Sundara by his mother; some that Sītā, Rāma and Laṅkā are all beautiful here; most simply that the turn from despair to hope is the most beautiful thing in the epic.

The book has a clear shape: a journey out with trials, a search in darkness, a meeting, a test of the enemy, a fire and a joyful return. It is one of the earliest great adventure stories in world literature.

THE LEAP

From Mahendra into the sky
MahendraHe pressed the mountain so hard that springs gushed and creatures fled
Like an arrowHe flew “like an arrow shot by Rāma”, the Wind cooling him
SizeHe grew vast; his shadow lay on the sea like a ship
The ringThe Cālīsā: he carried the Lord’s ring in his mouth

Vālmīki spends dozens of verses on the take-off alone: the mountain cracking, snakes spitting fire, sages and vidyādharas rising into the sky to watch, trees uprooted and flying after Hanumān for a distance before falling into the sea.

The simile of Rāma’s arrow is pointed: Hanumān goes as Rāma’s own weapon, sure to hit its mark. He calls on his father the Wind before he leaps.

The Cālīsā line about the ring in his mouth is not in Vālmīki, where he simply carries it, but it became the popular picture.

MAINĀKA

The mountain that offered rest
RisingThe golden mountain Maināka rose from the sea, sent by the Ocean
GratitudeThe Ocean owed the Ikṣvāku kings, Rāma’s ancestors; and Maināka owed the Wind, who had hidden him from Indra
The offer“Rest on me”
HanumānHe touched it with his hand in thanks and flew on: “I have promised not to stop”

ZAPPY: Why didn't he rest?

PROF. BOLT: He had work to do. He thanked the mountain and kept going.

Maināka’s story: once mountains had wings and flew about, crushing cities, until Indra cut off their wings with his vajra. The Wind carried Maināka away and hid him in the sea, saving his wings. So Maināka owes Hanumān’s father a debt.

The Ocean owes Rāma’s line too: the sea was dug by the sons of King Sagara, Rāma’s ancestor, which is why it is called sāgara. By hosting Hanumān it would repay both.

Hanumān’s courteous refusal sets the pattern for the journey: no rest until the work is done.

SURASĀ

The mother of serpents
The testThe gods sent Surasā, mother of nāgas, to test him
“Enter my mouth”She said no one could pass without entering her mouth
GrowingHe grew; she widened her mouth; they grew against each other
The trickSuddenly he shrank to the size of a thumb, darted in and out, and kept the rule
BlessingSurasā, pleased, blessed his mission

ZAPPY: He didn't fight her?

PROF. BOLT: He used his head. Kept her rule and passed.

Surasā is not an enemy but an examiner sent by the gods and sages, who want to see if Hanumān has wisdom as well as strength. Her rule is absolute; he cannot simply refuse it.

The duel of growing sizes is comic and tense at once: Hanumān grows ten yojanas, she opens her mouth twenty; he grows thirty, she forty; and so on. Then the sudden shrink.

Devotees read the scene as the right use of power: neither fighting nor fleeing, but finding the way that keeps the rule and still gets through.

SIṂHIKĀ

The shadow-catcher
The demonessSiṃhikā lived in the sea and caught creatures by seizing their shadows
CaughtShe grabbed Hanumān’s shadow and pulled him down
InsideHe shrank, entered her mouth and tore her vitals, then burst out
DifferenceSurasā was a test to be passed; Siṃhikā an enemy to be killed

Siṃhikā is the mother of Rāhu in some genealogies, and her method is shadow-seizing: she grips the reflection or shadow on the water and drags the creature down. Hanumān feels his speed suddenly checked and understands.

Unlike Surasā she is not a test but a killer, and Hanumān treats her so. Sugrīva had warned the search party about her.

The three trials are often read in sequence: Maināka tests resolve against comfort, Surasā tests wisdom, Siṃhikā tests courage.

LAṄKINĪ

The city’s guardian
LandingHe landed on Trikūṭa mountain, saw golden Laṅkā, and waited for dark
SmallHe made himself the size of a cat to slip in
The guardLaṅkā’s guardian spirit, Laṅkinī, stopped him
One blowHe struck her with his left fist and she fell
ProphecyShe said Brahmā had told her: when a monkey defeats you, Laṅkā’s end is near

ZAPPY: A cat-sized monkey beat the city?

PROF. BOLT: With his left fist. And held back.

Laṅkā’s guardian is the city itself personified, a fierce woman who keeps watch at the gate. Hanumān strikes her with his left hand, holding back his full force because she is a woman.

Her words are a turning point: Brahmā had told her that when a vānara defeated her, the rākṣasas’ time would be up. She lets him in and blesses his search.

Laṅkā itself is described in long cantos: golden walls, gateways set with jewels, palaces like clouds, the flying chariot Puṣpaka. Vālmīki makes the enemy city magnificent.

THE NIGHT SEARCH

Through Rāvaṇa’s palaces
LaṅkāVālmīki describes golden palaces, gardens, the Puṣpaka chariot, feasting and music
Rāvaṇa asleepHe saw Rāvaṇa among his queens
MandodarīFor a moment he thought the chief queen was Sītā, and danced with joy
Second thoughtSītā, parted from Rāma, would not sleep, eat or dress like this. It was not her
ScrupleHe worried about looking at sleeping women; he decided his mind was untouched

The night search is a long, detailed tour of Rāvaṇa’s palace, its sleeping queens and drunken revelries. Vālmīki uses it to show the wealth and sensuality of Laṅkā, in contrast to Sītā’s grief.

The Mandodarī moment shows Hanumān’s reasoning: he rejoices, then corrects himself by thinking about Sītā’s character. Judgement over first impressions.

His scruple about looking at sleeping women, and his conclusion that his intention was pure, is often cited as an example of moral self-examination.

HANUMĀN’S DESPAIR

When the search failed
NothingHe searched every house and did not find her
Dark thoughtsHe feared Sītā was dead and thought of the grief this would bring Rāma, Sugrīva and all
ResolveHe resolved not to return without news
PrayerHe bowed to Rāma, Sītā, Lakṣmaṇa and the gods, and looked again
Aśoka groveHe saw one garden he had not searched

ZAPPY: Even Hanumān despaired?

PROF. BOLT: Vālmīki lets him. Then he got up and looked one more time.

Hanumān’s despair is one of the most human passages of the epic. He imagines returning empty-handed: Rāma dying of grief, Lakṣmaṇa following, Bharata and the mothers, Sugrīva, the whole vānara race. He thinks of staying in Laṅkā as an ascetic, or dying there.

Then he stops himself: despair is the root of failure, effort the root of success. That line, anirvedaḥ śriyo mūlam, “not losing heart is the root of fortune”, is still quoted as a motto.

He prays, gathers himself, and sees the Aśoka grove.

THE AŚOKA GROVE

Sītā found
The treeHe hid in a śiṃśapā tree
SītāA woman in a single soiled garment, thin, weeping, surrounded by fierce rākṣasīs
RecognitionHe matched her to Rāma’s descriptions and the jewels; he knew her
JoyHe wept and thanked Rāma

Sītā sits under the śiṃśapā tree, wearing the same yellow garment she had on when abducted, now soiled. Vālmīki compares her to a moon hidden by clouds, a flame covered by smoke.

Hanumān recognises her by reasoning, checking her against the jewels he had seen on Ṛṣyamūka: the ones she still wears match the gaps.

He weeps for her. Vālmīki makes clear that this is the emotional heart of the book.

RĀVAṆA AND SĪTĀ

A blade of grass between them
Rāvaṇa comesAt dawn Rāvaṇa came with his queens, begging and threatening
The grassSītā placed a blade of grass between them and spoke to it, not to him
Her answerShe told him to return her to Rāma and save himself
DeadlineRāvaṇa gave her two months, then left in rage
ThreatsThe rākṣasīs tormented her

Rāvaṇa’s visit is both pleading and menace. He offers her his kingdom and all his queens as servants; then, refused, he threatens to have her cut up for his breakfast after two months.

Sītā’s blade of grass (tṛṇa) is a famous gesture. Speaking to a grass blade rather than to him shows her contempt and her refusal to acknowledge him directly.

Some of Rāvaṇa’s wives, including Dhānyamālinī, coax him away. The rākṣasī guards then press Sītā with threats.

TRIJAṬĀ’S DREAM

A rākṣasī on Sītā’s side
TrijaṭāAn old rākṣasī woke and told the others to stop
The dreamShe had dreamt of Rāma in white on an ivory chariot, and Rāvaṇa shaven, in red, riding south on a donkey
MeaningRāvaṇa’s fall and Rāma’s victory
RespiteThe rākṣasīs fell back

Trijaṭā is often said to be Vibhīṣaṇa’s daughter in later tellings. In Vālmīki she is an old rākṣasī whose dream foretells the war: Rāma on a white chariot, Laṅkā sinking into the sea, Rāvaṇa riding south, the direction of death.

Her dream turns the guards from torment to caution. In later Rāmāyaṇas she becomes Sītā’s friend and comforter in captivity.

THE SONG FROM THE TREE

How to speak to Sītā
DangerSītā, in despair, was thinking of ending her life
A dilemmaHow could a monkey speak to her without frightening her or alerting the guards?
LanguageHe chose the speech of ordinary people, not a priest’s Sanskrit, so she would not take him for Rāvaṇa in disguise
Rāma’s storyHe softly recited Rāma’s story from the tree
Sītā looks upShe saw a small monkey and thought she was dreaming

ZAPPY: He sang to her first?

PROF. BOLT: He told her Rāma's story. Only then did he show himself.

Hanumān’s dilemma is a fine example of his intelligence. If he spoke Sanskrit like a brahmin, Sītā might think it was Rāvaṇa in disguise, as he had been when he abducted her. If he showed himself suddenly, she might scream and bring the guards.

So he decides on mānuṣī vāk, ordinary human speech, and begins with Rāma’s story, told softly from the tree. The first thing Sītā hears in Laṅkā that is not a threat is her husband’s name.

This scene is why the Sundara Kāṇḍa is so loved: the strongest being in the story chooses gentleness.

THE RING

Proof from Rāma
DoubtSītā feared he was a trick of Rāvaṇa’s
QuestionsShe tested him on Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa; he described them exactly
The ringHe gave her Rāma’s ring; she held it as if she held Rāma
HopeFor the first time since her abduction, she was happy

Sītā’s questioning is careful. Hanumān describes Rāma’s body in detail, from his eyes to the marks on his feet, and tells her how Rāma and Sugrīva met. Only then does he show the ring.

Her joy at the ring is described as if Rāma himself had come. In many paintings this is the chosen scene: Sītā under the tree, Hanumān small before her, the ring between them.

“CLIMB ON MY BACK”

The offer Sītā refused
The offerHanumān offered to carry her back across the sea at once
Her reasonsShe feared falling in the fight; she would not willingly touch another man; and Rāma himself should come and win her back
RespectHanumān accepted her reasons
Her messageTell Rāma to come within the month

ZAPPY: He could have saved her right there!

PROF. BOLT: She said no. It had to be Rāma, and she chose.

Sītā’s refusal is one of the most discussed passages. She gives practical reasons, fearing the fall or a fight in the air, and principled ones: she would not willingly touch a man other than Rāma, and Rāma’s honour required him to come himself.

Hanumān grows to his vast size to show he could do it, and she is impressed, but holds firm. He accepts. Readers see in this both Sītā’s agency and the epic’s ideal of Rāma’s duty.

THE CREST-JEWEL

Sītā’s sign for Rāma
The crowShe told a story only Rāma knew: a crow pecked her at Citrakūṭa, and Rāma loosed a grass-blade weapon that chased it through the worlds
The jewelShe gave Hanumān her cūḍāmaṇi, the jewel from her hair
Her pleaTell Rāma: if he loosed a weapon for a crow, why not for me?
PartingHanumān bowed and left

The crow story (kākāsura) happened at Citrakūṭa: a crow, said to be Indra’s son Jayanta, pecked Sītā; Rāma loosed a blade of grass charged as the brahmāstra, and it chased the crow through all the worlds until it fell at Rāma’s feet. Rāma spared its life but took one of its eyes.

Only Sītā and Rāma knew this story, so it would prove the message came from her. The cūḍāmaṇi, a jewel from her hair given at her wedding, is the physical token.

THE GROVE DESTROYED

Testing Laṅkā’s strength
WhyHe decided to measure Rāvaṇa’s forces before leaving
DestructionHe tore up the Aśoka grove, except Sītā’s tree
BattlesHe killed the guards, Jambumālī, the ministers’ sons, five generals and Rāvaṇa’s son Akṣa
IndrajitRāvaṇa’s son Indrajit bound him with the brahmāstra
Let himself be caughtThe boon protected him, but he stayed bound to meet Rāvaṇa

Hanumān’s reasoning is a soldier’s: a messenger who sees the enemy’s capital should also test its army. The destruction of the grove brings out guard after guard, then senior commanders, then Rāvaṇa’s young son Akṣa, whom Hanumān kills in a fierce fight.

Indrajit, Rāvaṇa’s greatest son, binds him with the brahmāstra. Hanumān knows Brahmā’s boon makes him immune within moments, but stays bound to be taken before Rāvaṇa. The rākṣasas then bind him with ropes, which breaks the astra’s power.

IN RĀVAṆA’S COURT

The messenger speaks
RāvaṇaHanumān admired his splendour and was struck by it
The messageHe told Rāvaṇa to return Sītā, or Rāma would destroy Laṅkā
Death sentenceRāvaṇa ordered him killed
VibhīṣaṇaRāvaṇa’s brother said an envoy must not be killed; punish him another way
The tailRāvaṇa ordered his tail wrapped in cloth and set alight

ZAPPY: Vibhīṣaṇa saved him?

PROF. BOLT: The first sign Vibhīṣaṇa would side with dharma.

Hanumān’s view of Rāvaṇa is generous: he admires the king’s majesty and thinks that if he were not so unrighteous he could rule even heaven. Vālmīki never makes Rāvaṇa a cartoon.

His speech is a model of diplomacy: he introduces himself as Rāma’s envoy, recounts Rāma’s deeds, and urges Rāvaṇa to return Sītā for his own good.

Vibhīṣaṇa’s defence of the envoy is the first time he openly opposes his brother. He will soon leave Laṅkā for Rāma.

LAṄKĀ BURNS

The tail that set a city alight
ParadedThey paraded him through the streets with his burning tail
Cool fireSītā, hearing of it, prayed to Agni; the fire did not burn him
The burningHe broke free and leapt from roof to roof, setting Laṅkā ablaze
Fear for SītāThen he panicked: had he burned Sītā too?
SafeHe heard she was unharmed and went to see her once more

Sītā prays to Agni that if she has been faithful, the fire should be cool for Hanumān. It is: the tail burns, but he feels no heat. He understands this as Sītā’s power.

The burning of Laṅkā is told in vivid detail: golden palaces melting, rākṣasas fleeing, the fire leaping from house to house. Vibhīṣaṇa’s house alone is spared.

His panic for Sītā, and the relief when he hears from passing sages that she is safe, show again that even Hanumān can fear.

“SEEN SĪTĀ!”

The return and the news
Leap backHe leapt from Ariṣṭa mountain back across the sea, roaring
JoyThe waiting vānaras danced
MadhuvanaOn the way they raided Sugrīva’s honey grove, beating its guard Dadhimukha
SugrīvaHe knew at once: they would not dare unless they had succeeded
First wordsTo Rāma, Hanumān said first “dṛṣṭā sītā”: “Sītā is seen”

ZAPPY: Why say 'seen' first?

PROF. BOLT: So Rāma wouldn't suffer even a moment of doubt.

The Madhuvana episode is comic relief. The exhausted, triumphant vānaras raid Sugrīva’s royal honey grove, drink themselves silly, and beat up its keeper Dadhimukha, Sugrīva’s uncle. When he complains, Sugrīva laughs: they would never dare unless they had found Sītā.

Hanumān’s first words to Rāma, dṛṣṭā sītā, are famous for their order: “Seen, Sītā” puts the good news first. Commentators say he did not want Rāma to hear the name alone and fear the worst for even a moment.

RĀMA’S EMBRACE

The reward
The jewelRāma held Sītā’s cūḍāmaṇi and wept
PraiseHe said Hanumān had done what no one else could
His gift“All I have to give is this embrace”, and he embraced him
MeaningFor devotees, the highest reward: Rāma’s own arms

Rāma’s words are among the most quoted in Hanumān devotion: he has done what no one could, and Rāma has nothing worthy to give him except an embrace. Many temples show this scene.

The book ends here, with the army preparing to march south. The next book, the Yuddha Kāṇḍa, is the war.

READING THE SUNDARA KĀṆḌA

The book people recite
PārāyaṇaRecited whole, often over days, for help in hard times
WhyIt moves from despair to hope; devotees say it removes obstacles
In homesRead in Telugu, Kannada, Tamil, Hindi and Sanskrit households
TulasīdāsaHis Sundara Kāṇḍa, from the Rāmacaritamānas, is chanted in temples of the north, especially on Tuesdays

Sundara Kāṇḍa pārāyaṇa is a widespread practice, often over seven, nine or more days, sometimes one sarga a day for 68 days. Families do it for illness, exams, weddings or jobs.

In the north, Tulasīdāsa’s Sundara Kāṇḍa from the Rāmacaritamānas is sung by groups on Tuesday evenings, sometimes with harmonium and drums.
