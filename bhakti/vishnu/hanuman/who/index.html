WHO IS HANUMĀN?

HANUMĀN · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Son of the Wind, servant of Rāma

FOREWORD

Walk into almost any village in India and you will find him at the edge of it: a small orange figure with a mace, smeared with sindūr, guarding the road. Hanumān is everywhere. He is the strongest being in the Rāmāyaṇa and also its humblest. He leapt an ocean, carried a mountain and burned a city, and asked for nothing except to serve Rāma.

In Vālmīki’s Rāmāyaṇa he is a minister of the monkey king Sugrīva and the son of the Wind. Later tradition makes him a portion of Śiva, a model brahmacārī, a scholar of grammar, the healer of Lakṣmaṇa and one of the seven who never die. In Karnataka he is also Prāṇadevaru, the life-breath itself.

This first part is the reference page for Hanumān himself: who he is, what he is called, how he is shown and worshipped. The next four tell his story from birth to the end of the war, and the last visits his temples.

ZAPPY: Why does every village have a Hanumān?

PROF. BOLT: He guards. People put him at the boundary so nothing bad gets in.

Hanumān belongs to the oldest layer of the Rāma story. In Vālmīki he enters in the fourth book, the Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa, and from then on the epic turns on him: he finds the ally, finds Sītā, finds the herb and brings the news of victory. Without him Rāma’s search would have no direction.

His worship as a god of his own grew over many centuries. Early images show him as an attendant in Rāma panels; by the Vijayanagara period in the south and the Bhakti poets in the north he had his own shrines, hymns and days. Today he may be the most widely worshipped figure in Hinduism after Rāma and Kṛṣṇa, with small shrines on roadsides, in police stations, gyms and hospitals.

Part of his appeal is that he asks so little. No elaborate ritual is needed: a lamp, a little oil, the name of Rāma and a few lines of the Cālīsā are enough. He is the god of the person who has a hard day ahead.

Indian names often pile up as epithets, and Hanumān has hundreds; there are aṣṭottara (108) and sahasra (1,000) name lists for him. They fall into a few families: names from his family (Āñjaneya, Kesarīnandana), from the Wind (Māruti, Pavanasuta), from his deeds (Laṅkādahana, “burner of Laṅkā”; Sañjīvanīdhara, “bearer of the life-herb”), and from what he does for devotees (Saṅkaṭmocan).

The name “Hanumān” itself puzzled early scholars. The usual reading, from the epic, is “the one with a (marked) jaw”. Some modern linguists have suggested a Dravidian root, ān-manti, “male monkey”, Sanskritised into Hanumān; this remains a scholarly guess, not tradition.

Regional names show how far he travelled: Āñjaneya in Telugu and Kannada lands, Anuman in Tamil, Māruti in Marathi, Bajrangbalī and Mahāvīr across the Hindi belt, Hanuman in Thai and Hanoman in Javanese.

WHOSE SON?

Three ways of telling it
VālmīkiAñjanā, an apsaras born as a vānara, was the wife of Kesarī; the Wind god Vāyu embraced her and she bore Hanumān. So he is called the son of both
Śiva PurāṇaHanumān is an aṃśa of Śiva, born to serve Viṣṇu as Rāma; hence “Rudrāvatāra” in many hymns
Other Purāṇic tellingsSome say a portion of the divine pudding (pāyasa) from Daśaratha’s sacrifice was carried by a kite to Añjanā
MādhvaHanumān is the first avatāra of Vāyu (Mukhyaprāṇa); Bhīma is the second, and the teacher Madhva the third

ZAPPY: So which one is true?

PROF. BOLT: Each school tells it its own way. They all agree he was born to serve Rāma.

The full story: Birth and Boyhood

These tellings are not contradictions for devotees but layers. A Mādhva will say Prāṇadevaru; a Śaiva temple will call him Rudrāvatāra; a Rāma bhakta will simply say Rāmadūta. Many hymns hold all of them at once: the Cālīsā calls him both “Śaṅkara-suvana” (Śiva’s son or portion) and “Pavana-tanaya” (son of the Wind) in the space of a few verses.

Vālmīki’s own account is told twice: Jāmbavān tells it to Hanumān in the Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa to remind him of his strength, and the sage Agastya tells it again to Rāma in the Uttara Kāṇḍa. The Wind’s role is a blessing rather than a physical fatherhood: Vāyu tells Añjanā that her son will equal him in speed and strength.

The pāyasa story ties Hanumān to Rāma from before birth: the same sacred food that made Rāma and his brothers also made Hanumān. It appears in later Rāmāyaṇas and in some Purāṇic retellings, not in Vālmīki.

HIS FORMS

How Hanumān is shown
Dāsa HanumānThe servant: kneeling or standing with folded hands before Rāma; the form in Rāma temples
Vīra HanumānThe hero: striding, mace on the shoulder, tail raised; the guardian at village edges
Sañjīvī HanumānFlying with the mountain of herbs on one palm
Abhaya HanumānRight hand raised in “fear not”
Yoga HanumānSeated in meditation, as at Ghaṭikācalam (Sholingur) and Hampi’s Yantroddhāraka shrine

Hanumān images follow a few clear rules. Facing Rāma, he is small and bent in service. Facing the world, he is large, striding and armed. In Rāma temples he usually stands in front of or to the side of the main shrine, looking towards Rāma, like Garuḍa before Viṣṇu or Nandi before Śiva.

Many village and wayside Hanumāns are carved in low relief on a single stone slab, walking to the right with the tail curled over the head, one hand raised and a mace in the other. These slabs, often several centuries old, are why the striding Vīra form is the one most people picture.

Giant standing images are a newer fashion: 30-metre and taller figures now stand at Shimla (Jakhu), Paritala in Andhra, and Biḍadi and other places near Bengaluru. They follow the Vīra form, made large to be seen from the road.

PAÑCAMUKHA

The five-faced Hanumān
EastHanumān: removes impurity of mind
SouthNarasiṃha: removes fear
WestGaruḍa: removes poison and black magic
NorthVarāha: grants prosperity
UpwardHayagrīva: grants knowledge
StoryA later tradition: Hanumān took five faces to blow out five lamps at once and kill the demon Ahirāvaṇa (Mahirāvaṇa), who had carried Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa to the underworld
WherePañcamukha shrines at Kumbakonam, Thiruvallur and Mantrālayam’s Pañcamukhi hill, among many

ZAPPY: Five faces? Isn't that a bit much?

PROF. BOLT: Each face is a protection. People pray to the one they need.

The Ahirāvaṇa story comes from later regional Rāmāyaṇas, especially in Bengal and Odisha, and from the Ānanda Rāmāyaṇa tradition. Rāvaṇa’s ally Ahirāvaṇa (Mahirāvaṇa) drugs Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa and carries them to Pātāla to sacrifice them to his goddess. His life is hidden in five lamps burning in five directions, so Hanumān takes five faces and puts them all out in one breath.

Each added face is itself a deity linked to Viṣṇu: Narasiṃha and Varāha are avatāras, Garuḍa is his mount and Hayagrīva his horse-headed form of knowledge. So the Pañcamukha image is also a theology: Hanumān as a doorway to all of Viṣṇu’s protective forms.

Pañcamukha images are usually ten-armed, holding weapons including the mace, axe, trident, discus and a tree or mountain. They are popular in Tamil Nadu and in Mādhva and Vaiṣṇava homes as protective icons.

WHAT HE CARRIES

Mace, mountain, sindūr, tail
GadāThe mace: strength used in service
The mountainDroṇagiri or the Oṣadhi hill of herbs: healing, and doing more than asked
SindūrOrange vermilion smeared over his body. A North Indian tale: he saw Sītā wear sindūr for Rāma’s long life, and covered himself in it
OilSesame oil poured over the image, especially on Saturdays
The tailRaised high in Vīra forms; the tail that burned Laṅkā
Rāma’s nameOften written on his body or on the stones of the bridge; he is never shown far from it
KapidhvajaHis image on Arjuna’s chariot flag at Kurukṣetra

The mace is the weapon of strength without trickery: it has no edge and wins by force alone. Hanumān and Bhīma, the two sons of Vāyu, both fight with it.

Sindūr worship gives his images their bright orange colour; over decades of smearing, the original carving can disappear under layers of vermilion and oil. Many temples periodically strip the layers to reveal the stone, a rite called cola or kavaca changing.

The tail matters more than it looks. In Laṅkā, Rāvaṇa’s court decided the best insult for a monkey was to burn his tail; Hanumān let them wrap it and set it alight, then used it to burn the city. A tail held high therefore means a humiliation turned into victory.

THE PERFECT SERVANT

What Vālmīki says of him
SpeechWhen Rāma first heard him at Ṛṣyamūka, he told Lakṣmaṇa no one could speak like that without mastering the Ṛg, Yajur and Sāma Vedas and grammar: not one wrong word
BuddhiWisdom: he judges people and moments well, as when he chooses how to reveal himself to Sītā
BalaStrength: he can grow huge or tiny at will
RestraintTradition holds him a lifelong brahmacārī
HumilityAfter every great deed he credits Rāma
ForgettingA boyhood curse made him forget his powers until reminded; Jāmbavān reminds him before the leap

ZAPPY: He forgot he was strong?

PROF. BOLT: Until someone reminded him. Most of us need that reminder too.

Vālmīki gives Hanumān a set of virtues that later writers repeat as a list: dhṛti (steadiness), dṛṣṭi (insight), mati (judgement) and dākṣya (skill). In the Sundara Kāṇḍa he weighs his choices aloud: whether to speak to Sītā in Sanskrit or the common tongue, how not to frighten her, and how to test Laṅkā’s strength before leaving.

His power to change size is a running theme. He becomes huge to leap the sea, tiny to slip into Laṅkā at night, and huge again in the mouth of the sea-monster Surasā, then tiny to escape through her ear. It is a lesson in using the right amount of force.

Tradition adds that he studied with the Sun god himself, keeping pace with the Sun’s chariot across the sky, and so became master of grammar. That is why scholars and students also pray to him.

THE ONE WHO STAYS

Cirañjīvī: living on
Rāma’s boonIn the Uttara Kāṇḍa, when Rāma is leaving the world, Hanumān asks to stay as long as Rāma’s story is told; Rāma grants it
The sevenA well-known verse names seven cirañjīvīs: Aśvatthāmā, Bali, Vyāsa, Hanumān, Vibhīṣaṇa, Kṛpa and Paraśurāma
Where Rāma is toldA common belief: wherever the Rāmāyaṇa is recited, Hanumān is present, so a seat is left empty for him
Gandhamādana · KimpuruṣaThe Purāṇas place him on the Gandhamādana mountain or in Kimpuruṣa-varṣa, chanting Rāma’s name

aśvatthāmā balir vyāso hanūmāṃś ca vibhīṣaṇaḥ
kṛpaḥ paraśurāmaś ca saptaite cirajīvinaḥ

Aśvatthāmā, Bali, Vyāsa, Hanumān and Vibhīṣaṇa, Kṛpa and Paraśurāma: these seven live on.

The cirañjīvī verse is usually recited with a second line promising long life to anyone who remembers these seven, along with Mārkaṇḍeya. It is said at birthdays and other rites in many homes.

The belief that Hanumān is present at Rāmāyaṇa recitals shapes how those recitals are done. Many kathā leaders place a low seat or a cloth for him, and the Rāmacaritamānas is often begun and ended with Hanumān’s praise. Stories are told of devotees who met an old stranger at a recital who vanished at the end.

Tulasīdāsa himself, by tradition, was led to Rāma by Hanumān: a spirit told him to look for an old man who came first and left last at a Rāmāyaṇa reading in Vārāṇasī. That man, the story says, was Hanumān, and the Saṅkaṭ Mocan temple stands where they met.

IN THE MAHĀBHĀRATA

The old monkey on the path
Bhīma meets himIn the Vana Parva, Bhīma, looking for the Saugandhika flower, finds an old monkey lying across the path. Told to move his tail aside, Bhīma cannot lift it
BrothersThe monkey is Hanumān, his elder brother: both are sons of Vāyu. He shows Bhīma a glimpse of the form that crossed the sea
The promiseHe promises to sit on Arjuna’s flag and roar with Bhīma in battle
KapidhvajaSo Arjuna’s chariot, driven by Kṛṣṇa, flies the monkey banner

ZAPPY: Bhīma couldn't lift a tail?

PROF. BOLT: Pride met humility. That was the lesson.

The Bhīma episode is told in the Tīrthayātrā section of the Vana Parva. Draupadī finds a fragrant Saugandhika flower carried on the wind and asks Bhīma for more; he crashes through the Gandhamādana forest, scattering animals, until the old monkey blocks the way.

When Bhīma asks him to grow into the form that crossed the sea, Hanumān says that age has passed, but shows it anyway, so vast that Bhīma has to shut his eyes. He then teaches Bhīma about the four yugas and the duties of a king, a reminder that strength must be guided by dharma.

Arjuna’s monkey banner appears at Kurukṣetra. Some later tellings add that when the war ended and Hanumān left the flag, Arjuna’s chariot burst into flames, having been protected all along by Kṛṣṇa and Hanumān.

OTHER HANUMĀNS

Jain, Southeast Asian and folk tellings
JainIn Vimalasūri’s Prakrit Paumacariya, Hanumān is a vidyādhara prince, not a monkey; he marries and finally becomes a Jain ascetic
ThailandHanuman of the Ramakien is a dashing white monkey hero with wives and children
Indonesia · CambodiaA favourite of shadow-puppet (wayang) and masked dance; in Cambodia’s Reamker he loves the mermaid Suvannamaccha
MakaradhvajaLater tellings give him a son born of a drop of his sweat swallowed by a great fish
AhirāvaṇaThe underworld rescue story, loved in Bengal, Odisha and the north, is from later Rāmāyaṇas, not Vālmīki

Vimalasūri’s Paumacariya, in Prakrit, is usually dated to the early centuries CE and is the oldest Jain Rāmāyaṇa. Jain authors rejected the idea of a talking monkey army; they made the vānaras a royal clan of vidyādharas whose banner bore a monkey. Hanumān marries several princesses and in the end renounces the world.

In Southeast Asia the Rāmāyaṇa travelled with traders and kings from the first millennium CE. Hanuman there is often a playful hero and lover, quite unlike the celibate Indian ideal, and is a favourite role in Thai khon and Javanese wayang.

Within India too, regional Rāmāyaṇas add episodes: the Kṛttivāsī in Bengal, the Jagamohana in Odisha and folk tellings across the Deccan give Hanumān new adventures in the underworld, at Rāvaṇa’s court and after the war.

HOW HE IS WORSHIPPED

Days, offerings and vows
Tuesday (Maṅgalavāra)His chief day across North India
Saturday (Śanivāra)A folk story: Hanumān freed Śani from Rāvaṇa’s prison, and Śani promised not to trouble Hanumān’s devotees
Sindūr and oilVermilion mixed with sesame oil is smeared on the image
GarlandsVaḍa-mālā (garlands of urad vaḍas), veṭrilai (betel-leaf) garlands and butter coating in Tamil Nadu
RecitationThe Hanumān Cālīsā, the Sundara Kāṇḍa and Rāma nāma
Rāma firstMany temples say: worship Rāma and Hanumān is pleased

ZAPPY: Why butter?

PROF. BOLT: It is cooling. Devotees say it soothes him after carrying the mountain.

Tuesday is Maṅgala’s day, ruled by Mars, the red warrior planet; red and orange offerings fit both. Many people fast on Tuesdays, eat one meal and visit a Hanumān temple in the evening.

The Saturday link with Śani has become one of the strongest in popular Hinduism. Astrologers often advise Hanumān worship during Śani’s difficult periods (sāḍe-sātī). Hanumān and Śani are sometimes worshipped in adjacent shrines.

In Mādhva and other South Indian homes, Prāṇadevaru is worshipped daily along with Viṣṇu. Many households keep a small Hanumān near the door. Women can and do worship him freely, though some temples traditionally keep women from touching the image because of his celibacy; practice varies.

HANUMĀN JAYANTĪ

One birthday, many dates
North IndiaCaitra Pūrṇimā, the full moon of March–April
KarnatakaHanumad Vrata: Mārgaśīrṣa Śukla Trayodaśī (November–December)
Andhra · TelanganaVaiśākha Kṛṣṇa Daśamī, ending a 41-day Hanumān dīkṣā begun on Caitra Pūrṇimā
Tamil Nadu · KeralaThe new moon of Mārgaḻi (Dhanu), usually with Mūla nakṣatra (December–January)
OdishaPana Saṅkrānti, the Odia new year in mid-April

ZAPPY: Why so many birthdays?

PROF. BOLT: No one agrees on the day. So everyone celebrates on theirs.

Some traditions mark two days: one for his birth and one, Hanumān Vijayotsava, for his victory or his meeting with Rāma. The variety comes from different calendars (lunar months reckoned from full moon or new moon) and different stories of when he was born.

Typical rites are the same everywhere: a dawn bath, sindūr and oil for the image, a full recitation of the Cālīsā or the Sundara Kāṇḍa, processions with saffron flags, and wrestling matches in Maharashtra and the north.

In Andhra and Telangana devotees wear a mālā of beads and saffron or red clothes for the 41-day Hanumān dīkṣā, much like the Ayyappa vow, and climb to Koṇḍagaṭṭu and other hill temples at its end.

HANUMĀN IN KARNATAKA

Añjanādri, Prāṇadevaru and 732 idols
AñjanādriThe hill near Hampi across the Tuṅgabhadrā is held by tradition to be his birthplace; 575 steps lead to the white temple on top. Other places also claim the birth
KiṣkindhāThe boulder country of Hampi is the monkey kingdom of the Rāmāyaṇa by tradition
YantroddhārakaAt Hampi, Vyāsatīrtha’s Hanumān seated in a hexagonal yantra
732 PrāṇadevarusTradition credits the 16th-century saint Vyāsatīrtha with installing 732 Hanumān images across the Vijayanagara lands
Village guardianĀñjaneya stands at the edge of many villages and forts
Next door: Hanumān’s temples

Hampi’s identification with Kiṣkindhā is old: Vijayanagara inscriptions and temple carvings already use the Rāmāyaṇa geography, and the Hazāra Rāma temple’s walls tell the whole story. Locals point out Mālyavanta hill, Ṛṣyamūka, the cave of Sugrīva and the bank where Rāma waited out the rains.

Vyāsatīrtha (Vyāsarāja), rājaguru to Kṛṣṇadevarāya, is the great name of Hanumān worship in the south. His images often show Hanumān with a raised right hand, a flower or fruit in the left, and the tail curling over his head with a small bell at its tip; they can be recognised across Karnataka and Andhra.

Karnataka has a third layer: Mādhva tradition sees Madhvācārya himself as the third avatāra of Vāyu, after Hanumān and Bhīma. So the Hanumān in a Mādhva maṭha stands for the whole line of Vāyu’s service to Viṣṇu.

MĀRUTI OF MAHARASHTRA

Samartha Rāmadāsa’s eleven
Samartha RāmadāsaThe 17th-century saint, a great Hanumān devotee, founded eleven Māruti temples in the Krishna valley
Every villageA Māruti temple at the village gate, and a wrestling ground (ākhāḍā) beside it
WrestlersHanumān is the patron of the ākhāḍā; wrestlers salute him before a bout
BhīmarūpīRāmadāsa’s Marathi Māruti Stotra begins “bhīmarūpī mahārudrā”

ZAPPY: Wrestlers pray to him?

PROF. BOLT: Who else? He's the strongest one there is.

Samartha Rāmadāsa (1608–1681) was a Rāma bhakta who saw Hanumān as the model of a strong, disciplined devotee. He set up the eleven Māruti temples around Satara and Sangli, including Cāphaḷ, Śahāpūr and Śiṅgaṇavāḍī, and founded maṭhs with gymnasiums so young men would build body and character together.

His Marathi hymn to Māruti, sung in Maharashtrian homes and schools, praises Hanumān as Mahārudra and as the Wind’s son who flew with the mountain. The idea of a village Māruti beside the ākhāḍā spread across the Deccan from this movement.

The wrestling world still keeps it: bouts begin with a salute to the Hanumān image, and a champion wrestler may be called a “Hanumān” or “Bajrangī” by fans.

Verse by verse: Hanumān Cālīsā · Saṅkaṭmocan Aṣṭak · Sanskrit ślokas

Most Hanumān hymns are in the language of the people, not Sanskrit. The Cālīsā is in Awadhi, Rāmadāsa’s stotra in Marathi and the Daṇḍakam in Telugu. That fits Hanumān: he is approached directly, without a priest.

Tulasīdāsa (c. 1532–1623) also wrote the Vinaya Patrikā, which opens with prayers to Hanumān among others, and the Hanumān Bāhuk, written according to tradition during a plague, when his arm was in pain.

Three of them, the Cālīsā, the Saṅkaṭmocan Aṣṭak and the Sanskrit ślokas, are taken verse by verse in our Hymns door, with the original in transliteration, a plain-English meaning and a deep dive, like our Bhagavad Gītā.

WHAT HANUMĀN TEACHES

Strength in service
Power with humilityThe strongest one in the story calls himself only a servant
Remember who you areHe needed Jāmbavān to remind him of his strength
Do more than askedAsked for one herb, he brought the mountain
Speak wellRāma admired his speech before his strength
Devotion is a pathBhakti, not birth or form, made him great

ZAPPY: What should I take from Hanumān?

PROF. BOLT: Be strong, and use it for something bigger than yourself.

Hanumān is often called the ideal of dāsya bhakti, devotion as a servant, one of the nine forms of bhakti. Unlike the friend-devotion of Arjuna or the love-devotion of the gopīs, dāsya keeps the devotee small and the Lord great, and finds freedom in that.

There is also a Vedāntic reading, often quoted: Rāma asks Hanumān how he sees him. Hanumān answers that as a body he is Rāma’s servant, as a soul he is a part of Rāma, and in truth he and Rāma are one. The verse appears in later collections, not Vālmīki, but sums up how many schools read him.

For ordinary people the lessons are practical: courage in fear, clear speech under pressure, loyalty to a friend, and the habit of giving credit away.

A few more terms readers will meet in his hymns and temples: Gadādhara, “bearer of the mace”; Rāmabhakta, “devotee of Rāma”; Laṅkādahana, “burner of Laṅkā”; Sañjīvanī, the herb that restores life; Kapi, monkey; Vāyu, Pavana and Māruta, all names of the Wind.

Hanumān in six lines
Who. Son of Añjanā, Kesarī and the Wind; minister of Sugrīva; Rāma’s messenger.
Traditions. A portion of Śiva; for Mādhvas the first avatāra of Vāyu.
Forms. Dāsa, Vīra, Sañjīvī, Yoga and the five-faced Pañcamukha.
Lives on. A cirañjīvī, present wherever Rāma’s story is told.
Worship. Tuesday and Saturday; sindūr, oil, vaḍas, butter; the Cālīsā.
Lesson. Strength, wisdom and humility, all for something higher.

ZAPPY: One line to remember?

PROF. BOLT: Wherever Rāma's name is said, Hanumān is listening.

This part has given the who, the forms and the worship. The next four parts tell the story itself, in Vālmīki’s order: the birth and the leap at the sun; Kiṣkindhā and the meeting with Rāma; the Sundara Kāṇḍa’s leap to Laṅkā; and the war, the mountain and the long life afterwards.

SOURCES & NOTES

Where the facts come from

Texts: Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa (Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa 3 on Hanumān’s speech; Kiṣkindhā 66 on his birth and jaw; Uttara Kāṇḍa on his boyhood and Rāma’s boon); Mahābhārata, Vana Parva (Bhīma and Hanumān); Śiva Purāṇa, Śatarudra Saṃhitā (Hanumān as Śiva’s aṃśa); Vimalasūri, Paumacariya; Tulasīdāsa, Hanumān Cālīsā and Hanumān Bāhuk; Samartha Rāmadāsa, Māruti Stotra.

Festivals: regional Jayantī dates from Drik Panchang and standard almanacs. Karnataka: Añjanādri and Yantroddhāraka from Karnataka Tourism and Hampi guides; the 732 images from Mādhva tradition.

Sources differ / unverified: his birthplace (Añjanādri near Hampi, Añjaneri near Nashik, Tirumala’s Añjanādri and Gumla in Jharkhand all claim it); the Śani, sindūr, Pañcamukha and Makaradhvaja stories are later or folk traditions, not Vālmīki; the authorship of the Cālīsā and Bajrang Bāṇ is traditional. Meanings are our own plain-English renderings. Zappy and Prof. Bolt are fictional.
