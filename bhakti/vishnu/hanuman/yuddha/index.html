HANUMĀN · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The mountain, the necklace, the farewell

FOREWORD

When the war comes, Hanumān is everywhere: vouching for an enemy’s brother, fighting Rāvaṇa’s generals, carrying the wounded Lakṣmaṇa, and twice flying to the Himalaya for a mountain of herbs when the army lies dying.

After the victory he carries the news to Sītā, runs ahead to Bharata, receives Sītā’s pearl necklace, and when Rāma leaves the world, asks only to stay as long as Rāma’s story is told.

This part follows Vālmīki and marks clearly where later tellings add their own episodes.

ZAPPY: He did all that too?

PROF. BOLT: The war needed him almost as much as the search did.

Hanumān’s great solo work is done in the Sundara Kāṇḍa, but in the war he is everywhere: adviser, warrior, carrier, healer’s errand-runner and messenger. Vālmīki keeps giving him the decisive moments.

Many of the war episodes most loved today, like the floating stones, Kālanemi, Ahirāvaṇa and the torn chest, come from later Rāmāyaṇas and devotional poets. This part follows Vālmīki first and marks the later layers clearly.

VIBHĪṢAṆA

Should the enemy’s brother be trusted?
The defectionThrown out by Rāvaṇa for urging peace, Vibhīṣaṇa flew across the sea to Rāma
SuspicionSugrīva and most leaders wanted him seized or killed as a spy
Hanumān’s viewHe judged Vibhīṣaṇa sincere: he came openly, at the right time, after being wronged
RāmaRāma accepted him: “I will not reject anyone who comes to me for refuge”
CrownedRāma anointed Vibhīṣaṇa king of Laṅkā on the spot

ZAPPY: Hanumān trusted him?

PROF. BOLT: He'd seen Vibhīṣaṇa defend him in Rāvaṇa's court. He read people well.

The debate over Vibhīṣaṇa is a council of war in miniature. Each leader gives his view; most counsel caution. Hanumān argues from evidence: Vibhīṣaṇa came openly, not secretly; he came after his brother insulted him; he knows Rāma’s strength. A spy would not behave like this.

Rāma’s answer is the famous śaraṇāgati verse: anyone who comes once and says “I am yours” will be given safety from all beings. It is central to Śrīvaiṣṇava theology of surrender.

The moment shows Hanumān not just as a strong arm but as a judge of character, the same skill Rāma praised at their first meeting.

THE BRIDGE

Across the sea
The oceanRāma fasted three days; when the Ocean did not come, he raised his bow; the Ocean appeared and advised a bridge
NalaThe vānara architect Nala, son of Viśvakarmā, led the building
MaterialsRocks, trees and mountains carried by the vānaras; the bridge was built in five days
Rāma’s nameA later tradition: stones with Rāma’s name written on them floated
The squirrelA loved folk story: a squirrel helped, and Rāma stroked its back, leaving three stripes

The bridge story is famous across India and Sri Lanka. A chain of shoals between Rāmeśvaram and Mannar is called Rāma Setu or Adam’s Bridge. Whether it is natural or man-made is a matter of debate; geologists consider it natural.

The squirrel and the floating stones come from later retellings, including Kamban and regional folk versions, and are beloved children’s stories. Nala’s skill as a bridge-builder is in Vālmīki.

Many traditions say Rāma worshipped Śiva at Rāmeśvaram before the crossing or after the war, and that Hanumān was sent to Kailāsa for a liṅga; we tell that story in our Rāmeśvaram guide.

HANUMĀN IN BATTLE

The demons he killed
Dhūmrākṣa“Smoke-eyed”, killed with a mountain peak
AkampanaThe “unshakable” general, felled with a tree
Devāntaka and TriśirasTwo of Rāvaṇa’s sons; Triśiras killed with his own sword
NikumbhaKumbhakarṇa’s son, killed in a wrestling fight
RāvaṇaHanumān struck Rāvaṇa a blow that made him reel; Rāvaṇa praised him as a worthy enemy

Vālmīki gives Hanumān a string of duels with Rāvaṇa’s generals and sons. His weapons are those of a vānara: mountain peaks, trees, boulders, fists and sometimes an enemy’s own sword.

The exchange of blows with Rāvaṇa is striking. Hanumān slaps or punches Rāvaṇa so hard he staggers; Rāvaṇa strikes back and calls Hanumān a hero. Hanumān answers that he is ashamed Rāvaṇa still lives after his blow.

His wrestling match with Nikumbha, Kumbhakarṇa’s son, is the kind of scene that made him patron of the ākhāḍā.

CARRYING LAKṢMAṆA

When Rāvaṇa could not lift him
The śaktiRāvaṇa struck Lakṣmaṇa down with a powerful dart
Too heavyRāvaṇa tried to carry him off but could not lift him
HanumānHe picked Lakṣmaṇa up lightly and carried him to Rāma
Rāma on HanumānRāma then rode into battle on Hanumān’s shoulders against Rāvaṇa

ZAPPY: Why couldn't Rāvaṇa lift him?

PROF. BOLT: Lakṣmaṇa was Viṣṇu's own portion. Only love could lift him, the old commentators say.

Commentators make much of the weight scene. Rāvaṇa, who had lifted Mount Kailāsa, cannot lift Lakṣmaṇa; Hanumān carries him easily. The reading: Lakṣmaṇa is a portion of Viṣṇu, heavy to an enemy, light to a devotee.

Rāma then mounts Hanumān’s shoulders to fight Rāvaṇa in his first direct clash. Hanumān as Rāma’s vehicle, like Garuḍa for Viṣṇu, is a favourite image.

THE FIRST MOUNTAIN

After Indrajit’s weapon
The disasterIndrajit’s brahmāstra struck down Rāma, Lakṣmaṇa and most of the army
JāmbavānBarely alive, the old bear asked only: “Is Hanumān alive?”
The errandHe sent Hanumān to the Himalaya, to a herb mountain between Ṛṣabha and Kailāsa
Four herbsMṛtasañjīvanī (restores life), Viśalyakaraṇī (draws out arrows), Sāvarṇyakaraṇī (restores skin), Sandhānakaraṇī (joins bones)
The peakThe herbs hid themselves; Hanumān tore off the whole peak and flew back with it

ZAPPY: Why did Jāmbavān ask only about Hanumān?

PROF. BOLT: If Hanumān lived, he said, the army lived, even if everyone else fell.

The question Jāmbavān asks is one of the most quoted lines about Hanumān. Vibhīṣaṇa, puzzled that the bear asks about Hanumān rather than Rāma, is told: if Hanumān lives, all of us are alive though dead; if he is gone, we are dead though alive.

The four herbs reflect the medical thinking of the epic: restoring life, removing embedded weapons, restoring the skin’s colour and healing fractures. Hanumān cannot tell them apart in the night, and the herbs hide; so he brings the whole peak.

When the mountain arrives, its fragrance alone revives the army. Hanumān then takes the mountain back to the Himalaya.

THE SECOND MOUNTAIN

Saving Lakṣmaṇa
Lakṣmaṇa fallsStruck again by Rāvaṇa’s śakti, Lakṣmaṇa lay dying
SuṣeṇaThe vānara physician sent Hanumān to the herb mountain Mahodaya
AgainAgain he brought the mountain
Lakṣmaṇa risesSuṣeṇa crushed the herb and held it to his nose; Lakṣmaṇa got up

Vālmīki tells the herb journey twice, the second time for Lakṣmaṇa alone. The physician Suṣeṇa names the herbs and the mountain; again Hanumān cannot find them and brings the peak.

This episode is the source of Hanumān’s image as a healer. In many hospitals and clinics in India a small Hanumān with a mountain stands at the entrance, and he is invoked against illness in the Cālīsā: “nāsai roga harai saba pīrā”.

THE MOUNTAIN IN LATER TELLINGS

Kālanemi, Bharata and Droṇagiri
KālanemiTulasīdāsa: Rāvaṇa sent the demon Kālanemi, disguised as a sage, to delay Hanumān; he saw through it
Bharata’s arrowFlying over Ayodhyā, Hanumān was shot down by Bharata, who took him for a demon; then sent on with Bharata’s blessing
DroṇagiriLater tellings name the mountain Droṇagiri; a village of that name in Uttarakhand says the missing peak is theirs
PlacesSeveral hills in India and Sri Lanka are said to be fallen fragments of the mountain

Tulasīdāsa expands the night flight into an adventure. Kālanemi, disguised as a hermit, offers water from a lake; a crocodile there, actually a cursed apsaras, attacks Hanumān, and when freed warns him. He kills the false sage and goes on.

The Bharata episode is especially tender: Bharata shoots down an unknown giant carrying a mountain over Ayodhyā, hears Rāma’s name from his lips, and is heartbroken. He offers to send Hanumān on with one of his arrows; Hanumān declines and flies on.

Droṇagiri village in Uttarakhand is known for a local tradition that Hanumān tore off part of their mountain, and some families there still do not worship him.

AHIRĀVAṆA

The underworld rescue
SourceLater regional Rāmāyaṇas, not Vālmīki
KidnapThe sorcerer Ahirāvaṇa carried Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa to Pātāla to sacrifice them
MakaradhvajaAt the gate Hanumān met a vānara guard: his own son, Makaradhvaja
Five lampsHe took five faces and blew out five lamps at once, killing Ahirāvaṇa
AfterMakaradhvaja was made king of Pātāla

The Ahirāvaṇa (or Mahirāvaṇa) story became hugely popular through Bengali, Odia, Telugu and Hindi Rāmāyaṇas and folk theatre. Its villain is a brother or ally of Rāvaṇa who rules the underworld and worships a fierce goddess.

Makaradhvaja’s birth story: a drop of Hanumān’s sweat fell into the sea as he flew back from burning Laṅkā, and a great fish swallowed it. The son, born half-fish, became Ahirāvaṇa’s gatekeeper.

The Pañcamukha form, discussed in Part 1, comes from this story.

VICTORY

Rāvaṇa falls
The duelRāma and Rāvaṇa fought for days; Mātali brought Indra’s chariot for Rāma
ĀdityahṛdayaThe sage Agastya taught Rāma the hymn to the Sun before the last fight
The endRāma killed Rāvaṇa with the brahmāstra
Vibhīṣaṇa crownedRāma had Vibhīṣaṇa crowned king of Laṅkā

The final duel stretches over many cantos. Agastya teaches Rāma the Ādityahṛdaya, a hymn to the Sun still widely recited, and Indra sends his chariot and charioteer Mātali.

Rāvaṇa’s heads grow back as fast as Rāma cuts them; finally he uses the brahmāstra given by Agastya, which pierces Rāvaṇa’s chest. Rāma then tells Vibhīṣaṇa to give his brother full funeral rites: enmity ends with death.

NEWS FOR SĪTĀ

The second message
SentRāma sent Hanumān with Vibhīṣaṇa’s leave to tell Sītā
JoySītā was speechless with joy
His offerHanumān asked leave to kill the rākṣasīs who had tormented her
Her answerSītā refused: they had only obeyed their master; the noble do not repay evil with evil

ZAPPY: She forgave them?

PROF. BOLT: Completely. Vālmīki makes her the most forgiving person in the story.

Sītā’s refusal of revenge is one of the finest moments in the epic. She tells Hanumān a parable: a bear, chased by a tiger, climbed a tree where a man was already sitting; the tiger asked the bear to push the man down, but the bear refused, saying that the noble keep their word even to those who wrong them.

Her line that there is no one who has never done wrong (na kaścin nāparādhyati) is often quoted as the ethical heart of the Rāmāyaṇa.

Hanumān accepts. He asks what message to take to Rāma, and she says only that she wishes to see him.

THE ORDEAL BY FIRE

A hard scene
Rāma’s wordsRāma told Sītā, before all, that he had fought for his honour and she was free to go where she wished
SītāShe answered him with dignity and asked Lakṣmaṇa to build a fire
AgniShe entered it; Agni himself rose and returned her, unharmed and pure
DebateMany readers and later poets find this scene painful; some tellings soften or remove it

The ordeal is included here because Hanumān witnesses it and because readers always ask. In Vālmīki, Rāma’s harsh words and Sītā’s entry into the fire are followed by the gods themselves arriving to declare Rāma is Viṣṇu and Sītā is Lakṣmī.

Later traditions softened it. In the Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa and the Rāmacaritamānas, the real Sītā had been hidden in fire before the abduction, and the ordeal simply brings her back. Others, like some women’s folk Rāmāyaṇas, openly side with Sītā.

We present it without a verdict: it remains one of the most argued passages in Indian literature.

BACK TO AYODHYĀ

Hanumān sent ahead
PuṣpakaThey flew home in Rāvaṇa’s chariot Puṣpaka; Rāma showed Sītā the places of their journey
AheadRāma sent Hanumān to Bharata at Nandigrāma to watch his face when he heard the news
WhyIf Bharata now wanted to keep the throne, Rāma would let him
BharataHe fainted with joy; Hanumān told him the whole story
NandigrāmaBharata had ruled fourteen years from a village, with Rāma’s sandals on the throne

The Puṣpaka flight is a lovely passage: Rāma points out to Sītā the battlefield, the bridge, Kiṣkindhā, Paṃpā, the hermitages and Citrakūṭa, a review of the whole story from the air.

Rāma’s instruction to Hanumān shows his care for Bharata: he wants to see whether fourteen years of power had changed his brother’s heart, and to give him a way out if it had.

Bharata’s joy settles it. Vālmīki’s scene of the brothers meeting, with Bharata putting the sandals back on Rāma’s feet, is one of the most loved in the epic.

THE PEARL NECKLACE

Sītā’s gift
The coronationRāma was crowned with Sītā in Ayodhyā
GiftsHe gave gifts to all the vānara and rākṣasa allies
The necklaceSītā took off her pearl necklace and looked at Rāma; he said: give it to the one you are most pleased with
HanumānShe gave it to Hanumān

ZAPPY: Not to Sugrīva or Vibhīṣaṇa?

PROF. BOLT: To the one who found her. Rāma agreed.

The pearl necklace is the final, personal honour. Rāma had given it to Sītā; she, wanting to honour Hanumān, looks at Rāma for permission. He tells her to give it to whoever she is most pleased with, and she gives it to Hanumān, who wears it with pride.

Vālmīki lists the qualities for which she chose him: energy, skill, fame, cleverness, valour, humility, wisdom and courage.

RĀMA IN HIS HEART

A loved later story
The pearlsLater tellings: Hanumān bit into each pearl, looking for Rāma inside
MockedCourtiers laughed at the monkey breaking a priceless necklace
The chestHe tore open his chest, and Rāma and Sītā were seen in his heart
ImageThe form with the open chest is one of the most popular Hanumān images
SourceFolk and later devotional tradition, not Vālmīki

The torn-chest story probably developed in medieval devotional tradition and was spread widely by calendar art, film and television. It is now one of the best-known images in Hinduism.

Its meaning is simple: everything Hanumān values is inside him already. A pearl without Rāma is worthless to him.

THE FAREWELL

Rāma leaves, Hanumān stays
The endAfter a long reign Rāma walked into the Sarayū and returned to Viṣṇu; Ayodhyā followed
Hanumān’s wishHe asked to stay on earth as long as Rāma’s story was told
GrantedRāma blessed him: his life would last as long as the Rāma story lives
OthersVibhīṣaṇa and Jāmbavān were also asked to remain

ZAPPY: So he's still here?

PROF. BOLT: That's the belief. Wherever Rāma's story is told.

The Uttara Kāṇḍa ends with Rāma’s departure, the mahāprasthāna: the citizens of Ayodhyā follow him into the Sarayū and attain heaven. Rāma asks a few beings to remain: Vibhīṣaṇa to rule Laṅkā, Jāmbavān, and Hanumān.

Hanumān’s request is recorded in words often quoted in Hindi tradition: “As long as your story is told on earth, let my life last.” This is the root of the belief that he is present wherever the Rāmāyaṇa is recited.

WHAT THE WAR TEACHES

Hanumān’s lessons
Read peopleHe trusted Vibhīṣaṇa when others did not
Fetch the whole mountainWhen unsure, he did more than enough
Mercy is Sītā’sHe offered revenge; she taught forgiveness
Reward is loveHe wanted nothing but to stay near Rāma

Across the war books Hanumān grows from messenger to pillar. The army’s survival rests on him twice. Yet his final reward, by his own choice, is not a kingdom but nearness to Rāma.
