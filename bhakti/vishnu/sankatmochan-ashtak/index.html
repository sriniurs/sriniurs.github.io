WHERE WE ARE

The Saṅkaṭmocan Hanumānāṣṭak is eight long verses and a closing dohā in Awadhī, chanted with the Cālīsā in Hanumān temples, especially on Tuesdays. It is usually attributed to Tulasīdāsa, though it has no signature line.

Its plan is simple and powerful: seven verses each tell one rescue from the Rāma story, the eighth asks for the singer’s own rescue, and every verse ends with the same question: who in the world does not know that your name is Saṅkaṭmocan, remover of trouble?

VERSE 1

THE CHILD WHO ATE THE SUN
bāla samaya rabi bhakṣi liyo taba tīnahuṃ loka bhayo aṃdhiyārotāhi soṃ trāsa bhayo jaga ko yaha saṅkaṭa kāhu soṃ jāta na ṭārodevana āni karī binatī taba chāṃḍi diyo rabi kaṣṭa nivāroko nahiṃ jānata hai jaga meṃ kapi saṅkaṭamocana nāma tihāro ‖1‖
As a child you swallowed the sun, and the three worlds went dark. The whole world was terrified; no one could remove this trouble. Then the gods came and pleaded, and you let the sun go and ended their distress. Who in the world does not know, O Monkey, that your name is Saṅkaṭmocan, the remover of trouble?

bāla samaya rabi bhakṣi liyoas a child you swallowed the sun

VERSE 1 · DEEP DIVE

THE CHILD WHO ATE THE SUN

🔎 In context

The Saṅkaṭmocan Hanumānāṣṭak, “eight verses to Hanumān the remover of trouble”, is chanted with the Cālīsā in temples across North India, especially on Tuesdays. It is commonly attributed to Tulasīdāsa, though it carries no signature and the attribution is traditional. Each verse tells one rescue, and each ends with the same refrain.

📜 Looking deeper

The metre is the mattagayanda savaiyā, a long line of seven bhagaṇa feet (long-short-short) and two long syllables, which gives the hymn its rolling, swinging rhythm. The first rescue is a reversal: Hanumān himself caused the trouble by swallowing the sun, and he is the one who ends it. The folk version, in which the child actually holds the sun in his mouth, goes beyond Vālmīki, where he only leaps for it. The refrain asks a rhetorical question: who does not know? It makes the listener part of the chorus of witnesses.

🌱 For today

Even the trouble we cause can be undone, if we are willing to let go of what we grabbed.

↑ VERSE 1 · He let the sun go.

VERSE 2

BRINGING RĀMA TO SUGRĪVA
bāli kī trāsa kapīsa basai giri jāta mahāprabhu paṃtha nihārocauṃki mahāmuni sāpa diyo taba cāhiya kauna bicāra bicārokai dvija rūpa livāya mahāprabhu so tuma dāsa ke soka nivāroko nahiṃ jānata hai jaga meṃ kapi saṅkaṭamocana nāma tihāro ‖2‖
Afraid of Vālī, the monkey king lived on the mountain, watching the road for the Lord. The great sage, startled, had cursed Vālī; what else was there to think? Taking a brahmin’s form you brought the Lord, and so ended your servant’s sorrow. Who does not know…
WordMeans
bāli kī trāsaout of fear of Vālī
kapīsa basai girithe monkey king lived on the hill
dvija rūpa livāya mahāprabhuin brahmin form you brought the Lord
dāsa ke soka nivāroremoved your servant’s grief
He brought the Lord to the frightened king.

VERSE 2 · DEEP DIVE

BRINGING RĀMA TO SUGRĪVA

🔎 In context

The second rescue is Sugrīva’s, from the Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa. Sugrīva hid on Ṛṣyamūka because the sage Mataṅga had cursed Vālī never to set foot there. Hanumān went down disguised as a brahmin, here dvija, “twice-born”, met Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa and brought them up to Sugrīva.

📜 Looking deeper

Vālmīki calls the disguise a bhikṣu, a mendicant; Tulasīdāsa’s Rāmacaritamānas calls it a brahmin, and the Aṣṭak follows him. The line “jāta mahāprabhu paṃtha nihāro” has a double sense: Sugrīva was watching the road anxiously for enemies, but he was in fact watching for the Lord without knowing it. The rescue is not violent: Hanumān simply connects the one in trouble with the one who can help. Note that Sugrīva is called Hanumān’s dāsa, his dependant, here.

🌱 For today

Sometimes the best help is an introduction to the right person.

↑ VERSE 2 · He brought the Lord to the frightened king.

VERSE 3

NEWS OF SĪTĀ
aṃgada ke saṃga lena gaye siya khoja kapīsa yaha baina ucārojīvata nā bacihau hama so ju binā sudhi lāye ihāṃ pagu dhāroheri thake taṭa siṃdhu sabai taba lāya siyā-sudhi prāna ubāroko nahiṃ jānata hai jaga meṃ kapi saṅkaṭamocana nāma tihāro ‖3‖
You went with Aṅgada to find Sītā, and the monkey king had said: “If you come back here without news of her, you will not escape me alive.” All were searching, worn out on the seashore; then you brought news of Sītā and saved their lives. Who does not know…

VERSE 3 · DEEP DIVE

NEWS OF SĪTĀ

🔎 In context

The third rescue is the southern search party’s, from the end of the Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa and the Sundara Kāṇḍa. Sugrīva had given a deadline of one month, on pain of death. The party lost its way, overran the deadline and sat on the shore resolved to fast to death.

📜 Looking deeper

This verse reminds us that Hanumān’s leap saved not only Sītā but his own companions. Aṅgada, Jāmbavān and the rest would have died on the shore, either by their vow or by Sugrīva’s order. Sudhi means news, tidings, and also awareness or memory; Hanumān brings “Sītā’s sudhi”, and in doing so he too remembers his own strength. Prāna ubāro, “lifted up their breath”, is a lovely phrase for one who is son of the Wind, the breath of life.

🌱 For today

When the whole team has given up, one person who keeps going can save everyone.

↑ VERSE 3 · His news saved everyone’s life.

VERSE 4

THE RING FOR SĪTĀ
rāvana trāsa daī siya ko saba rākṣasi soṃ kahi soka nivārotāhi samaya hanumāna mahāprabhu jāya mahā rajanīcara mārocāhata sīya asoka soṃ āgi su dai prabhu mudrikā soka nivāroko nahiṃ jānata hai jaga meṃ kapi saṅkaṭamocana nāma tihāro ‖4‖
Rāvaṇa terrified Sītā and told the rākṣasīs to torment her. At that moment you, great lord Hanumān, went and slew the great night-wanderers. When Sītā wanted fire from the Aśoka tree, you gave her the Lord’s ring and ended her grief. Who does not know…

VERSE 4 · DEEP DIVE

THE RING FOR SĪTĀ

🔎 In context

The fourth rescue is Sītā’s, in the Aśoka grove. Tulasīdāsa’s version is especially moving: Sītā, in despair after Rāvaṇa’s threats, asks the Aśoka tree for a spark to build her own pyre. At that moment Hanumān drops Rāma’s ring from the branches, and it falls glittering at her feet like the fire she asked for.

📜 Looking deeper

The verse puts two rescues together in a strange order: the killing of the rākṣasas, and the ring. In the story, the ring came first, then the destruction of the grove. Tulasīdāsa’s point is not sequence but what Hanumān does for Sītā: he defends her with force, and comforts her with a token. Aśoka itself means “without sorrow”; Sītā’s grief under the sorrowless tree is an irony the poets loved. Rajanīcara, “night-walker”, is an old name for rākṣasas.

🌱 For today

Comfort sometimes comes as a small thing at exactly the right moment.

↑ VERSE 4 · A ring instead of a pyre.

VERSE 5

LAKṢMAṆA REVIVED
bāna lagyo ura lachimana ke taba prāna taje suta rāvana mārolai gṛha baidya suṣena sameta tabai giri drona su bīra upāroāni sajīvana hātha daī taba lachimana ke tuma prāna ubāroko nahiṃ jānata hai jaga meṃ kapi saṅkaṭamocana nāma tihāro ‖5‖
When the arrow struck Lakṣmaṇa in the chest, fired by Rāvaṇa’s son, and his life was leaving, you brought the physician Suṣeṇa with his whole house, and then, hero, uprooted Mount Droṇa. Bringing the sañjīvanī, you put it in their hands and saved Lakṣmaṇa’s life. Who does not know…

VERSE 5 · DEEP DIVE

LAKṢMAṆA REVIVED

🔎 In context

The fifth rescue is the most famous, from the war. In Tulasīdāsa’s version, Lakṣmaṇa is struck by Meghanāda (Indrajit), Rāvaṇa’s son, and the physician Suṣeṇa lives in Laṅkā. Hanumān simply picks up Suṣeṇa’s whole house and brings it to Rāma’s camp, then flies to the Himalaya for the herb.

📜 Looking deeper

Both details, the house and the name Droṇa, come from later tellings. In Vālmīki, Suṣeṇa is a vānara physician already in Rāma’s army, and the mountain is unnamed or called Mahodaya. The image of Hanumān carrying a doctor’s house is a comic touch in a tense scene: he does not argue or persuade, he just brings everything. That is the theme of the herb story: when in doubt, bring the whole mountain.

🌱 For today

In an emergency, do not argue about the details. Get the doctor.

↑ VERSE 5 · He brought the doctor’s whole house.

VERSE 6

THE SERPENT NOOSE
rāvana juddha ajāna kiyo taba nāga ki phāṃsa sabai sira ḍārośrīraghunātha sameta sabai dala moha bhayo yaha saṃkaṭa bhāroāni khageśa tabai hanumāna ju baṃdhana kāṭi sutrāsa nivāroko nahiṃ jānata hai jaga meṃ kapi saṅkaṭamocana nāma tihāro ‖6‖
When Rāvaṇa fought a hidden battle and threw the serpent-noose over everyone, Rāma himself and his whole army fell into delusion: a heavy trouble. Then you, Hanumān, brought Garuḍa, king of birds, who cut the bonds and ended the terror. Who does not know…

VERSE 6 · DEEP DIVE

THE SERPENT NOOSE

🔎 In context

The sixth rescue is from the nāgapāśa episode. In Vālmīki it is Indrajit, invisible in the sky, who binds Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa with serpent-arrows, and Garuḍa comes of his own accord. In Tulasīdāsa and popular tellings, Hanumān (or Nārada) fetches Garuḍa, and the serpents flee at the sight of their eternal enemy.

📜 Looking deeper

The episode raises a question devotees love: how could Rāma, who is God, be bound? Tulasīdāsa answers in the Rāmacaritamānas that Rāma was playing a human role; and Garuḍa, who doubted Rāma’s divinity after seeing him bound, was later taught by the crow-sage Kākabhuśuṇḍi. Ajāna means unknowing or deceptive: a war fought by stealth. Khageśa, “lord of birds”, is Garuḍa, Viṣṇu’s mount.

🌱 For today

Some bonds need the right helper. Know who to call.

↑ VERSE 6 · He fetched Garuḍa to cut the snakes.

VERSE 7 · DEEP DIVE

INTO THE UNDERWORLD

🔎 In context

The seventh rescue is the Ahirāvaṇa story, from later regional Rāmāyaṇas, not Vālmīki or the Rāmacaritamānas. Its presence here shows how popular the story had become in North Indian devotion.

📜 Looking deeper

In the full story, Ahirāvaṇa, a sorcerer-king of Pātāla allied to Rāvaṇa, abducts Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa at night and plans to offer them to his goddess. Hanumān follows, meets his son Makaradhvaja at the gate, takes five faces to blow out five lamps holding Ahirāvaṇa’s life, and kills him. The verse lists the sacrifice plan carefully, “pūji bhalī bidhi soṃ bali”, a properly performed offering, to show how close the danger came.

🌱 For today

Even the strongest can be carried off in the dark. Friends who follow you there are rare.

↑ VERSE 7 · He went to the underworld for Rāma.

VERSE 8

WHAT TROUBLE OF MINE?
kāja kiye bara devana ke tuma bīra mahāprabhu dekhi bicārokauna so saṃkaṭa mora garība ko jo tumase nahiṃ jāta hai ṭārobegi haro hanumāna mahāprabhu jo kachu saṃkaṭa hoya hamāroko nahiṃ jānata hai jaga meṃ kapi saṅkaṭamocana nāma tihāro ‖8‖
You did great work for the gods; consider it, great hero and lord. What trouble of mine, a poor man, is there that you cannot remove? Quickly take away, great lord Hanumān, whatever trouble I have. Who does not know…
WordMeans

VERSE 8 · DEEP DIVE

WHAT TROUBLE OF MINE?

🔎 In context

After seven rescues from the story, the eighth verse turns to the singer. This is the structure of many Indian hymns: recall what the god has done for others, then ask for yourself.

📜 Looking deeper

The logic is the argument from greater to lesser, a classical form called kaimutika nyāya: if he did that, how much more easily this. The devotee calls himself garība, poor or humble, not in money only but in power. Begi, quickly, adds urgency; the hymn is meant for moments of real trouble. The refrain now answers the singer’s own question: everyone knows his name is Saṅkaṭmocan, so he will help me too.

🌱 For today

Remember what has been overcome before. Your trouble is probably smaller.

↑ VERSE 8 · If he did all that, my trouble is small.

CLOSING DOHĀ

THE RED ONE

His body is red and glows red, and he bears a red tail; with a body like the thunderbolt he crushes demons. Victory, victory, victory to the hero among monkeys!

CLOSING DOHĀ · DEEP DIVE

THE RED ONE

🔎 In context

The closing dohā is a short dhyāna, a picture to hold in mind. Lāla, red, is repeated three times: it is the colour of sindūr, of Tuesday’s planet Maṅgala, and of the rising sun he once grabbed.

📜 Looking deeper

The repetition of red has the effect of a mantra, and the dohā is often chanted on its own. Bajra deha recalls the Cālīsā’s “bajaraṅgī”; dānava dalana, crusher of dānavas, the demons. Kapi sūra, the hero (śūra) among monkeys, is a friendly, almost affectionate title to end on. In many homes the dohā is followed by the Cālīsā’s closing shouts and an ārati.

🌱 For today

End with a clear picture of what you trust. It stays with you.

↑ CLOSING DOHĀ · The red hero: victory!

THE HYMN ON ONE PAGE

The Aṣṭak in five lines
The sun (1). He swallowed it, the worlds went dark, he let it go.
Sugrīva (2), the search party (3), Sītā (4). An introduction, a message, a ring.
Lakṣmaṇa (5), the noose (6), Ahirāvaṇa (7). The herb, Garuḍa, the underworld.
Me (8). If he did all that, what trouble of mine can he not remove?
The red one (dohā). Victory to the hero among monkeys.
