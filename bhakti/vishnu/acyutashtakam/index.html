ACYUTĀṢṬAKAM

ĀDI ŚAṄKARĀCĀRYA · A GARLAND OF THE LORD’S NAMES
“Acyutaṃ keśavaṃ” · explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: First the whole hymn for chanting in one go. Then the story of how the hymn came to be and its background, and two pages for each verse: the chant, a picture, what it says and the key words; then a deeper look, background facts, the inner teaching and the one thing to remember.

FOREWORD

"Acyutaṃ keśavaṃ rāma-nārāyaṇaṃ…" It opens like a bell ringing: name after name of the Lord, in a lilting, swinging rhythm that children learn before they know what it means.

The Acyutāṣṭakam is traditionally attributed to Ādi Śaṅkarācārya. In eight verses it worships the Lord by His names, then turns to Rāma in the forest and to little Kṛṣṇa among the cows, and ends with a meditation on His form, from rain-cloud body to jingling waist-bells.

It is a hymn for singing aloud: nāma-saṅkīrtana, the joy of the holy names.

srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Is this the bhajan 'Acyutam Keśavam Kṛṣṇa Dāmodaram'?

PROF. BOLT: That popular Hindi bhajan borrows this hymn's opening, then goes its own way. This is the original Sanskrit hymn.

THE STORY · A TEACHER WHO SANG

Śaṅkara, the philosopher of oneness, singing the names
The story in 5 frames
The philosopher. Ādi Śaṅkarācārya, from Kālaḍi in Kerala, taught Advaita Vedānta: the Self and Brahman are one.
The poet. Tradition credits him with many devotional hymns too: Bhaja Govindam, Śiva Pañcākṣara, Kanakadhārā and others.
Knowledge and love. For Śaṅkara, devotion purifies the mind and prepares it for knowledge; singing the names is open to everyone.
A garland of names. The Acyutāṣṭakam is pure nāma-saṅkīrtana: the Lord's names, strung together in a singing metre.
A note on authorship. Scholars debate which hymns Śaṅkara himself wrote; this one is traditionally attributed to him.

ZAPPY: A philosopher of oneness singing to Kṛṣṇa?

PROF. BOLT: Why not? The Lord we sing to is our own deepest Self, says verse 3. Love and knowledge meet.

ABOUT THE ACYUTĀṢṬAKAM

Eight verses and a blessing

🔔 The rhythm

Sragviṇī, "garland-wearer": four DUM-da-DUM beats a line. ACyu-TAM KEśa-VAM.

📛 The name

"Acyuta" means unfailing: the One who never falls, and never lets His devotee fall.

VerseWhat it does
1I worship: a garland of His names
2I hold in mind: Devakī's joy, Nanda's son
3Salutations to You: conch, discus, flute
4Calling out: "O Kṛṣṇa! O Govinda!"
5May Rāma of the forest protect me
6May little Gopāla always protect me
7–8Meditation on His form, head to foot
9The fruit: Hari is won by love

Not to be confused with another hymn of the same name, "acyutācyuta hare paramātman…", also attributed to Śaṅkara.

VERSE 1 · GOING DEEPER

A GARLAND OF NAMES

Twelve names in one verse. The hymn opens with a cascade of the Lord's names, then one verb at the end: bhaje, "I worship". This is nāma-saṅkīrtana, singing the names.

Acyuta. "The One who never slips": never falls from His nature, and never lets His devotees fall. The hymn is named after this first word.

Dāmodara. "Rope (dāma) round the belly (udara)": little Kṛṣṇa, tied to a mortar by Yaśodā for stealing butter. God, bound by a mother's love.

💡 Nāma-saṅkīrtana

Singing the names of God: a simple, open path of devotion.

💡 Acyuta

"Unfailing": a name in the Viṣṇu Sahasranāma.

Each name of God is a doorway. Say them slowly, and walk through.

ZAPPY: Is it just a list of names?

PROF. BOLT: A garland of names! Each one is a story: Dāmodara is baby Kṛṣṇa tied with a rope; Jānakī-nāyaka is Sītā's Rāma.

"Acyutaṃ keśavaṃ": a garland of the Lord's names.

VERSE 2 · GOING DEEPER

HELD IN THE MIND

Two mothers. Kṛṣṇa is Devakī-nandana, born to Devakī in Kaṃsa's prison, and Nanda-ja, raised as Nanda's son in Gokula. Both families belong to Him.

Three women who loved Him. Satyabhāmā, His queen in Dvārakā; Rādhā, His beloved in Vraja; Indirā, Lakṣmī Herself, who lives in Him.

Sandadhe. Verse 1 said "I worship"; verse 2 says "I hold Him in my mind": worship moving inward.

💡 Rādhā

Kṛṣṇa's beloved in Vraja, the supreme devotee in many traditions.

💡 Satyabhāmā

One of Kṛṣṇa's principal queens in Dvārakā.

Worship outside, and then hold Him inside.

ZAPPY: Two mothers?

PROF. BOLT: Devakī gave birth to Him; Yaśodā and Nanda raised Him. The hymn honours both.

I hold in my heart Devakī's joy, Nanda's son.
Some texts read nandanaṃ for nandajaṃ.

VERSE 3 · GOING DEEPER

SALUTATIONS TO YOU

A change of grammar. Verses 1–2 used the accusative ("I worship X"); verse 3 uses the dative ("to X"), ending te namaḥ, "salutations to You".

Rāma and Kṛṣṇa together. Rukmiṇī (Kṛṣṇa's chief queen) and Jānakī (Rāma's Sītā) stand side by side; the gopīs' darling and Kaṃsa's destroyer are one Lord.

Ātman. Arcitāya ātmane: "to the worshipped Self". Śaṅkara's Advaita sees the Lord we worship as our own deepest Self.

💡 Rukmiṇī

Kṛṣṇa's chief queen at Dvārakā, honoured as Lakṣmī.

💡 Kaṃsa

The tyrant king of Mathurā, Kṛṣṇa's uncle, slain by Him.

The Lord we bow to outside is the Self within.

ZAPPY: Why "to You" now?

PROF. BOLT: First "I worship", then "I hold in mind", now "salutations to You": the devotee speaks to Him directly.

"Te namaḥ": salutations to the Self of all.
Some texts read rukmiṇi-rāgiṇe.

VERSE 4 · GOING DEEPER

O KṚṢṆA, O GOVINDA!

Only calling. This verse has no verb at all: just names, in the vocative, "O…!". It is pure calling out, like a cry for help.

Draupadī's cry. The verse ends with Draupadī-rakṣaka. When Draupadī was being disrobed in the Kaurava court, tradition says she called "Govinda!", and her sari became endless. The verse itself sounds like that cry.

Adhokṣaja. "Beyond the senses": the Lord can't be grasped by the eyes or mind, yet answers when called.

💡 Draupadī

The Pāṇḍavas' queen, saved by Kṛṣṇa in the dice-game court.

💡 Dvārakā

Kṛṣṇa's city on the western sea, His capital as king.

When you have nothing else, call His name. It is enough.

ZAPPY: No verb in this verse?

PROF. BOLT: None! Just calling: "O Kṛṣṇa! O Govinda!", like Draupadī calling for help.

"Kṛṣṇa govinda he": the cry that saved Draupadī.

VERSE 5 · GOING DEEPER

MAY RĀMA PROTECT ME

The Rāmāyaṇa in four lines. The demons who troubled the sages, Sītā at his side, the forest exile, Lakṣmaṇa, the monkey army of Hanumān and Sugrīva, and the sage Agastya: the whole forest story.

He made the forest holy. The Daṇḍaka forest was wild and fearsome; Rāma's footsteps made it sacred. (Compare the Prapatti's "feet in the wild forests".)

Agastya. The great sage who welcomed Rāma in the forest and gave him divine weapons; later, on the battlefield, he teaches Rāma the Āditya Hṛdayam.

💡 Daṇḍakāraṇya

The vast forest of central India where Rāma spent much of his exile.

💡 Agastya

The sage who honoured Rāma and gave him the Āditya Hṛdayam.

Hardship walked with grace becomes holy ground.

ZAPPY: Where did Kṛṣṇa go?

PROF. BOLT: This verse is all Rāma: the forest, Sītā, Lakṣmaṇa, the monkeys. The next is all Kṛṣṇa!

Rāma of the forest: may he protect me.

VERSE 6 · GOING DEEPER

MAY LITTLE GOPĀLA PROTECT ME

The Bhāgavatam in four lines. The demon-donkey Dhenuka, the demon-bull Ariṣṭa, the horse-demon Keśī (from which "Keśava" is sometimes explained), the demoness Pūtanā, the tyrant Kaṃsa: all defeated by a child with a flute.

Sūrajā. The Yamunā is the daughter of Sūrya, the Sun. Kṛṣṇa played (khelana) in her waters.

Rāma then Kṛṣṇa. Verse 5 asked Rāma for protection; verse 6 asks little Gopāla, and adds sarvadā, "always".

💡 Pūtanā

The demoness who tried to kill baby Kṛṣṇa with poisoned milk.

💡 Bāla Gopāla

Kṛṣṇa as a little cowherd boy, much loved in home worship.

Evil is defeated not only by force, but by innocence and joy.

ZAPPY: A child beat all these demons?

PROF. BOLT: In the Bhāgavatam, yes! Donkey, bull, horse and Pūtanā: all defeated by a flute-playing boy.

The child with the flute: may He always protect me.
Some texts read dhenukāriṣṭako'niṣṭakṛd dveṣiṇāṃ.

VERSE 7 · GOING DEEPER

LIKE A RAIN CLOUD WITH LIGHTNING

A picture to meditate on. Verses 7–8 are a dhyāna, a description to hold in the mind's eye, head to foot.

Cloud and lightning. Kṛṣṇa's dark body is the monsoon cloud; His yellow silk is the lightning. To Indian eyes, the first monsoon cloud is pure joy and relief.

Vanamālā. The "forest garland" of wild flowers and leaves: the cowherd's ornament, not a king's.

💡 Dhyāna

A meditation verse describing the deity's form.

💡 Vanamālā

Kṛṣṇa's garland of forest flowers, reaching to His knees.

Hold one beautiful image of the Lord in your mind. Let it calm you like rain.

ZAPPY: A rain cloud?

PROF. BOLT: Dark and full of rain, with a flash of lightning: that's Kṛṣṇa in yellow silk. Perfect for a hot land!

Dark as a rain cloud, flashing like lightning.

VERSE 8 · GOING DEEPER

CURLS, CROWN AND JINGLING BELLS

Head to waist. Verse 7 gave the whole form; verse 8 looks closely: curls, crown, earrings, necklace, armlets, bracelets, bells. Like a painter adding detail.

The sound. Kiṅkiṇī-mañjulam: you can almost hear the little bells on His waist-chain as He runs about Gokula.

Śyāmala. "Dark", the colour of Kṛṣṇa (whose name itself means "dark"). The meditation ends on that single word.

💡 Kṛṣṇa

The name itself means "dark", "black-blue".

💡 Kiṅkiṇī

A waist-chain with small bells, worn by children and by the child Kṛṣṇa.

Look closely at what you love. Devotion lives in the details.

ZAPPY: Every jewel described!

PROF. BOLT: It's a meditation: picture Him detail by detail, and you can even hear His little bells!

Curls, crown, bells: I worship the dark One.

VERSE 9 · GOING DEEPER

THE FRUIT OF RECITING

The phalaśruti. The ninth verse states the fruit of reciting. The fruit is remarkable: not wealth or heaven, but Hari Himself, won over by love.

God won by love. Vaśyo harir jāyate: the Lord who sustains the universe becomes "subject" to the devotee. Bhakti texts often say this: the Lord bound Himself to Yaśodā's rope, to the gopīs, to His devotees.

The metre. Vṛttataḥ sundaram, "beautiful in its metre": the verses run in sragviṇī, a lilting DUM-da-DUM ×4 rhythm that is easy to sing.

💡 Sragviṇī

"Wearing a garland": a 12-syllable metre of four DUM-da-DUM beats.

💡 Bhakta-vaśya

"Under the devotee's sway": a name of the Lord.

The Lord who holds the universe lets love hold Him.

ZAPPY: God comes under our control?

PROF. BOLT: Not by force; by love. Like Dāmodara tied by Yaśodā's rope in verse 1!

Sung with love each day, it wins Hari's heart.

Verses 1–3. I worship, I hold in mind, I bow: Acyuta, Keśava, Rāma, Nārāyaṇa, Kṛṣṇa, the Self of all.
Verse 4. Pure calling: "Kṛṣṇa! Govinda! Protector of Draupadī!"
Verses 5–6. Rāma of the forest and little Gopāla among the cows: may they protect me.
Verses 7–8. Meditation: rain-cloud body, lightning silk, curls, crown and tinkling bells.
Verse 9. Sung with love each day, the hymn wins Hari's heart.

ZAPPY: What's the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: The names of God are enough. Sing them with love, and the Unfailing One comes close.

"Acyutaṃ keśavaṃ": sing the names, and He comes. 🙏

Authorship of many hymns attributed to Śaṅkara is debated by scholars. The metre (sragviṇī) is from standard prosody. Meanings are our own plain-English renderings.
