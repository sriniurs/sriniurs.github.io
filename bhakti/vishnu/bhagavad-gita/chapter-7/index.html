WHERE WE ARE

In Chapter 6 Kṛṣṇa taught meditation and ended: the best yogi is the one who worships me with faith.

Chapter 7 begins the middle part of the Gītā, about the Lord himself. Kṛṣṇa describes his two natures, matter and life; he is the thread through all things, the taste in water and the light in the sun. Few know him; māyā veils him; but those who take refuge cross over.

ŚLOKA 7.1 · DEEP DIVE

HOW YOU WILL KNOW ME FULLY

🔎 In context

The second six chapters begin. After the yoga of action and meditation, Kṛṣṇa now speaks of himself. Chapter 7 is named Jñāna-Vijñāna Yoga, the yoga of knowledge and realisation. It opens the middle six chapters, where Kṛṣṇa speaks of his own nature. Arjuna asked no question to prompt it: Kṛṣṇa continues from 6.47, where the devoted yogi was called the best.

📜 Looking deeper

Many commentators divide the Gītā into three groups of six chapters: action (1–6), devotion and the nature of God (7–12), knowledge (13–18). Here the focus shifts to knowing the Lord samagram, fully: in his formless and personal aspects alike. Commentators note āsakta, attached: the Gītā has warned against attachment to objects, but attachment to the Lord is praised. Śaṅkara reads samagram, “in full”, as knowing the Lord with all his powers and glories. Rāmānuja reads it as knowing his nature, qualities and manifestations. The chapter unfolds what is to be known: his lower and higher natures, his presence in all things, and who comes to him. Commentators note mad-āśrayaḥ, “taking refuge in me”: the knowledge to come is for the one who depends on the Lord. Madhusūdana says the middle six chapters are the heart of the Gītā, joining the action of the first six with the knowledge of the last. Many devotees read chapters 7–12 most often.

🌱 For today

Knowing someone fully takes time and love. So does knowing the Divine. Attachment to what is highest frees rather than binds. Let your deepest attachment be to what is truly worthy of it.

ŚLOKA 7.2 · DEEP DIVE

KNOWLEDGE AND REALISATION

🔎 In context

Kṛṣṇa promises both jñāna, knowledge, and vijñāna, realised knowledge.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators distinguish them: jñāna is understanding; vijñāna is direct experience, or knowledge of the details of the Lord’s manifestation. The chapter’s name, Jñāna Vijñāna Yoga, comes from this pair. The Chāndogya and Muṇḍaka Upaniṣads ask about the knowledge by which everything else becomes known. Muṇḍaka 1.1.3 asks: kasmin nu bhagavo vijñāte sarvam idaṃ vijñātaṃ bhavati?, “Sir, by knowing what does all this become known?”. Commentators see 7.2 as the Gītā’s answer. Śaṅkara defines jñāna as knowledge of the Self from scripture and vijñāna as its direct experience. Rāmānuja takes jñāna as knowledge of the Lord’s nature and vijñāna as knowledge of his distinguishing qualities. The promise is bold: nothing more will remain to be known. Commentators note aśeṣataḥ, “completely”: Kṛṣṇa promises a full teaching. Madhusūdana says jñāna is knowing the Lord as the Self, and vijñāna is seeing him in all things. Some teachers compare the two to knowing the theory of music and hearing it played.

🌱 For today

Knowing about something and knowing it from inside are different. Seek both. Some knowledge opens all other doors. Look for the root, not just the branches.

ŚLOKA 7.3

ONE IN THOUSANDS

“Among thousands of people, hardly one strives for perfection; and of those who strive and succeed, hardly one knows me in truth.”
WordMeans
manuṣyāṇām sahasreṣuamong thousands of people
kaścit yatati siddhayesomeone strives for perfection

kaścit mām vetti tattvataḥsomeone knows me in truth

ŚLOKA 7.3 · DEEP DIVE

ONE IN THOUSANDS

🔎 In context

True knowledge of God is rare.

📜 Looking deeper

Not discouragement but a sense of the treasure’s value. Commentators note it is rare not because it is hidden, but because few seek it earnestly. Commentators find this verse both humbling and inspiring. Śaṅkara says it shows how rare and precious this knowledge is, to encourage the seeker. Rāmānuja says even among the successful yogis who know the self, few know the Lord fully. The verse is often quoted to explain why spiritual teachers are rare. Some modern readers find it discouraging; teachers answer that rarity does not mean impossibility. 7.19 says the wise one comes after many births, and 6.45 promises that effort accumulates. Commentators note siddhaye, “for perfection”: few even try. Madhusūdana says among those who try, few succeed, and among those, few know the Lord fully. The verse is meant to inspire effort, not discourage it.

🌱 For today

Rarity is no reason to stop. It means the gift is precious. Something rare is not out of reach; it is precious. Let that make you more careful, not less hopeful.

↑ 7.3 · True knowing is rare.

ŚLOKA 7.4

“Earth, water, fire, air, space, mind, intellect and ego: this is my nature, divided eightfold.”

kham manaḥ buddhiḥ eva caspace, mind, intellect

ŚLOKA 7.4 · DEEP DIVE

MY LOWER NATURE

🔎 In context

The material world, including the mind, is God’s lower nature.

📜 Looking deeper

Note that mind, intellect and ego are counted as “matter”, not Self. This echoes the Sāṅkhya system of 24 principles. Commentators say all of them are instruments, not the one who knows. The five elements, pañca-bhūta, plus mind, intellect and ego make eight. Commentators note that the Sāṅkhya system lists twenty-four principles of matter; this verse names their main headings. Śaṅkara reads each as standing for a group: earth for its subtle form, mind for its source, and so on. Rāmānuja says these eight are the Lord’s material nature, his body. The striking point is that mind and ego are counted as matter, not self. The thinking, planning, “I”-making part of us belongs to nature. The true self is something else. Commentators note bhinnā, “divided”: nature is split into eight parts. Madhusūdana says these eight are the causes of all material things. Modern science also sees mind and brain as linked to matter; the Gītā places the knower beyond both.

🌱 For today

Even our thoughts and ego belong to nature. The knower behind them is something else. Your thoughts and moods are real, but they are not the whole of you. There is a knower behind them.

↑ 7.4 · Matter and mind: my lower nature.

ŚLOKA 7.5 · DEEP DIVE

MY HIGHER NATURE

🔎 In context

The higher nature is life itself, the conscious principle.

📜 Looking deeper

For Rāmānuja, this is the realm of individual souls (jīvas), part of God’s body. For Śaṅkara, the knower appearing as many through the lower nature. All agree: consciousness is higher than matter. Parā prakṛti, higher nature, is consciousness, the life in beings. Commentators differ on how it relates to the Lord. Śaṅkara: it is the knower, the conscious principle that appears as many individual selves through ignorance. Rāmānuja: it is the totality of individual selves, real and many, forming part of the Lord’s body. Madhva: the selves are eternally distinct from the Lord and depend on him. All agree that consciousness is higher than matter, and that the world is “upheld” by it: without life, matter would have no meaning. Commentators note jagat dhāryate, “the world is held up”: life sustains the world. Madhusūdana says the higher nature is consciousness, which gives meaning to matter. Without consciousness, there would be no one to know the world.

🌱 For today

The world is held up by life. Every living being shares in God’s higher nature. Every living being carries the higher nature, life itself. That is reason enough for reverence.

↑ 7.5 · Life itself is my higher nature.

ŚLOKA 7.6 · DEEP DIVE

ORIGIN AND END OF ALL

🔎 In context

Matter and life together produce all beings, and the Lord is the source and end of all.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators see God as both the material and efficient cause of the world, like a spider spinning its web from itself (Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad 1.1.7). Commentators see the Lord as both the material cause (what the world is made of) and the efficient cause (who makes it). The Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad (1.1.7) gives the image of a spider spinning its web from itself and drawing it back. Śaṅkara says the Lord, through his māyā, is the cause of all. Rāmānuja says the Lord, with matter and selves as his body, transforms into the world while remaining himself. The words prabhava, origin, and pralaya, dissolution, return in 9.18 and 10.8. The world comes from one source and returns to it. Commentators note prabhava and pralaya, origin and dissolution: the Lord is both. Madhusūdana says the Lord is the cause of the world as gold is the cause of ornaments. Ornaments come and go; the gold remains.

🌱 For today

Everything comes from one source and returns to it. That makes everything related. If everything comes from one source, then everything is related. That changes how we treat the world.

↑ 7.6 · I am the origin and end of all.

ŚLOKA 7.7 · DEEP DIVE

PEARLS ON A THREAD

🔎 In context

One of the Gītā’s loveliest images: God as the hidden thread holding the pearls together.

📜 Looking deeper

The thread is unseen, yet without it the necklace falls apart. Commentators of all schools love this verse: God is the unity running through all diversity. The thread image appears in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad (3.7), where Uddālaka asks Yājñavalkya about the sūtra, the thread by which this world and the next are tied together, and the inner controller. Commentators of every school love the Gītā’s version. Śaṅkara: the Lord is the reality that runs through all appearances. Rāmānuja: the Lord is the inner support of all beings, as the thread supports the pearls. Madhva: the Lord holds all in being. The thread is hidden; the pearls are seen. Without the thread, they scatter. Commentators note mattaḥ parataraṃ nānyat, “nothing higher than me”: the Lord is the highest reality. Madhusūdana says the thread is unseen but holds everything. Some teachers suggest a meditation: see the thread of awareness running through all your experiences of the day.

🌱 For today

Look for the thread in your life: what holds everything together? Look for the thread in your own life: what holds your days, relationships and work together?

↑ 7.7 · All strung on me like pearls on a thread.

ŚLOKA 7.8

THE TASTE IN WATER
raso’hamapsu kaunteya prabhāsmi śaśisūryayoḥpraṇavaḥ sarvavedeṣu śabdaḥ khe pauruṣaṃ nṛṣu ‖8‖
“I am the taste in water, son of Kuntī; I am the light in the moon and the sun; I am Oṃ in all the Vedas, the sound in space, and manhood in men.”

ŚLOKA 7.8 · DEEP DIVE

THE TASTE IN WATER

🔎 In context

Kṛṣṇa begins to name himself in the essence of things.

📜 Looking deeper

The essence of each thing: taste in water, light in sun and moon, Oṃ in the Vedas. Commentators say this is a meditation: see the Divine in the best quality of everything. Verses 8–11 name the Lord in the essence of each thing. Commentators note the pattern from the Sāṅkhya elements: water’s quality is taste, fire’s is light, space’s is sound, earth’s is smell (7.9). Śaṅkara says the Lord is the essence that makes each thing what it is. Rāmānuja says the Lord is the inner controller of each quality. Praṇava, Oṃ, is called the essence of the Vedas; the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad is devoted to it. Pauruṣa, manliness, is read as courage, energy and capacity in people. This list anticipates the longer list of chapter 10. Commentators note that each item names the essence of a thing: water’s taste, the sun’s light, space’s sound. Madhusūdana says meditating on these essences leads to the Lord. Some teachers call this way of seeing “sacramental”: ordinary things become windows to the sacred.

🌱 For today

When you drink water, taste it, and remember this verse. Taste your water slowly once today, as a reminder. The extraordinary often hides in the ordinary.

ŚLOKA 7.9 · DEEP DIVE

FRAGRANCE AND FIRE

🔎 In context

More essences: the fragrance of earth, the brightness of fire.

📜 Looking deeper

The smell of rain on dry earth is the Divine, says this verse. Commentators note that each element is named with its subtle quality: earth with smell, fire with light. Commentators note that the Lord is the pleasant smell of earth, not the foul; the best, not the worst, of each thing. Śaṅkara says “pure” means natural, unmixed. Many readers think of the smell of rain on dry soil, which Indian poets call mṛd-gandha. Jīvana, life, is the Lord in all beings. Tapas, austerity, is the Lord in the disciplined. The list moves from elements to living beings to spiritual effort. The Divine is in the senses’ best experiences and in the soul’s highest efforts. Commentators note jīvanam, “life”: the Lord is the life in all beings. Madhusūdana says without life, a body is only matter. Many find in this verse reverence for all living things, plants and animals included.

🌱 For today

Notice the smell of earth after rain. That too is a doorway. Next time you smell rain on earth, pause. Let it be a moment of quiet wonder.

THEY THINK I AM MERELY HUMAN

🔎 In context

Many see only the human Kṛṣṇa, not the divine.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators: seeing Kṛṣṇa as a mere man is the error; his form is divine. Śaṅkara’s and Rāmānuja’s readings differ on the nature of that form, but both reject the “merely human” view. Commentators read this verse in two main ways. Śaṅkara: the unwise think the formless Lord has taken on a body like ours, through karma; they do not know his true nature as the Self. Rāmānuja and Madhva: the unwise think Kṛṣṇa is an ordinary man who has become prominent; they do not know that his form is divine and eternal. Both readings warn against reducing the Divine to the merely human. Many in the Mahābhārata saw Kṛṣṇa as a clever prince; few, like Bhīṣma and Vidura, recognised him. Commentators note avyayam anuttamam, “imperishable and supreme”: the Lord’s higher nature. Madhusūdana says the unwise see only the body of Kṛṣṇa. Many people in the epic saw Kṛṣṇa only as a cowherd or prince; only some saw more.

🌱 For today

People are often more than they seem. So is the world. People are often more than they seem. Look again at those you think you already know.

God knows all; few know God.

📜 Looking deeper

BORN INTO DELUSION

🔎 In context

Why we don’t see: likes and dislikes cloud us from birth.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators: the pairs of opposites are the basic programme of every creature. Freedom means seeing past them. Sarge, at birth or at creation. Commentators say every being is born with likes and dislikes, the basic programme of survival. These create the delusion of opposites: pleasant and painful, mine and not mine. Śaṅkara says this delusion hides the Self. Rāmānuja says beings are born with impressions from past lives that produce attraction and aversion. Patañjali names rāga and dveṣa as afflictions (Yoga Sūtra 2.7–8). The verse explains why spiritual life feels like swimming upstream: we start with a strong current of likes and dislikes. Commentators note sarge, “at birth”: beings are born into delusion. Madhusūdana says likes and dislikes are the first fruits of ignorance. Spiritual practice can be seen as slowly unlearning these automatic reactions.

🌱 For today

Notice your likes and dislikes. They are not the whole truth. Your likes and dislikes are real, but they are not the whole truth about the world. Hold them a little more loosely.
