WHERE WE ARE

In Chapter 13 Kṛṣṇa separated the field (body and mind) from its knower (the Self).

Chapter 14 looks closely at the stuff of the field: the three guṇas. Sattva (clarity), rajas (restless energy) and tamas (inertia) bind us in different ways. Kṛṣṇa shows how to recognise each, what each leads to, and what a person looks like who has gone beyond all three, through unwavering devotion.

ŚLOKA 14.1 · DEEP DIVE

THE SUPREME KNOWLEDGE

🔎 In context

Chapter 14 opens with Kṛṣṇa promising more of the highest knowledge. Chapter 14 is named Guṇa-Traya-Vibhāga Yoga, the yoga of the division of the three guṇas. It is the Gītā’s fullest account of the three qualities of nature, which shape every mind, body and action.

📜 Looking deeper

This chapter explains the three guṇas, the qualities of nature introduced earlier (3.27, 7.12–14, 13.21). Commentators note “again”: the teaching spirals, returning to key ideas at deeper levels. Understanding the guṇas is practical psychology as well as philosophy. The guṇas come from Sāṅkhya philosophy, but the Gītā uses them practically: as a map of the mind. Śaṅkara says the teaching is repeated to create dispassion toward the guṇas. Rāmānuja says it shows how the self is bound to matter and how it can be freed. The theory of guṇas also shaped Āyurveda. Jñāneśvar, like many commentators, opens each chapter with praise of the guru. Many modern teachers of yoga and psychology have taken up the guṇa framework because it is simple to use: three tendencies, always present, always shifting. It describes moods, foods, homes, cities and even times of day.

🌱 For today

Understanding how our moods and habits work is itself a kind of liberation. Understanding how your moods and habits work is already the beginning of freedom from them.

ŚLOKA 14.2

BEYOND CREATION AND DISSOLUTION
idaṃ jñānamupāśritya mama sādharmyamāgatāḥsarge’pi nopajāyante pralaye na vyathanti ca ‖2‖
“Those who take refuge in this knowledge and attain my nature are not born at the time of creation, nor are they disturbed at the time of dissolution.”
WordMeans
idam jñānam upāśrityataking refuge in this knowledge
mama sādharmyam āgatāḥhaving reached likeness with me
sarge api na upajāyanteare not born even at creation
pralaye na vyathanti canor disturbed at dissolution
Beyond creation and dissolution.

ŚLOKA 14.2 · DEEP DIVE

BEYOND CREATION AND DISSOLUTION

🔎 In context

This knowledge frees from the cosmic cycle.

📜 Looking deeper

Sādharmya, “sharing my nature”: Rāmānuja stresses this as becoming like God in purity, while remaining distinct; Śaṅkara as identity with Brahman. Either way, the cosmic cycles no longer touch the freed soul. Sādharmya, “having the same dharma” or nature as the Lord, is a key word in the Vedānta debates. Śaṅkara takes it as oneness. Rāmānuja takes it as likeness: the freed soul shares the Lord’s purity and bliss but remains a distinct self. Madhva insists on distinction even in freedom. The Brahma Sūtras (4.4.17–21) discuss what powers the freed soul has; all agree it is free of the cycle. The cosmic cycle of creation and dissolution was described in 8.17–19 and 9.7–8. Commentators note that the freed soul is not dragged along by these cycles. Madhusūdana says even the gods are born and dissolved, but the knower is not. The Brahma Sūtras end with the words anāvṛttiḥ śabdāt, “there is no return, as scripture declares” (4.4.22).

🌱 For today

True knowledge brings a peace that even the end of the world cannot shake. Lasting peace does not depend on what happens outside. It rests on what you know inside.

↑ 14.2 · Beyond creation and dissolution.

ŚLOKA 14.3

MY WOMB AND MY SEED
mama yonirmahadbrahma tasmin garbhaṃ dadhāmyahamsaṃbhavaḥ sarvabhūtānāṃ tato bhavati bhārata ‖3‖
“Great Brahman (mahat-prakṛti) is my womb; in it I place the seed. From that comes the birth of all beings, Bhārata.”
WordMeans
mama yoniḥ mahat brahmagreat Brahman is my womb
tasmin garbham dadhāmi ahamin it I place the seed
sambhavaḥ sarva-bhūtānāmthe birth of all beings
tataḥ bhavati bhāratacomes from that

ŚLOKA 14.3 · DEEP DIVE

MY WOMB AND MY SEED

🔎 In context

Creation as birth: nature the womb, God the seed.

📜 Looking deeper

Here “great Brahman” means prakṛti, primal nature, not the Absolute. Commentators: matter alone cannot produce life; the Lord’s seed of consciousness is needed. The image is of cosmic parenthood. Mahad brahma, “great Brahman”, here means prakṛti, primal matter, as Śaṅkara and Rāmānuja both explain. The “seed” is consciousness, the higher nature of 7.5. Sāṅkhya taught that matter evolves when spirit is near; the Gītā adds that the Lord brings them together. Many Hindu images show the Divine as father and mother together, as in Ardhanārīśvara, Śiva and Pārvatī in one body. Commentators note that the Lord calls prakṛti “my womb”, mama yoniḥ: nature is not opposed to the Divine but belongs to it. Some readers find here a basis for reverence toward nature and for the image of the Goddess as Mother Nature. Śākta traditions see in prakṛti the Devī herself, the creative power of the Lord.

🌱 For today

Every living being carries a divine seed. Treat life with reverence. Every living being carries a divine seed. Treat all life with reverence.

↑ 14.3 · Nature the womb, God the seed.

THE FATHER WHO GIVES THE SEED

“Whatever forms are born in all wombs, son of Kuntī, great Brahman is their womb, and I am the seed-giving father.”
WordMeans
sarva-yoniṣu kaunteyain all wombs

ŚLOKA 14.4 · DEEP DIVE

THE FATHER WHO GIVES THE SEED

🔎 In context

God as father of all beings.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators note every species, not only humans. All living things share one father. A basis for kindness to all creatures. Indian tradition speaks of 8.4 million species, caurāsī lakh yoni, through which souls pass. Commentators say the verse covers all of them: plants, animals, humans and gods. Śaṅkara says all bodies come from nature and the Lord as consciousness. Rāmānuja says the Lord unites each soul with its body. Many find in this verse a basis for kindness to all creatures and care for the earth. Commentators note that the verse calls the Lord pitā, father, of all forms in all wombs. Śrī Vaiṣṇava teachers say this is why every being deserves kindness: all are the Lord’s children. Jain and Buddhist ethics reach a similar respect for all life by different reasoning.

🌱 For today

All creatures are family. That changes how we treat them. All creatures are family, with one father. Let that shape how you treat animals and nature.

ŚLOKA 14.5

THREE QUALITIES BIND
sattvaṃ rajastama iti guṇāḥ prakṛtisaṃbhavāḥnibadhnanti mahābāho dehe dehinamavyayam ‖5‖
“Sattva, rajas and tamas: these guṇas born of prakṛti bind the imperishable embodied one in the body, mighty-armed one.”
WordMeans
sattvam rajaḥ tamaḥ itisattva, rajas, tamas
guṇāḥ prakṛti-sambhavāḥthe guṇas born of nature
nibadhnanti mahā-bāhobind, mighty-armed
dehe dehinam avyayamthe imperishable embodied one in the body
Three strands that bind.

ŚLOKA 14.5 · DEEP DIVE

THREE QUALITIES BIND

🔎 In context

The three guṇas introduced: they bind the soul.

📜 Looking deeper

Guṇa means quality, and also rope or strand. All three bind, even sattva. Commentators: sattva is light, rajas is activity, tamas is inertia. Everything in nature is a mix of the three. Guṇa also means a strand of rope; the three strands twist together to bind. The Sāṅkhya Kārikā (12–13) describes sattva as light and pleasant, rajas as moving and stimulating, tamas as heavy and covering. They work together like the wick, oil and flame of a lamp. Commentators note that the Self is called avyaya, imperishable, even while bound: the binding is not of its nature. Commentators note that the Self is called dehī, “the embodied one”, and avyaya, imperishable, in the same line. The binding is real in experience but not in essence. Śaṅkara says the guṇas seem to bind because the Self is mistaken for the body. Rāmānuja says the self is truly bound by karma, but its nature remains pure.

🌱 For today

Our moods are woven from these three strands. Learning to see them is the first step to freedom. Notice which strand is strongest in you right now: clarity, restlessness or heaviness.

↑ 14.5 · Three strands that bind.

“Of these, sattva, being pure, is illuminating and free from ill. It binds by attachment to happiness and to knowledge, sinless one.”

ŚLOKA 14.6 · DEEP DIVE

SATTVA: THE GOLDEN CHAIN

🔎 In context

Even goodness binds, through attachment to happiness and knowledge.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators call sattva a golden chain: still a chain. Pride in being good or learned is subtle bondage. Yet sattva is the best starting point for freedom. Ramakrishna told a famous story. Three robbers stood for the three guṇas. Tamas wanted to kill a traveller, rajas to tie him up, and sattva freed him and led him to the edge of the forest. But sattva would not go home with him, for fear of the police. Sattva brings you near freedom but cannot take you all the way. Commentators say the pride of the learned or the good is the golden chain. Commentators note anagha, “sinless one”, used for Arjuna here: a gentle reminder that he is fit for the teaching. Madhusūdana says attachment to knowledge binds when one thinks “I am learned”. Many teachers warn against spiritual pride, the subtle sense of being better than others because of one’s practice or knowledge.

🌱 For today

Notice attachment even to good things: being right, being calm, knowing more. Notice attachment even to good things: being right, being calm, knowing more. Hold them gently.

↑ 14.6 · Even goodness can bind.

ŚLOKA 14.7 · DEEP DIVE

RAJAS: THE DRIVE TO ACT

🔎 In context

Rajas binds through restless activity.

📜 Looking deeper

Rajas is the energy of ambition and busyness. Commentators: it is not bad in itself, but its craving keeps the mind restless. Modern life is often called rājasika. Tṛṣṇā, thirst or craving, is also the Buddha’s word (taṇhā) for the cause of suffering. Commentators note that rajas is necessary for action: without it nothing gets done. The problem is its craving. Śaṅkara says rajas binds by making one think “I must do this to get that”. Āyurveda links rajas with spicy, stimulating foods and fast living. Commentators note that rajas is called rāgātmaka, “of the nature of passion”. Śaṅkara links it to the restless desire that keeps beings busy. Modern life, with its emphasis on productivity and constant stimulation, is often described as rājasika. The Gītā does not ask us to stop working, but to notice when work is driven by craving.

🌱 For today

Busyness can be a trap. Notice when you act from restlessness rather than purpose. Before starting the next thing, ask: am I acting from purpose or from restlessness?

↑ 14.7 · Rajas binds by restless action.

“Know tamas as born of ignorance, deluding all embodied beings. It binds, Bhārata, through negligence, laziness and sleep.”

ŚLOKA 14.8 · DEEP DIVE

TAMAS: THE FOG

🔎 In context

Tamas binds through dullness.

📜 Looking deeper

Three marks: pramāda (carelessness), ālasya (sloth), nidrā (excess sleep). Commentators: tamas is the heaviest; rajas at least moves, and can be redirected. Tamas is necessary too: sleep restores, stability holds things together. The problem is excess. Commentators list three marks: pramāda, carelessness, not attending to what matters; ālasya, sloth, not wanting to act; nidrā, too much sleep or dullness. Śaṅkara says tamas covers discrimination. Teachers advise moving from tamas to rajas first: get up and act, then aim at sattva. Commentators note that tamas is said to delude all embodied beings: no one is entirely free of it. Śaṅkara says it covers knowledge as clouds cover the sun. Some modern readers compare tamas to depression or burnout, though the Gītā’s idea is broader. The remedy offered in Indian traditions is gentle activity, light food and good company.

🌱 For today

When you feel heavy and dull, gentle action is the cure. When you feel heavy and dull, take one small action. Movement is the first cure for fog.

ŚLOKA 14.9 · DEEP DIVE

WHAT EACH ATTACHES TO

🔎 In context

A summary of the three bindings.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators offer a ladder: rise from tamas to rajas (get moving), from rajas to sattva (calm and clarity), and then beyond sattva too. A summary in one verse. Commentators often teach the ladder: from tamas to rajas, from rajas to sattva, then beyond. Some compare the guṇas to traffic lights: red (tamas) stops, yellow (rajas) rushes, green (sattva) flows. The aim is to drive with awareness, not to stop forever on green. Madhusūdana says even sattva must be offered to the Lord. Commentators note jñānam āvṛtya, “covering knowledge”: tamas does not destroy wisdom; it hides it. When tamas lifts, knowledge reappears. Śaṅkara says the three guṇas each bind to something different, so the remedy for each differs. The Gītā’s three-part map helps one see which remedy is needed.

🌱 For today

Know which guṇa holds you today. Then you know what to do next. Know which guṇa holds you today. Then you know what step to take next.

ŚLOKA 14.10

THEY COMPETE
rajastamaścābhibhūya sattvaṃ bhavati bhāratarajaḥ sattvaṃ tamaścaiva tamaḥ sattvaṃ rajastathā ‖10‖
“Sattva prevails, overpowering rajas and tamas, Bhārata; rajas, overpowering sattva and tamas; and likewise tamas, overpowering sattva and rajas.”
WordMeans
rajaḥ tamaḥ ca abhibhūyaoverpowering rajas and tamas
sattvam bhavati bhāratasattva arises
rajaḥ sattvam tamaḥ ca evarajas, over sattva and tamas

The three take turns ruling.

ŚLOKA 14.10 · DEEP DIVE

THEY COMPETE

🔎 In context

The three are always mixed, one dominating at a time.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators: the mix changes through the day. Morning may be sāttvika, afternoon rājasika, night tāmasika. We can tilt the balance by food, company and habit. The guṇas are never found alone; they compete, and one dominates at a time. Āyurveda and yoga traditions link this to daily rhythms: early morning, brāhma muhūrta, is sāttvika and best for meditation. Commentators note that food (17.8–10), company and habits all tilt the balance. Śaṅkara says knowing this helps one guard against the lower guṇas. Commentators note that the three guṇas are compared to three strands of one rope: never fully separate. Śaṅkara says one prevails by suppressing the other two. In Āyurveda, the doṣas vāta, pitta and kapha are related but different ideas; practitioners often speak of the guṇas and doṣas together when advising on diet and routine.

🌱 For today

THE ABODE OF BRAHMAN

🔎 In context

The chapter ends with Kṛṣṇa as the ground of all.

📜 Looking deeper

A much-debated verse: “I am the abode of Brahman.” Śaṅkara reads it as identity: Kṛṣṇa is Brahman itself. Rāmānuja reads: the Lord is the support even of the freed soul’s Brahman-state. Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavas read the personal Lord as the source of the impersonal Brahman. Pratiṣṭhā means foundation, abode or ground. Śaṅkara reads it as “I am the very substance of Brahman”. Rāmānuja reads it as “I am the support of the freed self’s state”. Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavas read it as Kṛṣṇa the person being the ground even of the impersonal Brahman, as the sun is the source of its rays. We note these readings without choosing among them. The colophon names chapter 14 Guṇa-Traya-Vibhāga Yoga. Commentators note sukha aikāntika, “absolute bliss”: joy that does not depend on anything else. Madhva says the Lord is the source of the bliss of the freed. The chapter ends by pointing beyond the guṇas to their source.

🌱 For today

Behind every ideal, truth, dharma, bliss, is a single source. Behind every ideal, truth, dharma and joy, there is one source. Seek it.

Chapter 14 in five lines
The source (14.1–4). Nature is the womb, the Lord the seed-giving father of all beings.
Binding (14.5–9). Sattva binds by happiness and knowledge, rajas by action, tamas by negligence.
Signs (14.10–13). Clarity shows sattva; greed and restlessness, rajas; fog and inertia, tamas.
Fruits (14.14–18). Sattva leads up, rajas stays in the middle, tamas goes down.
Beyond (14.19–27). The guṇātīta is the same in all; unwavering devotion carries one beyond; the Lord is the abode of Brahman and bliss.
