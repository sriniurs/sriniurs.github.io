FOREWORD

On a battlefield, between two armies of one family, the greatest archer of his age drops his bow and refuses to fight. His charioteer, Kṛṣṇa, answers him. Their conversation, 700 verses long, is the Bhagavad Gītā.

For over two thousand years it has been chanted in homes, argued over by philosophers, carried by soldiers and saints, and read by people facing every kind of crisis. It asks the oldest questions: who am I, what should I do, and how can I be free?

This series takes all 18 chapters, every śloka on two pages, in plain English with comic illustrations, alongside our other scriptures and the Daśāvatāra.

WHAT IS THE GĪTĀ?

The essentials on one page

Name

Bhagavad Gītā, “the Song of the Lord”. Also called the Gītopaniṣad.

Size

700 ślokas in 18 chapters (Gītā Press count).

Where

Chapters 25–42 of the Bhīṣma Parva of the Mahābhārata, the sixth of its eighteen books.

Speakers

Kṛṣṇa teaches; Arjuna asks; Sañjaya reports to the blind king Dhṛtarāṣṭra.

Setting

The field of Kurukṣetra, on the first day of the great war, between two armies of one family.

Author

Traditionally Vyāsa, compiler of the Mahābhārata.

Place in tradition

One of the three foundations of Vedānta (prasthāna-traya), with the Upaniṣads and the Brahma Sūtras.

Day

Gītā Jayantī: the bright ekādaśī of Mārgaśīrṣa (Mokṣadā Ekādaśī).

ZAPPY: Why is it so loved?

PROF. BOLT: It speaks to a person in crisis, in a few hundred verses, and gives not one answer but several paths, for different kinds of people.

A conversation on a battlefield.

HOW DID IT COME TO THIS?

The story before the Gītā
Two families, one throne. The blind king Dhṛtarāṣṭra rules Hastināpura. His hundred sons, led by Duryodhana, are the Kauravas; his late brother Pāṇḍu’s five sons are the Pāṇḍavas.
The dice. Śakuni wins the Pāṇḍavas’ kingdom in a rigged game; Draupadī is insulted in the court.
Exile. Twelve years in the forest and one in hiding. On return, Duryodhana will not give even five villages.
Kṛṣṇa’s choice. He offers one side his army and the other himself, unarmed. Arjuna chooses Kṛṣṇa as charioteer; Kṛṣṇa also goes to the Kaurava court to plead for peace, and fails.
The divine eye. Vyāsa gives Sañjaya sight to see the whole war from the palace. After ten days, when Bhīṣma falls, Sañjaya tells the king everything, including the Gītā.

ZAPPY: So the Gītā is told in a flashback?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. Sañjaya reports it on the tenth day of the war, to the blind king who started it all.

A family war, and a reluctant hero.

WHO IS WHO

The people of the Gītā

Kṛṣṇa

Arjuna’s cousin, friend and charioteer; for the Gītā, the Supreme Lord. Called Keśava, Mādhava, Govinda, Hṛṣīkeśa, Vāsudeva…

Arjuna

Third Pāṇḍava, the greatest archer; called Pārtha, Dhanañjaya, Guḍākeśa, Kaunteya, Bhārata…

Dhṛtarāṣṭra

The blind king, father of the Kauravas; speaks only the first verse.

Sañjaya

His minister, given divine sight by Vyāsa; narrates the Gītā.

Bhīṣma

The grandsire, bound by a vow to serve the throne; commander of the Kauravas.

Droṇa

Teacher of both sides in arms.

Duryodhana

Eldest Kaurava, whose refusal to share led to the war.

Karṇa

Duryodhana’s friend, secretly Kuntī’s eldest son.

ZAPPY: Why so many names for Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna?

PROF. BOLT: Each name is chosen for the moment and often carries a hint. Watch for them as you read.

Many teachers divide the Gītā into three ṣaṭkas (groups of six): the first on the Self and action, the middle on God and devotion, the last on knowledge and freedom. The divisions overlap: every part has action, devotion and knowledge in it.

FOUR PATHS IN ONE BOOK

The yogas of the Gītā

Karma yoga

Action without craving the fruit, as an offering (chapters 2–5, 18). For the active.

Jñāna yoga

Knowledge of the Self as distinct from body and mind (chapters 2, 4, 13–15). For the reflective.

Dhyāna yoga

Meditation: steadying the mind like a lamp in a windless place (chapters 5–6). For the contemplative.

Bhakti yoga

Devotion: love of the Lord, offering everything (chapters 7–12, 18). For the loving.
The Gītā does not force one path. It weaves them together, and the great commentators disagree on which is chief. That disagreement is part of its richness.

ZAPPY: Which path is best?

PROF. BOLT: Kṛṣṇa says different paths suit different natures (3.3, 12.8–11, 13.24). Find yours, and walk it sincerely.

Many paths, one goal.

HOW THE GREAT TEACHERS READ IT

Commentators across the centuries

Śaṅkara (8th c.)

Advaita: the Self is one with Brahman; knowledge frees; action purifies the mind for knowledge. The oldest surviving full commentary.

Rāmānuja (11th–12th c.)

Viśiṣṭādvaita: souls and world are the body of the Lord; devotion and surrender (prapatti) lead to him.

Madhva (13th c.)

Dvaita: God, souls and matter are eternally distinct; devotion to Viṣṇu with knowledge.

Jñāneśvar (13th c.)

The Jñāneśvarī, a beloved Marathi poetic commentary, reading the Gītā as a song of devotion and wisdom.

Modern readers

Tilak saw a call to action (Gītā Rahasya); Gandhi a guide to selfless, non-violent work (Anāsakti Yoga); Vivekananda and Aurobindo a manifesto of strength and spiritual life.

Our approach

Where readings differ, we give the main ones without taking sides. Meanings are our own plain-English renderings.
One text, many readings.

The whole teaching in five lines
