FREEDOM BY LETTING GO

BHAGAVAD GĪTĀ · CHAPTER 18 · MOKṢA SANNYĀSA YOGA
Do not grieve · every śloka explained
HOW THIS WORKS: The whole chapter in one go for chanting, then every śloka on two pages: first the chant, a picture, what it says and the key words; then a deep dive into its context, how the great teachers read it, and what it means for us today.

WHERE WE ARE

In Chapter 17 Kṛṣṇa showed how faith, food, worship and giving take on the colours of the three guṇas, and taught oṃ tat sat.

Chapter 18, the longest, gathers the whole Gītā. True renunciation is letting go of the fruits, not of the work. Knowledge, action, the doer, understanding and even happiness come in three kinds. Work done as worship leads to perfection. Then come Kṛṣṇa’s last words, “abandon all dharmas and take refuge in me alone; do not grieve”, and Arjuna’s answer: “my delusion is gone; I will do as you say.”

ŚLOKA 18.1 · DEEP DIVE

SANNYĀSA AND TYĀGA

🔎 In context

The last chapter opens with Arjuna’s final question. Chapter 18 is named Mokṣa-Sannyāsa Yoga, the yoga of freedom through renunciation. At 78 verses it is the longest chapter, and many teachers call it a summary of the whole Gītā. Arjuna addresses Kṛṣṇa as Keśi-niṣūdana, slayer of the horse-demon Keśī, a story from Kṛṣṇa’s youth in Vraja.

📜 Looking deeper

Two words that both mean “giving up”: sannyāsa and tyāga. The whole chapter, the longest, gathers the Gītā’s teaching under this theme. Its name, Mokṣa Sannyāsa Yoga, means freedom through renunciation. Kṛṣṇa is called “slayer of Keśī”, a horse-demon he killed as a boy. Commentators note that the question goes back to the very first doubt of the Gītā. In chapter 1 Arjuna wanted to give up the fight; in 3.1 and 5.1 he asked whether renouncing action is better than doing it. Now, at the end, he asks for a final, careful distinction. Śaṅkara treats the chapter as gathering the teachings of the whole Gītā into one place. Rāmānuja says it sums up karma, jñāna and bhakti and then gives the final teaching of surrender. Commentators note that Arjuna uses three names for Kṛṣṇa in one verse: mahā-bāho, Hṛṣīkeśa and Keśi-niṣūdana. Madhusūdana says this shows his eagerness at the close of the teaching. Jñāneśvar devotes nearly a fifth of his whole commentary to this final chapter, calling it the temple’s golden pinnacle.

🌱 For today

Even at the end, keep asking clear questions. A good question asked at the end of a long study can bring everything together. Keep asking.

ŚLOKA 18.2 · DEEP DIVE

TWO DEFINITIONS

🔎 In context

Sannyāsa: dropping selfish actions. Tyāga: dropping attachment to results of all actions. The tradition knows many kinds of sannyāsa: the formal monastic vow, with ochre robes and the giving up of the sacred fire, and the inner renunciation the Gītā has praised throughout (5.3, 6.1).

📜 Looking deeper

This distinction is central to the chapter. Commentators note the Gītā prefers tyāga: it can be practised in any life, by anyone. Commentators note that Kṛṣṇa gives the views of the kavayaḥ, the wise, and the vicakṣaṇāḥ, the discerning, without yet giving his own. Śaṅkara says kāmya karma, actions done for desired results, such as rituals for wealth or heaven, are what sannyāsa gives up. Duties that must be done, nitya karma, are not included. Rāmānuja says the two terms ultimately mean the same thing: giving up attachment to fruits. Gandhi built his life on tyāga in this sense: full work, no personal claim. The order of sannyāsins founded in Śaṅkara’s tradition, the Daśanāmī order, still gives monks the ochre robe and new names. Vaiṣṇava and other traditions have their own renunciant orders. The Gītā honours formal renunciation but teaches that tyāga, inner letting go, is open to everyone, monk or householder.

🌱 For today

You may not leave your work, but you can always let go of its fruits. You may not be able to leave your work, but you can always loosen your grip on its rewards.

WHERE KṚṢṆA AND ARJUNA ARE

🔎 In context

The Gītā’s last verse: where divine wisdom and human effort join, there is victory. This is the last verse of the Gītā. In the epic, the war that follows lasts eighteen days, and the Pāṇḍavas win.

📜 Looking deeper

Sañjaya’s final word to the blind king is a quiet warning: the Pāṇḍavas will win. Commentators read it universally: where God (Kṛṣṇa, wisdom) and the dedicated human (Arjuna, effort) work together, success follows. The Gītā ends not with the war, but with this promise. Commentators read the verse at two levels. In the story, it is Sañjaya’s last warning to the blind king: with Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna together, the Pāṇḍavas cannot lose. Universally, it is a formula: where divine wisdom (Kṛṣṇa, the Lord of yoga) and dedicated human effort (Arjuna, the archer) join, there is fortune, victory, well-being and sound policy. Some note that the Gītā’s first word is dharma and its last is mama. Its story moves from māmakāḥ, “mine”, to true dharma. Commentators note dhruvā nītiḥ, “sound policy” or “firm justice”: where divine wisdom and human effort unite, there is lasting good governance. Madhusūdana ends his commentary with praise of Kṛṣṇa. Gītā Jayantī is celebrated on the bright ekādaśī of Mārgaśīrṣa, the day the Gītā is said to have been spoken.

Chapter 18 in five lines
Letting go (18.1–12). Keep sacrifice, charity and austerity, but drop attachment and fruit: that is sāttvika tyāga.
Action and its kinds (18.13–40). Five causes of every act; knowledge, action, doer, understanding, resolve and happiness in three kinds.
Work as worship (18.41–49). Worship the Lord through your own work; your own dharma, even imperfect, is best.
The summit (18.50–66). Become Brahman, reach supreme devotion, take refuge in the Lord; “abandon all dharmas, take refuge in me; do not grieve.”
The close (18.67–78). Arjuna: “my delusion is gone; I will do as you say.” Where Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna are, there is victory.
