WHERE WE ARE

In Chapter 5 Kṛṣṇa showed that true renunciation is inner, and ended with a first glimpse of meditation (5.27–28).

Chapter 6 is the Gītā’s meditation manual: where to sit, how to sit, how much to eat and sleep, and what to do when the mind wanders. Arjuna protests that the mind is like the wind; Kṛṣṇa agrees, and gives two keys: practice and detachment. And he promises that no sincere effort is ever lost.

ŚLOKA 6.1

THE TRUE SANNYĀSĪ AND YOGI
śrībhagavān uvācaanāśritaḥ karmaphalaṃ kāryaṃ karma karoti yaḥsa saṃnyāsī ca yogī ca na niragnirna cākriyaḥ ‖1‖
The Lord said: One who does the work that is to be done without depending on its fruit is a renunciant and a yogi; not one who merely gives up the sacred fire and all activity.
WordMeans
anāśritaḥ karma-phalamnot depending on the fruit of action
kāryam karma karoti yaḥwho does the work to be done
saḥ sannyāsī ca yogī cahe is a renunciant and a yogi

Renunciation is inner, not outer.

ŚLOKA 6.1 · DEEP DIVE

THE TRUE SANNYĀSĪ AND YOGI

🔎 In context

Chapter 6, on meditation, opens by repeating chapter 5’s lesson: renunciation is inner. Chapter 6 is named Dhyāna Yoga or Ātma-Saṃyama Yoga, the yoga of meditation or self-control. It is the Gītā’s practical guide to meditation and closes the first group of six chapters, often called the karma ṣaṭka.

📜 Looking deeper

A renunciant traditionally gave up the household fire (agni). Kṛṣṇa says this outer act alone does not make one a sannyāsī. Commentators note the Gītā keeps returning to this point because it is easily misunderstood. The traditional sannyāsī gave up the sacred household fire and the Vedic rites. Commentators note that Kṛṣṇa does not reject formal renunciation; he says it is not enough. Śaṅkara accepts that karma yoga is called sannyāsa here as praise, because both share inner renunciation. Rāmānuja says the karma yogi is already a renunciant in the true sense. The chapter begins by placing meditation inside an active life. Commentators note kāryaṃ karma, “work that should be done”: the true renunciant still does his duty. Madhusūdana says giving up the fire without giving up desire is only a change of clothes. Many teachers say the test of renunciation is inner freedom, visible in calm and kindness.

🌱 For today

You don’t need to give up your life to be spiritual. Give up clinging to results. Before you meditate, check your work. A mind that clings to results will carry that clinging to the cushion.

↑ 6.1 · Renunciation is inner, not outer.

ŚLOKA 6.2

RENUNCIATION IS YOGA
yaṃ saṃnyāsamiti prāhuryogaṃ taṃ viddhi pāṇḍavana hyasaṃnyastasaṅkalpo yogī bhavati kaścana ‖2‖
“Know that what they call renunciation is yoga, Pāṇḍava; for no one becomes a yogi without renouncing selfish schemes (saṅkalpa).”
WordMeans
yam sannyāsam iti prāhuḥwhat they call renunciation
yogam tam viddhi pāṇḍavaknow that as yoga
na hi asannyasta-saṅkalpaḥfor without renouncing schemes
yogī bhavati kaścanano one becomes a yogi
Drop the schemes: that is yoga.

ŚLOKA 6.2 · DEEP DIVE

RENUNCIATION IS YOGA

🔎 In context

Renunciation and yoga are the same at heart: giving up selfish planning.

📜 Looking deeper

Saṅkalpa means the mind’s plans and wishes for the future. Commentators say the root of restlessness is constant scheming; letting it go is the start of meditation. Saṅkalpa has several meanings: resolve, intention, imagination, the mind’s planning for its own ends. Here it means the restless scheming that drives desire (4.19, 6.4, 6.24). Commentators note that the Gītā does not oppose good resolve; it opposes the endless selfish planning that keeps the mind agitated. Śaṅkara says renouncing saṅkalpa is the shared core of sannyāsa and yoga. The Yoga Sūtra speaks of calming the movements of the mind; letting go of saṅkalpa is a practical way into that calm. Commentators note that sannyāsa and yoga are called one because both require letting go of selfish plans. Madhusūdana says the karma yogi who drops saṅkalpa is a renunciant in spirit. Modern mindfulness teachers note that much anxiety comes from constant planning; letting it rest brings peace.

🌱 For today

Notice how much of the mind is busy planning gains. Letting some of that go brings rest. Notice how often your mind plans gains for yourself. Letting some of those plans rest is the first step to stillness.

↑ 6.2 · Drop the schemes: that is yoga.

ŚLOKA 6.3

CLIMBING AND ARRIVING
ārurukṣormuneryogaṃ karma kāraṇamucyateyogārūḍhasya tasyaiva śamaḥ kāraṇamucyate ‖3‖
“For the sage who wishes to climb to yoga, action is said to be the means; for one who has climbed to yoga, calm is said to be the means.”
WordMeans
ārurukṣoḥ muneḥ yogamfor the sage wishing to climb to yoga
karma kāraṇam ucyateaction is called the means
yoga-ārūḍhasyafor one who has climbed to yoga
śamaḥ kāraṇam ucyatecalm is called the means
Climb by work; rest in calm.

ŚLOKA 6.3 · DEEP DIVE

CLIMBING AND ARRIVING

🔎 In context

Two stages: climbing (by action) and arriving (by calm).

📜 Looking deeper

A clear map of the spiritual life. Beginners need work; the advanced need stillness. Śaṅkara reads it as a progression from karma yoga to renunciation; others as a shift of emphasis within one life. Ārurukṣu, one who wishes to climb, and yogārūḍha, one who has climbed: two stages of practice. Commentators of every school use this verse as a map. Śaṅkara: action purifies the mind until one can climb; then renunciation and calm lead to knowledge. Rāmānuja: action leads to the vision of the self; once reached, calm sustains it. Many teachers compare it to a ladder: one climbs with effort and then stands at the top in stillness. The verse also guards against two errors: lazy calm before one is ready, and restless action after one has arrived. Commentators note kāraṇa, “means”: action is the means for the climber, calm for the one who has arrived. Madhusūdana says one should not mistake the stage. Many traditions use the image of a boat: you need it to cross the river, but not once you have landed.

🌱 For today

Know your stage. Effort first; ease later. Ask honestly: am I still climbing, or have I arrived? Most of us need effort now. Ease will come.

↑ 6.3 · Climb by work; rest in calm.

ŚLOKA 6.4

WHEN HAS ONE ARRIVED?

“When one is not attached to sense objects or to actions, and has renounced all selfish schemes, then he is said to have climbed to yoga.”
WordMeans
yadā hi na indriya-artheṣuwhen not to sense objects
na karmasu anuṣajjatenor to actions is attached

ŚLOKA 6.4 · DEEP DIVE

WHEN HAS ONE ARRIVED?

🔎 In context

The mark of arrival: no attachment to objects or to action itself.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators note two attachments: to things, and to being busy. Both must go. Commentators note two attachments: to objects of the senses and to actions themselves. The second is subtler. Many people let go of pleasures but cling to being busy, useful or in control. Sarva-saṅkalpa-sannyāsī, “one who has given up all scheming”, is the mark of arrival. Śaṅkara says this is the state where renunciation becomes natural. Rāmānuja says it is the state where the mind rests in the self. The verse gives a practical test: if we can sit still without craving objects or activity, we have climbed. Commentators note anuṣajjate, “clings”: the mark of arrival is no clinging to objects or to activity. Madhusūdana says this is a high state, reached after long practice. Teachers warn students not to claim it too early.

🌱 For today

Some of us are attached to being busy. Notice that too. Sit for five minutes with nothing to do. Notice whether the urge to be busy arises. That urge is also an attachment.

↑ 6.4 · Unattached to things and to doing.

ŚLOKA 6.5

LIFT YOURSELF BY YOURSELF

“Lift yourself by yourself; do not let yourself sink. For the self alone is the friend of the self, and the self alone is the enemy of the self.”

ŚLOKA 6.5 · DEEP DIVE

LIFT YOURSELF BY YOURSELF

🔎 In context

One of the most practical verses of the Gītā: you are your own best friend and worst enemy.

📜 Looking deeper

Here “self” (ātman) means one’s own mind and will. Commentators say no one else can do our inner work for us. Teachers guide; we must climb. This is one of the most quoted verses in the Gītā. The word ātman is used six times, in different senses. Commentators explain: the higher self, our understanding and will, lifts the lower self, the restless mind. Śaṅkara says no one else is our real friend or enemy in the matter of liberation; it depends on us. Rāmānuja says the mind, rightly trained, is the self’s friend. Vivekananda loved this verse and often taught self-reliance from it. Modern psychology speaks of self-compassion and self-talk; the verse anticipates both. Commentators note avasādayet, “let sink”: the self can drag itself down through laziness, despair and wrong habits. Madhusūdana says no one else can save us if we refuse to help ourselves. Many modern self-help ideas, from self-efficacy to positive self-talk, echo this ancient verse.

🌱 For today

Be your own friend today. Encourage yourself as you would a friend. Speak to yourself today as a good friend would: honestly, kindly, with encouragement.

↑ 6.5 · You are your own friend and enemy.

ŚLOKA 6.6

FRIEND OR ENEMY
bandhurātmā’tmanastasya yenātmaivātmanā jitaḥanātmanastu śatrutve vartetātmaiva śatruvat ‖6‖
“The self is a friend to one who has conquered himself by himself; but for one who has not, the self acts like an enemy.”
WordMeans
bandhuḥ ātmā ātmanaḥ tasyathe self is a friend to him
yena ātmā eva ātmanā jitaḥby whom the self is conquered by the self
anātmanaḥ tu śatrutvebut for the unconquered, in enmity

ŚLOKA 6.6 · DEEP DIVE

FRIEND OR ENEMY

🔎 In context

A mastered mind helps; an unmastered one harms.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators: the same mind that drags us down, once trained, lifts us. Discipline turns an enemy into a friend. This verse explains the last: whether the self is friend or enemy depends on whether it has been mastered. Commentators note the military language: jita, conquered; śatru, enemy. Arjuna is a warrior; Kṛṣṇa speaks his language. Śaṅkara says the unconquered mind harms us like a real enemy, driving us into wrong action. Rāmānuja says the same mind, trained by yoga, becomes our closest ally. The Dhammapada (verse 160) has a similar thought: the self is the master of the self; who else could be the master? Commentators note jitaḥ, “conquered”: the mind once mastered becomes an ally. Madhusūdana says the unconquered mind leads us into harm like an enemy who lives in our own house. Training the mind is the most important work, because the mind is with us everywhere.

🌱 For today

Train the mind gently, and it will serve you. Your mind can be your toughest critic or your best coach. Training it is the most useful work you can do.

↑ 6.6 · A trained mind is a friend.

ŚLOKA 6.7

ŚLOKA 6.7 · DEEP DIVE

CALM IN HEAT AND COLD

🔎 In context

Peace holds through every pair of opposites, including praise and insult.

📜 Looking deeper

Honour and dishonour are added to heat and cold: social opposites are often harder to bear. Commentators read paramātmā samāhitaḥ as “absorbed in the Supreme”. Commentators read paramātmā samāhitaḥ in two ways. Some take paramātmā as the Supreme Self, on which the yogi’s mind is fixed. Others take it as the yogi’s own self, now elevated and steady. Śaṅkara takes it as the Self of the knower, which, freed of agitation, becomes the Supreme. Rāmānuja reads it as the yogi’s self in its supreme state. The verse widens the pairs of opposites from physical (cold and heat) to social (honour and dishonour), which many find harder to bear. Chapter 12 (12.18–19) returns to praise and blame. Commentators note praśānta, “deeply peaceful”: peace comes after self-conquest. Madhusūdana says such a person is not shaken by heat or cold, praise or blame. Many saints met insults calmly; Jesus’ teaching to turn the other cheek is a familiar parallel.

🌱 For today

Praise and blame are weather too. Let them pass. Praise and criticism are like weather. Notice them, learn what is useful, and let the rest blow over.

↑ 6.7 · Steady in praise and blame.

ŚLOKA 6.8 · DEEP DIVE

GOLD AND CLAY ALIKE

🔎 In context

To the yogi, earth, stone and gold have the same worth.

📜 Looking deeper

Kūṭastha: like an anvil, which stays the same while things are hammered on it. Commentators say this is not ignorance of value, but freedom from greed. Kūṭastha, literally “standing on the peak” or “like an anvil”, describes something that stays unchanged while things happen to it. The word returns in 12.3 and 15.16. Commentators note that seeing gold and clay as equal is not ignorance of value but freedom from greed. The sage knows gold can buy things but does not crave it. Śaṅkara says the knower sees the one Self in all, so value differences do not disturb him. Rāmānuja says the knower sees all matter as matter, different from the self. Ramakrishna famously practised by holding a coin in one hand and a clod in the other, saying “money is earth”, and throwing both into the Ganges. Commentators note jñāna-vijñāna-tṛptātmā, “satisfied with knowledge and realisation”: the yogi’s contentment comes from understanding. Madhusūdana says one who has knowledge and its experience needs nothing more. The famous image of gold and clay shows freedom from greed, not ignorance of value.

Money matters for living; it need not rule our heart. Money is useful; it is not the measure of a person or a life. Hold it lightly.

FRIEND AND FOE ALIKE

Even harder than gold and clay: friend and foe alike. Suhṛn-mitrāry-udāsīna-madhyastha-dveṣya-bandhuṣu, a long compound listing well-wishers, friends, enemies, the indifferent, the neutral, the hateful and relatives.

📜 Looking deeper

Equal-mindedness toward people is the highest test. Commentators note it does not mean treating everyone the same way, but holding no hatred and no favouritism in the heart. Commentators distinguish the terms carefully. A suhṛt helps without expecting return. A mitra is a friend through affection. An ari is an enemy. The verse is the hardest test of equanimity so far, after objects in 6.8. Śaṅkara says equal-mindedness means no hatred or attachment, while still acting appropriately. Rāmānuja says the yogi sees the same self in all. For Arjuna, facing kinsmen as enemies, the verse is pointed. Commentators note sādhuṣv api ca pāpeṣu, “among the good and the wicked”: even toward wrongdoers, the yogi holds no hatred. Madhusūdana says this is the highest test. Mahatma Gandhi often said, “hate the sin, not the sinner”, a principle close to this verse.

🌱 For today
