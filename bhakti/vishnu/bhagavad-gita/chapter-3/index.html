Work as offering · every śloka explained

WHERE WE ARE

In Chapter 2 Kṛṣṇa taught that the Self never dies, and that one should act without craving the fruit, with an even mind.

Arjuna is confused: if understanding is higher, why fight? Chapter 3 answers: no one can stop acting, so act well. Do your work as an offering (yajña), for the good of the world, without the ego claiming it. And know your real enemy: desire.

ŚLOKA 3.1

“WHY PUSH ME INTO THIS?”
arjuna uvācajyāyasī cetkarmaṇaste matā buddhirjanārdanatatkiṃ karmaṇi ghore māṃ niyojayasi keśava ‖1‖
Arjuna said: If you hold that understanding is better than action, Janārdana, why then do you urge me into this terrible deed, Keśava?
WordMeans
jyāyasī cetif it is better
karmaṇaḥ te matā buddhiḥunderstanding is, in your view, than action
tat kimthen why
karmaṇi ghoreinto a terrible deed
niyojayasido you urge
Arjuna asks: why act at all?

ŚLOKA 3.1 · DEEP DIVE

“WHY PUSH ME INTO THIS?”

🔎 In context

Arjuna has heard chapter 2 praise understanding (2.49: action is far inferior to buddhi-yoga) and also command him to fight. He hears a contradiction. Chapter 3 is called Karma Yoga, the yoga of action. It opens with Arjuna’s second question in the Gītā (after 2.54). He calls Kṛṣṇa both Janārdana, “mover of people”, and Keśava: two names in one verse, perhaps showing his agitation.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators take this question seriously. Śaṅkara uses it to argue that knowledge and action cannot be combined for the same person at the same time. Rāmānuja answers that action done with knowledge is itself the path. The Gītā’s own answer unfolds in this chapter. Arjuna has heard two things: “understanding is better” (2.49) and “fight” (2.18, 2.37). He wants to know which. Commentators find his question the key to the Gītā’s structure. Śaṅkara’s long introduction to chapter 3 argues that knowledge and action are for different kinds of people at different stages, so the Gītā cannot be teaching their combination for one person. Rāmānuja argues the opposite: action, done with knowledge of the self, is itself a means to liberation. Madhva sides with action done in devotion. Tilak later built his Gītā Rahasya around this chapter. Commentators note ghora karma, “terrible action”: Arjuna still sees war as dreadful. Madhusūdana says his question is sincere, not argumentative. Many readers recognise the dilemma: if wisdom is higher than action, why must the wise still act? Chapter 3 answers it at length.

🌱 For today

When advice seems to contradict itself, ask. Arjuna’s honest confusion leads to one of the clearest chapters in the Gītā. Contradictions in advice are often levels in disguise. Asking “which applies to me, now?” can resolve them.

↑ 3.1 · Arjuna asks: why act at all?

ŚLOKA 3.2

“TELL ME ONE THING”
vyāmiśreṇeva vākyena buddhiṃ mohayasīva metadekaṃ vada niścitya yena śreyo’hamāpnuyām ‖2‖
“With these seemingly mixed words you confuse my understanding. Tell me for certain the one thing by which I may reach the highest good.”
WordMeans
vyāmiśreṇa iva vākyenawith seemingly mixed words
buddhim mohayasi iva meyou seem to confuse my mind
tat ekam vada niścityatell me that one thing for certain
yena śreyaḥ aham āpnuyāmby which I may reach the good
A student asks for one clear path.

ŚLOKA 3.2 · DEEP DIVE

“TELL ME ONE THING”

🔎 In context

Arjuna asks for a single, clear answer.

📜 Looking deeper

Note the word iva, “seemingly”: Arjuna is polite; he does not say Kṛṣṇa is confused, only that his words seem mixed. Commentators praise this as the attitude of a good student: questioning without accusing. Arjuna uses iva, “as if”, twice. Commentators read this as courtesy toward the teacher, but also as a hint: the confusion lies in the hearer, not the teaching. Śreyas, the highest good, is the word Arjuna used in 2.7. Śaṅkara says Arjuna wants one path, either knowledge or action, because he cannot follow both at once. Rāmānuja says Arjuna wants to know how they fit together. The chapter will answer both: there are two paths (3.3), but action is needed by all who are not yet perfect (3.4–8), and even the perfect act for the world (3.20–25). Commentators note that Arjuna admits his own confusion: “you seem to confuse my understanding”. A good student reports confusion honestly. Madhusūdana says the confusion arises because Kṛṣṇa praised knowledge and then urged action. The answer will show that the two serve one goal.

🌱 For today

We all want one simple rule. Sometimes life needs two truths held together. A good teacher shows how they fit. Asking for one clear answer is fine. Be ready, though, for an answer that shows how two things fit together.

↑ 3.2 · A student asks for one clear path.

ŚLOKA 3.3

TWO PATHS
śrībhagavān uvācaloke’smindvividhā niṣṭhā purā proktā mayānaghajñānayogena sāṃkhyānāṃ karmayogena yoginām ‖3‖
The Lord said: In this world a twofold path was taught by me of old, sinless one: the yoga of knowledge for those of contemplation, and the yoga of action for those of action.
WordMeans
loke asmin dvi-vidhā niṣṭhāin this world a twofold path
purā proktā mayāwas taught by me of old
jñāna-yogena sāṅkhyānāmby knowledge for the contemplative
karma-yogena yogināmby action for the active
Two paths, for two natures.

ŚLOKA 3.3 · DEEP DIVE

TWO PATHS

🔎 In context

Kṛṣṇa answers: there have always been two paths, suited to two kinds of people. Jñāna-yogena sāṅkhyānām, knowledge for the contemplatives; karma-yogena yoginām, action for the yogis.

📜 Looking deeper

This verse is central to the debate between schools. Śaṅkara reads it as two paths for two different kinds of seeker, renunciants and householders; action prepares the mind, knowledge frees. Others, like Tilak in modern times, held that the Gītā mainly teaches karma yoga for everyone. Traditions differ. Niṣṭhā means a firm standing, a settled way of life. Purā, “of old”, hints that this teaching is ancient, as 4.1–3 will explain. Anagha, “sinless”, is a gentle encouragement: Arjuna feared sin, but Kṛṣṇa calls him sinless. Commentators debate whether the two paths are parallel or successive. Śaṅkara: successive, action first, then knowledge. Rāmānuja: both valid; karma yoga is easier and safer for most. Madhva: both culminate in devotion. Chapter 5 returns to the question (5.2–5) and concludes that the wise see the two as one. Vivekananda later spoke of four yogas, adding bhakti and rāja yoga, for four temperaments. Commentators note niṣṭhā, “settled way”: a whole life is shaped by the path chosen. Some modern teachers compare the two to two kinds of temperament, the reflective and the active. Neither is better in itself; each suits different people.

🌱 For today

People differ. Some find peace in study and reflection; others in work and service. Both can lead to the same goal. There is more than one way up the mountain. Choose the path that suits your nature, and respect others on theirs.

↑ 3.3 · Two paths, for two natures.

ŚLOKA 3.4

NOT BY AVOIDING WORK
na karmaṇāmanārambhānnaiṣkarmyaṃ puruṣo’śnutena ca saṃnyasanādeva siddhiṃ samadhigacchati ‖4‖
“Not by not starting actions does a person reach freedom from action, nor by mere renunciation does one reach perfection.”
WordMeans
na karmaṇām anārambhātnot by not beginning actions
naiṣkarmyamactionlessness, freedom from action
puruṣaḥ aśnutedoes a person attain
na ca sannyasanāt evanor by renunciation alone
siddhim samadhigacchatidoes one attain perfection
Freedom is not doing nothing.

ŚLOKA 3.4 · DEEP DIVE

NOT BY AVOIDING WORK

🔎 In context

Freedom from action does not come from simply not doing anything.

📜 Looking deeper

Naiṣkarmya is a key word: the state in which actions no longer bind. Kṛṣṇa says it cannot be reached by idleness, nor by outer renunciation alone. Śaṅkara agrees that action purifies the mind first; renunciation is meaningful only after that. Naiṣkarmya, the state beyond action, is a central Vedānta term. Sureśvara, Śaṅkara’s disciple, wrote a whole work titled Naiṣkarmya-siddhi. Commentators agree that naiṣkarmya does not mean doing nothing. It means a state where action no longer binds, because the sense of “I am the doer” is gone (4.18–20). Śaṅkara says outer renunciation without a pure mind does not bring it; inner purity comes first, through selfless action. Rāmānuja says the self is realised through action done without attachment. The verse warns against a shortcut that many seekers are tempted by: dropping duties early. Commentators note that siddhi, perfection, is not reached merely by giving up. Gandhi wrote that he found peace not by withdrawing from work but by changing his attitude to it. Many monks also say that outer renunciation is only a beginning.

🌱 For today

Dropping duties does not bring peace. Peace comes from doing them in the right spirit. Quitting does not make us free. Doing well, without grasping, does.

↑ 3.4 · Freedom is not doing nothing.

ŚLOKA 3.5

NO ONE CAN STOP ACTING
na hi kaścitkṣaṇamapi jātu tiṣṭhatyakarmakṛtkāryate hyavaśaḥ karma sarvaḥ prakṛtijairguṇaiḥ ‖5‖
“For no one can remain even for a moment without acting; everyone is made to act, helplessly, by the guṇas born of prakṛti.”
WordMeans
na hi kaścit kṣaṇam apifor no one, even for a moment
jātu tiṣṭhati akarma-kṛtever remains without acting
kāryate hi avaśaḥ karmais made to act helplessly

Living is acting: the question is how.

ŚLOKA 3.5 · DEEP DIVE

NO ONE CAN STOP ACTING

🔎 In context

Even sitting still, we breathe, think and live. Nature keeps us acting.

📜 Looking deeper

The guṇas, nature’s three qualities, drive every living thing. Commentators add: even the wish to stop acting is itself an action of the mind. The only real choice is how to act. Prakṛti, nature or matter, and its three guṇas, sattva (clarity), rajas (activity) and tamas (inertia), are the Gītā’s map of the moving world. They are explained in chapter 14. Commentators note that even sleep is a kind of action of tamas, and thinking is action of rajas or sattva. Śaṅkara adds that this applies to the unenlightened; the knower, identified with the Self, sees the guṇas acting but does not think “I act”. Rāmānuja says even the knower must act while embodied. The point for Arjuna: refusing to fight is still an action, driven by guṇas, and will have results. Commentators note avaśaḥ, “helplessly”: the guṇas drive action even when we resist. Modern biology agrees that the body is never fully at rest; the heart beats, cells work, the brain is active even in sleep. The Gītā’s point is that we cannot opt out of action, only choose its spirit.

🌱 For today

You cannot opt out of acting. So the real question is: how will you act? Since we are always acting, let us choose our actions rather than drift into them.

↑ 3.5 · Living is acting: the question is how.

ŚLOKA 3.6

THE HYPOCRITE
karmendriyāṇi saṃyamya ya āste manasā smaranindriyārthānvimūḍhātmā mithyācāraḥ sa ucyate ‖6‖
“One who restrains the organs of action but sits dwelling in his mind on sense objects is deluded and is called a hypocrite.”
WordMeans

yaḥ āste manasā smaranwho sits remembering in mind
indriya-arthānsense objects

A still body with a busy craving is pretence.

ŚLOKA 3.6 · DEEP DIVE

THE HYPOCRITE

🔎 In context

Outer stillness with inner craving is pretence. Karmendriyāṇi saṃyamya, “restraining the organs of action”: the five organs of action are speech, hands, feet, the organs of excretion and of generation.

📜 Looking deeper

The Gītā cares about the mind, not the show. A man sitting in meditation while dreaming of pleasures is worse off than an honest worker. Commentators warn against spiritual display without inner change. Manasā smaran, “remembering with the mind”, dwelling on objects. Commentators link this verse to 2.59: abstaining from objects leaves the taste. The pretend renouncer sits still, but the mind keeps feeding. Śaṅkara says such a person is vimūḍhātmā, deluded, because he thinks outer stillness is the goal. The Gītā consistently values the inner over the outer: chapter 17 will distinguish austerity done for show (17.18) from true austerity. Many traditions have the same warning. Jesus’ words against those who pray to be seen are a familiar parallel. Commentators note vimūḍhātmā, “deluded self”: the pretender deceives himself before others. Many traditions warn against outward piety without inner change. The Tamil saint-poet Tiruvaḷḷuvar says that matted hair and ash do not make a sage; inner purity does.

🌱 For today

Looking spiritual is not the same as being spiritual. The test is what fills the mind. If you meditate or pray, notice where the mind goes. Honest wandering is better than pretend stillness.

↑ 3.6 · A still body with a busy craving is pretence.

ŚLOKA 3.7 · DEEP DIVE

THE YOGI AT WORK

🔎 In context

The honest worker who acts without attachment surpasses the pretend renouncer.

📜 Looking deeper

This is the first full statement of karma yoga: mind controls senses; hands and feet work; the heart stays unattached. Commentators say this person is superior to the hypocrite of 3.6, and is on the way to knowledge. This verse defines karma yoga practically: mind in charge, senses governed, body active, heart free. Commentators contrast 3.6 and 3.7: one controls the body but not the mind; the other controls the mind and uses the body. Śaṅkara says the karma yogi is superior to the hypocrite, not to the true renunciant. Rāmānuja says the karma yogi is superior to anyone who attempts knowledge without first purifying the mind. The phrase viśiṣyate, “excels”, is strong praise for ordinary working life done well. Commentators note viśiṣyate, “excels”: the karma yogi excels the pretender. Madhusūdana says self-control with the mind, combined with active work, is the right balance for most people. A farmer, a nurse or a teacher who works without selfishness is practising this verse.

🌱 For today

You can practise yoga at your desk, in your kitchen, in your clinic. It is about how you work. Most of us will never sit in caves. The Gītā says that is fine: a well-done day of work, free of grasping, is yoga.

WORK AS YAJÑA

🔎 In context

The key idea of chapter 3: action binds when done for oneself; it frees when done as yajña, an offering.

📜 Looking deeper

Yajña began as the Vedic fire rite, but the Gītā widens it. Śaṅkara and others quote a Vedic saying, “yajña is Viṣṇu”: work done for the Lord. Commentators note that the Vedic idea of yajña was not only ritual but a cosmic principle of exchange. Modern ecological thinkers have found in this idea a basis for sustainable living: take from nature only with gratitude, and give back. Before a task, quietly offer it: to God, to family, to the world. The same work feels different. Choose one daily task and silently dedicate it to something larger than yourself. Watch how it changes the doing.

Life is built on giving. What we give into the world comes back in ways we do not always see. Every good thing we enjoy came through someone’s effort or nature’s gift. Remembering that is the start of gratitude.

Taking without giving back is theft.

A strong word: stena, thief. Commentators say whoever enjoys the earth, sun and rain without gratitude or return is stealing. Say thanks, give back, share. Gratitude turns taking into receiving. Count today what you received without paying: sunlight, a smile, clean water. Then find one way to give back.

EATING THE LEFTOVERS

🔎 In context

Food first offered and then eaten is pure; food cooked only for oneself is sin.

📜 Looking deeper

This is the root of prasāda: food offered to God and then shared. Many Hindu homes still offer food before eating. Commentators widen the point: whatever we enjoy after first giving is blessed. Ritually, food is first offered to the deity and then eaten as prasāda. Ethically, whatever we enjoy should first be shared. The custom of feeding a guest, a cow or a bird before eating, still kept in many homes, comes from this. Śaṅkara notes that the five daily offerings remove the small, unavoidable harms of household life, such as killing insects while cooking. The verse turns eating into worship. Commentators note that food cooked only for oneself is called agham, sin. The Sikh langar, community kitchens in temples and the custom of feeding guests before oneself all express this verse. Many families still set aside a portion of each meal for a cow, a crow or a beggar.

Share first, enjoy after. Even a small habit of offering changes our relationship to what we have. Before eating, pause for a moment of thanks or offering. It changes food from fuel into a gift.

📜 Looking deeper

Ancient people believed sacrifice brought rain. Read more widely, the verse shows interdependence: our actions affect the whole cycle of life. Commentators of every school agree on the point of connection. This chain is found in older texts too. Every job, however small, is part of a larger cycle that keeps life going. Next time you eat, trace the chain back: farmer, rain, sun, soil. You are part of that chain.

The ordinary and the sacred are not separate. The Divine is present in work done as an offering. The sacred is not elsewhere. It is present in honest work and sincere offering, right where we are.

ŚLOKA 3.35 · DEEP DIVE

BETTER YOUR OWN DHARMA

🔎 In context

Be yourself, not a copy. Arjuna was tempted to live like a monk, which was not his path.

📜 Looking deeper

This verse returns as 18.47. Commentators debate what svadharma is: duty by birth (traditional) or by nature and calling (many modern readers). Both agree: imitation of another’s path, however attractive, is unsafe. Arjuna, a warrior, was drawn to the life of a beggar-monk (2.5). Kṛṣṇa says: that is not your path. The same teaching returns in 18.47, with the addition that one who acts by one’s own nature incurs no sin. Commentators read svadharma in different ways. Traditionally, it meant duties of one’s station in life. Many modern readers, including Vivekananda and Aurobindo, read it as one’s own inner calling. Gandhi took it as a call to serve in one’s own setting rather than imitate others. All agree the verse warns against borrowed lives. Commentators note bhayāvahaḥ, “fraught with fear”: another’s path brings danger. Madhusūdana says one acting against one’s nature is always anxious. Career counsellors often give similar advice: work that fits your strengths brings both success and peace.

🌱 For today

Your own path, with its flaws, is safer than borrowing someone else’s. Comparing ourselves with others makes us restless. Doing our own work, even clumsily, brings peace.

↑ 3.35 · Your own path, even imperfect.

ŚLOKA 3.36 · DEEP DIVE

WHAT DRIVES US TO WRONG?

🔎 In context

Arjuna asks the question everyone asks: why do I do what I know I shouldn’t? Arjuna’s question begins with atha kena prayukto’yam, “then impelled by what does a person…”.

📜 Looking deeper

This is a universal human question, found also in other traditions. Arjuna notices the gap between knowing and doing. Commentators praise the question as deeply practical. This is Arjuna’s third question. Commentators admire its universality. Duryodhana in the Mahābhārata is quoted in a famous verse: jānāmi dharmaṃ na ca me pravṛttiḥ, jānāmy adharmaṃ na ca me nivṛttiḥ, “I know dharma but cannot do it; I know adharma but cannot stop”. That verse comes from a later text, the Pāṇḍava Gītā, but it is widely quoted. St Paul says something similar in Romans 7: “the good that I would, I do not”. Śaṅkara notes that Arjuna has understood 3.34 and now asks for the root cause. The answer comes in one word: desire. Commentators note balād iva niyojitaḥ, “as if forced”: wrongdoing feels compelled. The Mahābhārata shows many characters who knew right from wrong but could not act on it, such as Dhṛtarāṣṭra, who loved his son too much to restrain him.

🌱 For today

We all know the gap between what we intend and what we do. Asking why is the first step to closing it. If you keep doing what you don’t want to do, don’t only blame willpower. Look for the hidden driver.

↑ 3.36 · Why do we do what we know is wrong?

ŚLOKA 3.37 · DEEP DIVE

DESIRE, THE ENEMY

🔎 In context

Kṛṣṇa’s answer: one enemy with two faces, desire and anger.

📜 Looking deeper

Anger is desire blocked (2.62). Both come from rajas, restless energy. Mahāśana, a great eater: desire is never filled. Commentators call this the most practical diagnosis in the Gītā. Kṛṣṇa answers in one breath and names the enemy precisely. Commentators note that desire and anger are one force: anger is desire frustrated (2.62). Rajas, the guṇa of passion and restlessness, is their source (14.7, 14.12). Mahāśana, “great eater”: desire consumes everything given to it and asks for more. Śaṅkara says desire is the enemy because it drives one to wrong action. Rāmānuja says it is the soul’s chief obstacle. In 16.21, desire, anger and greed are called the three gates of hell. Commentators note mahā-pāpmā, “greatly sinful”: desire leads to many wrongs. Madhusūdana says desire and anger are one force in two forms. Many teachers advise studying one’s own desire closely, since it is the root of anger.

🌱 For today

Look behind anger and you will often find a desire. Name it, and its grip loosens. Track your anger back. Often you will find a desire that was blocked. Deal with the desire, and the anger cools.

↑ 3.37 · The enemy is desire, turning into anger.

ŚLOKA 3.38 · DEEP DIVE

SMOKE, DUST, WOMB

🔎 In context

Three images of how desire hides wisdom.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators read them as grades: smoke is light and clears with a breeze (sattva), dust needs wiping (rajas), the womb needs time (tamas). Desire covers wisdom lightly or heavily depending on its strength. Three images, three degrees of covering. Commentators often map them to the three guṇas. Smoke barely hides a fire and a breeze clears it; this is the slight covering of a sattvic mind. Dust on a mirror needs effort to wipe, the rajasic mind. The womb fully hides the child until birth, the tamasic mind, which needs time and patience. Śaṅkara keeps it simpler: knowledge is always there, like fire, but desire hides it. The mirror image is a favourite in many traditions. Zen and Sufi teachers also speak of polishing the mirror of the heart. Commentators note āvṛtam, “covered”: knowledge is not destroyed by desire, only hidden. This gives hope: the Self’s clarity is always there, waiting to be uncovered. The three images show different degrees of effort needed.

🌱 For today

Some confusion clears quickly; some needs effort; some needs patience. Know which you face. Wisdom is not missing, only covered. The work is clearing, not adding.

↑ 3.38 · Desire covers wisdom like smoke covers fire.

ŚLOKA 3.39 · DEEP DIVE

AN INSATIABLE FIRE

🔎 In context

Desire is like fire: the more you feed it, the bigger it grows.

📜 Looking deeper

The Manusmṛti and other texts say the same: desire is never satisfied by enjoyment, as fire is not quenched by ghee. Commentators note that for the wise, desire is an enemy even when it looks like a friend. The Manusmṛti (2.94), and the Mahābhārata in the story of King Yayāti, give the classic simile. Desire is never satisfied by enjoyment; like fire fed with ghee, it only grows. Yayāti, after a thousand years of pleasure, reached this very conclusion. Commentators note that desire is called the “constant enemy of the wise” because the ignorant mistake it for a friend; the wise see its harm. Kāma-rūpa also means “able to take any form”: desire changes shape to tempt. Śaṅkara says it is the cause of every pain the wise see coming. Commentators note jñānino nitya-vairiṇā, “the eternal enemy of the wise”: even the wise must stay alert. Madhusūdana says the ignorant see desire as a friend because it promises pleasure. The wise see it as an enemy because it brings pain.

🌱 For today

Feeding a craving rarely ends it. Starving it of attention works better. Notice the cycle: desire, satisfaction, more desire. Stepping out of it brings a quieter joy.

↑ 3.39 · Desire is a fire never full.

ŚLOKA 3.40 · DEEP DIVE

WHERE DESIRE HIDES

🔎 In context

To fight an enemy, know where it lives: senses, mind and intellect.

📜 Looking deeper

Desire enters through the senses, settles in the mind, and even corrupts the intellect with clever reasons. Commentators say this is why mere argument cannot defeat it. This verse is a map of the enemy’s territory: three strongholds. Commentators explain how desire uses each. It catches the eye through the senses, it settles in the mind as longing, and in the intellect it builds reasons why giving in is right. The last is the most dangerous, because it looks like wisdom. Śaṅkara says knowing the seats of desire is the first step to defeating it. The next verses describe the strategy: start at the outer gate (3.41), then rise through the levels (3.42–43). Commentators note adhiṣṭhāna, “seat” or “base”: desire has strongholds. Madhusūdana says one must attack it in its seats. Military imagery fits Arjuna, a warrior: know the enemy’s bases before battle.

🌱 For today

Watch how desire makes clever arguments for itself. The intellect too can be captured. When you hear yourself making very clever arguments for something you crave, be suspicious. Desire may be speaking.

↑ 3.40 · Desire lives in senses, mind and intellect.

ŚLOKA 3.41 · DEEP DIVE

BEGIN WITH THE SENSES

🔎 In context

A practical order: start with the senses.

📜 Looking deeper

Jñāna is knowledge; vijñāna is realised, lived knowledge (the theme of chapter 7). Commentators say control of the senses is the first step because they are the gate. Jñāna and vijñāna: Śaṅkara takes the first as knowledge from scripture and teacher, the second as one’s own direct experience of it. Rāmānuja takes them as knowledge of the self and of its distinct nature. The pair returns as the title of chapter 7, Jñāna-Vijñāna Yoga. Commentators note the practical order: start with the senses. One cannot argue with desire in the intellect while letting it pour in through the eyes. Ancient training of students, brahmacarya, began with sense discipline for this reason. Commentators note ādau, “first”: begin with the senses. Madhusūdana says controlling the senses weakens desire’s supply lines. Simple practices like fasting on certain days, keeping silence or limiting entertainment train the senses.

🌱 For today

Start with the gates: what you watch, eat and listen to. The mind follows. Guard the gates first: what you see, hear, eat. Inner work is easier when the outer doors are watched.

↑ 3.41 · Begin with the senses.

ŚLOKA 3.42 · DEEP DIVE

THE LADDER UP

🔎 In context

A ladder of power from the senses up to the Self.

📜 Looking deeper

This closely follows the Kaṭha Upaniṣad (1.3.10). Commentators: use each higher level to govern the lower. Ultimately the Self, beyond all, is the source of strength. The Kaṭha Upaniṣad (1.3.10–11) gives a fuller ladder: beyond the senses are the objects, beyond them the mind, then the intellect, then the great self, then the unmanifest, then the Person, beyond whom there is nothing. The Gītā uses a shorter form. Commentators read saḥ, “He”, as the Self (Śaṅkara) or the individual self (Rāmānuja), or, in some readings, desire itself, which hides even beyond the intellect. Most take it as the Self. The practical meaning: each higher level can govern the lower. When all fail, rest in what is beyond all. Commentators note that this ladder shows the order of control: what is higher can govern what is lower. Madhusūdana says the Self, being highest, can govern all. Many meditation teachers use this order to guide practice: senses, mind, intellect, then rest in the Self.

🌱 For today

Let the intellect guide the mind, and the mind guide the senses. And remember what is beyond all three. When the senses pull, call on the mind. When the mind wavers, call on reason. When reason tires, remember who you are.

↑ 3.42 · The Self is beyond the intellect.

ŚLOKA 3.43 · DEEP DIVE

SLAY THE ENEMY

🔎 In context

The chapter ends as a battle cry, not against the Kauravas but against desire.

📜 Looking deeper

The real battlefield is inside. Commentators note the phrase “steady the self by the Self”: the lower self is steadied by the higher. Chapter 6 develops this (6.5–6). The chapter ends with the same verb Kṛṣṇa used of the outer battle: jahi, “slay”. But the enemy now is desire. Commentators note how the Gītā moves the battlefield inside: the true Kurukṣetra is the heart. Śaṅkara says the lower self, the mind, is steadied by the higher Self through knowledge. Rāmānuja says the mind is steadied by fixing it on the self. Gandhi read the whole Gītā this way: the war is within, and the weapon is selfless action. The chapter colophon names it Karma Yoga, the yoga of action. Chapter 4 will add knowledge, and reveal who the teacher really is. Commentators note saṃstabhya, “steadying”: the Self is used to steady the mind. Madhusūdana says the victory over desire is the greatest victory. The chapter that began with Arjuna’s confusion about action ends with a call to inner battle.

🌱 For today

The hardest battle is within. Knowing what you truly are gives the strength to win it. The hardest victories are inner. Name your enemy (one craving, one habit) and take one step against it today.

↑ 3.43 · Know the Self, and slay desire.
