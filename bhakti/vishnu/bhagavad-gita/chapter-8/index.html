BHAGAVAD GĪTĀ · CHAPTER 8 · AKṢARA BRAHMA YOGA
What you remember at the end · every śloka explained
HOW THIS WORKS: The whole chapter in one go for chanting, then every śloka on two pages: first the chant, a picture, what it says and the key words; then a deep dive into its context, how the great teachers read it, and what it means for us today.

WHERE WE ARE

In Chapter 7 Kṛṣṇa revealed his two natures and ended with words like adhyātma and adhiyajña, and a hint about the moment of death.

Chapter 8 answers Arjuna’s seven questions. Its heart is simple: what you remember at death, you become; so remember me at all times, and fight. Then Kṛṣṇa opens a vast view of cosmic time and the two paths of departure.

SEVEN QUESTIONS · I

ŚLOKA 8.1 · DEEP DIVE

SEVEN QUESTIONS · I

🔎 In context

Chapter 7 ended with technical terms (7.29–30). Arjuna asks what they mean. Chapter 8 is named Akṣara-Brahma Yoga, the yoga of the imperishable Brahman. It is the Gītā’s chapter on death and what lies beyond.

📜 Looking deeper

Arjuna asks seven questions in two verses: six about terms and one about death. Commentators praise his alertness: a good student asks about every unclear word. Commentators note that Arjuna calls Kṛṣṇa Puruṣottama, “Supreme Person”, a title explained in 15.18. He has been listening closely; every term from 7.29–30 is picked up. Śaṅkara says Arjuna wants to know the meaning of each, so that he can meditate rightly. Rāmānuja says each term names something to be known by those who seek different goals. The terms come from Upaniṣadic and Brāhmaṇa vocabulary of the time. Madhusūdana says Arjuna’s seven questions show careful listening. Students in traditional schools were trained to repeat and question each word their teacher used.

🌱 For today

Never pass over a word you don’t understand. Ask. When a teacher uses words you don’t understand, ask about each one. Clear words lead to clear thinking.

“Who is the Lord of sacrifice (adhiyajña), and how is he here in this body, Madhusūdana? And how are you to be known at the time of death by the self-controlled?”
WordMeans

ŚLOKA 8.2 · DEEP DIVE

SEVEN QUESTIONS · II

🔎 In context

The last question is the most personal: how to know God at the moment of death.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators note the progression from abstract terms to the most practical question of all: how to die well. The seventh question is the most personal: how is the Lord known at the moment of death? Commentators note that the dying mind is weak and confused, so the question is practical. Śaṅkara says the question is how the self-controlled can fix their minds on the Lord when the senses fail. Rāmānuja says Arjuna asks how each type of seeker should meditate at the end. The Kaṭha Upaniṣad is built around a similar question, Naciketas asking Death what happens after death. The Gītā’s answer, in 8.5–14, is remembrance practised through life. Commentators note niyatātmabhiḥ, “by the self-controlled”: the question is how the disciplined can remember the Lord when the body fails. Many traditions prepare for death through daily practice; the Tibetan Book of the Dead and the Christian ars moriendi are well-known examples outside India.

🌱 For today

How we want to die says a lot about how we should live. Thinking about the end clarifies the present. What would you want to have in mind at your last moment?

ŚLOKA 8.3

THE DEFINITIONS · I
śrībhagavān uvācaakṣaraṃ brahma paramaṃ svabhāvo’dhyātmamucyatebhūtabhāvodbhavakaro visargaḥ karmasaṃjñitaḥ ‖3‖
The Lord said: Brahman is the Imperishable, the Supreme. Its own nature (svabhāva) is called adhyātma. The creative offering that brings beings into existence is called karma.
WordMeans
akṣaram brahma paramamBrahman is the supreme Imperishable
svabhāvaḥ adhyātmam ucyateits own nature is called adhyātma
bhūta-bhāva-udbhava-karaḥthat which brings beings into being
visargaḥ karma-saṃjñitaḥthe offering is called karma
Brahman, Self, karma defined.

ŚLOKA 8.3 · DEEP DIVE

THE DEFINITIONS · I

🔎 In context

Kṛṣṇa defines the first three terms.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators differ on adhyātma: Śaṅkara takes it as Brahman present in each body as the inner Self; Rāmānuja as the nature of the individual soul. Karma here means the cosmic creative act. Akṣara means both “imperishable” and “syllable”; the word links Brahman to Oṃ (8.13). The Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad (3.8.8–9) describes the Akṣara: not coarse, not fine, not short, not long; by its command the sun and moon stand apart. Commentators differ on adhyātma. Śaṅkara: Brahman dwelling in each body as the inner Self. Rāmānuja: the nature of the individual self, or the subtle body. Visarga, the creative “pouring forth”, is read as the cosmic act of creation, or as the offering in sacrifice that sustains beings (3.14). Brief definitions, but each opens a field. Commentators note paramam, “supreme”: Brahman is the highest imperishable. Madhusūdana says the definitions are brief because they were well known to the Gītā’s first hearers. For later readers, the commentaries fill them out.

🌱 For today

Big words become simple when defined. Keep asking until they are. Big ideas become usable when defined simply. Write down in one line what you mean by your own key words.

↑ 8.3 · Brahman, Self, karma defined.

ŚLOKA 8.4 · DEEP DIVE

THE DEFINITIONS · II

🔎 In context

Kṛṣṇa defines the rest: matter, the cosmic Person, and himself as Lord of sacrifice within the body.

📜 Looking deeper

The Lord is present in each body as the receiver of all offerings. Commentators see this as God dwelling within, witnessing every act. Commentators explain the three terms. Adhibhūta: all that changes and perishes, the material world. Adhidaiva: the cosmic Person, Puruṣa, often identified with Hiraṇyagarbha, the cosmic mind from which the gods arise. Adhiyajña: the Lord himself, who receives all sacrifices and dwells in the body as inner witness. Śaṅkara says the Lord as adhiyajña is Viṣṇu, the deity of sacrifice. Rāmānuja says the Lord is the inner controller who receives every offering. The phrase atra dehe, “here in the body”, brings the Divine very close. Commentators note deha-bhṛtāṃ vara, “best of the embodied”: Kṛṣṇa honours Arjuna as he reveals that the Lord dwells within the body. Madhusūdana says the Lord as adhiyajña receives every offering made in the body’s fire of digestion and breath.

🌱 For today

God is not far away; he dwells in this very body. The Divine, in this view, is not far off. It dwells within this very body, receiving every sincere offering.

ŚLOKA 8.5

REMEMBER ME AT THE END
antakāle ca māmeva smaranmuktvā kalevaramyaḥ prayāti sa madbhāvaṃ yāti nāstyatra saṃśayaḥ ‖5‖
“Whoever, at the time of death, leaves the body remembering me alone, reaches my being; there is no doubt of this.”
WordMeans
anta-kāle ca mām evaand at the end, me alone
smaran muktvā kalevaramremembering, leaving the body
yaḥ prayāti saḥ mat-bhāvamwho departs, reaches my being

ŚLOKA 8.5 · DEEP DIVE

REMEMBER ME AT THE END

🔎 In context

The answer to the last question: remember me at the end.

📜 Looking deeper

This verse is why many Hindus chant God’s name at a deathbed. Commentators add that remembrance at the end is the fruit of a whole life of remembrance. This verse lies behind a widespread Hindu custom: chanting God’s name, or reading the Gītā, at the bedside of the dying. Tulasī leaves and Gaṅgā water are often given. Commentators stress that remembrance at the end cannot usually be forced; it grows from a life of remembrance. Śaṅkara says such a person reaches the Lord’s nature. Rāmānuja says he reaches the Lord himself. The story of Ajāmila in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa tells of a sinner who, dying, called his son Nārāyaṇa and was saved by the very name. Devotees love the story; teachers note it as grace, not a technique. Commentators note asaṃśayaḥ, “without doubt”: the promise is firm. Madhusūdana says remembrance at the end is the fruit of daily remembrance. Hospice workers in India often describe families gathering to chant the Lord’s names as a loved one dies.

🌱 For today

Practise remembering the Divine every day; it will be there at the end. Practise remembering what you love most, every day. That is the best preparation for any ending.

↑ 8.5 · Remember me at the end.

ŚLOKA 8.6

WHAT YOU THINK OF, YOU BECOME

“Whatever state one remembers when leaving the body at the end, son of Kuntī, to that state one goes, always absorbed in it.”
WordMeans
yam yam vā api smaran bhāvamwhatever state remembering
tyajati ante kalevaramone leaves the body at the end

ŚLOKA 8.6 · DEEP DIVE

WHAT YOU THINK OF, YOU BECOME

🔎 In context

The last thought shapes the next destination.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators tell the story of King Bharata, who in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa thought of a deer at death and was reborn as one. The point: the habits of a lifetime decide the last thought. The story of Bharata (Jaḍa Bharata) is told in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa (Book 5). A king turned hermit, he became attached to an orphaned deer, thought of it at death, and was reborn as a deer. In his next life, wise from that lesson, he lived as a silent sage. Commentators use the story to explain sadā tad-bhāva-bhāvita, “always coloured by that thought”. The last thought is not random; it is the summary of a lifetime’s habits. Śaṅkara and Rāmānuja agree that the dying mind follows its strongest impressions. The verse is a strong argument for daily practice. Commentators note sadā, “always”: the state at the end is formed by what we dwell on always. Madhusūdana says the last thought cannot be chosen at the last moment by one who has not practised. The verse turns attention from death to daily life.

🌱 For today

What you dwell on daily is what you will dwell on at the end. Choose well. What you think about most is quietly shaping you. Choose your daily thoughts as carefully as your food.

↑ 8.6 · What you think of, you become.

“Therefore, at all times remember me, and fight. With mind and intellect offered to me, you will surely come to me.”

ŚLOKA 8.7 · DEEP DIVE

REMEMBER ME AND FIGHT

🔎 In context

A balanced instruction: remember God and do your duty.

📜 Looking deeper

“Remember me and fight”: not one or the other but both together. Commentators say this sums up the Gītā: devotion in action. Mām anusmara yudhya ca, “remember me and fight”, is one of the Gītā’s shortest summaries. Commentators note the two verbs side by side: remembrance and action, not one instead of the other. Śaṅkara says constant remembrance purifies the mind, and the duty of fighting is done in that spirit. Rāmānuja says remembrance of the Lord while doing one’s duty is the essence of bhakti yoga. Gandhi often quoted this verse. For Arjuna it answers chapter 1: he can act and still be devoted, still be good. Commentators note arpita-mano-buddhiḥ, “mind and intellect offered”: both feeling and thinking are given to the Lord. Madhusūdana says this is how remembrance becomes constant. Many find it practical to link remembrance to daily acts: waking, eating, beginning work.

🌱 For today

Work with God in your heart. That is the whole method in four words. Do your work with the sacred in your heart. Remembrance and action belong together.

↑ 8.7 · Remember me, and fight.

ŚLOKA 8.8 · DEEP DIVE

PRACTICE MAKES IT STEADY

🔎 In context

Practice makes remembrance steady.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators link this to 6.35: practice tames the mind. Here it is directed at the Supreme Person. Abhyāsa, practice, links back to 6.35. Commentators note the method: repeated practice makes the mind ananya-gāmin, “not going to another”. Śaṅkara says practice means repeatedly bringing the mind to one object. Rāmānuja says it means constant meditation on the Lord. The goal, paramaṃ puruṣaṃ divyam, the supreme divine Person, is described in the next verse. The Gītā’s meditation is not empty; it has an object, the Lord, and a means, practice. Commentators note nānya-gāminā, “not going elsewhere”: the trained mind stays on its object. Madhusūdana says practice makes it so. Modern research on attention shows that the ability to focus improves with regular training.

🌱 For today

A little daily practice makes remembering natural. A few minutes of daily practice, kept up for years, builds a mind that remembers on its own.

ŚLOKA 8.9 · DEEP DIVE

THE ANCIENT SEER

🔎 In context

A description of the Supreme to meditate on.

📜 Looking deeper

These phrases echo the Upaniṣads (Śvetāśvatara 3.8: “sun-coloured, beyond darkness”). Commentators say each word is a meditation in itself. The sentence continues into 8.10. Eight descriptions in one verse, each a meditation. Kavi, seer or poet, the all-knowing. Purāṇa, ancient, without beginning. Anuśāsitṛ, ruler, governor of all. Aṇor aṇīyān, subtler than an atom; the Kaṭha Upaniṣad (1.2.20) adds “greater than the great”. Dhātṛ, upholder. Acintya-rūpa, of unthinkable form. Āditya-varṇa, sun-coloured, and tamasaḥ parastāt, beyond darkness, come from the Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad (3.8): “I know that great Person, sun-coloured, beyond darkness”. The verse is in triṣṭubh metre. Commentators note acintya-rūpa, “of unthinkable form”: the Lord’s form is beyond imagination, yet meditated on. Madhusūdana says the descriptions guide the mind toward what is beyond them. A finger pointing at the moon is not the moon, but it helps one find it.

🌱 For today

Choose one phrase, like “beyond darkness”, and hold it in mind for a few breaths. Pick one phrase, like “beyond darkness”, and hold it quietly for a few breaths. Let it light the mind.

“Fire, light, day, the bright fortnight, the six months of the northern course: departing then, knowers of Brahman go to Brahman.”

ŚLOKA 8.24 · DEEP DIVE

THE BRIGHT PATH

🔎 In context

The path of light leads to Brahman.

📜 Looking deeper

This is the devayāna, path of the gods. Bhīṣma waited for uttarāyaṇa before dying in the Mahābhārata. Commentators read these as stages guarded by deities of light. Uttarāyaṇa is the six months when the sun moves north, from the winter solstice to the summer solstice; it is celebrated at Makara Saṅkrānti. In the Mahābhārata, Bhīṣma, with the boon of choosing his death, lay on a bed of arrows for many days and waited for uttarāyaṇa to die. Commentators of the Vedānta schools read the stages as deities guiding the soul through realms of light. Śaṅkara says this path leads to Brahmaloka and gradual liberation. Rāmānuja says it leads to the Lord’s abode. Many Hindus still consider uttarāyaṇa an auspicious time to die. Commentators note brahma-vidaḥ, “knowers of Brahman”: the bright path is for those who know. Madhusūdana says the stages are guarded by deities of light. The image has inspired many to see life itself as a journey toward light.

🌱 For today

Live toward light, and the path will be bright. Live toward light: knowledge, kindness, truth. Then whatever path lies ahead will be bright.

↑ 8.24 · The bright path leads to Brahman.

ŚLOKA 8.25

THE DARK PATH
dhūmo rātristathā kṛṣṇaḥ ṣaṇmāsā dakṣiṇāyanamtatra cāndramasaṃ jyotiryogī prāpya nivartate ‖25‖
“Smoke, night, the dark fortnight, the six months of the southern course: departing then, the yogi reaches the light of the moon and returns.”
WordMeans

ṣaṇmāsāḥ dakṣiṇāyanamsix months of the southern course

The dark path returns.

ŚLOKA 8.25 · DEEP DIVE

THE DARK PATH

🔎 In context

The path of smoke leads to the lunar heaven and back to birth.

📜 Looking deeper

This is the pitṛyāna, path of the ancestors, for those who did good works with desire. Commentators: a good path, but it returns. Dakṣiṇāyana is the sun’s southward course, from summer to winter solstice. The pitṛ-yāna, the path of the ancestors, leads to the lunar realm, a heaven for those who did good works with desire for rewards. When their merit is used up, they return to earth (9.21). Commentators note that this is not a bad path; it is a path of reward within the cycle. Śaṅkara says “yogi” here means one who performed rites. Rāmānuja agrees. The smoke stands for unclarity, the moon for reflected light. Commentators note cāndramasaṃ jyotiḥ, “the light of the moon”: the lunar heaven is a reflected light, as the moon reflects the sun. Madhusūdana says this path is for those who did good works for reward. The moon’s borrowed light suits a borrowed, temporary reward.

🌱 For today

Good works done for reward bring rewards, and then return. Good done for reward brings reward, then returns. Good done for its own sake leads further.

↑ 8.25 · The dark path returns.

ŚLOKA 8.26

ETERNAL PATHS
śuklakṛṣṇe gatī hyete jagataḥ śāśvate mateekayā yātyanāvṛttimanyayā’vartate punaḥ ‖26‖
“These two paths of the world, the bright and the dark, are considered eternal. By one, one goes not to return; by the other, one returns again.”
WordMeans

ŚLOKA 8.26 · DEEP DIVE

ETERNAL PATHS

🔎 In context

Two eternal paths: one to freedom, one back to birth.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators simplify: the bright path of knowledge and devotion, and the dark path of desire-bound works. Commentators simplify the two paths. The bright one is knowledge, devotion and selfless action, leading to freedom. The dark one is action done for reward, leading back to birth. Śaṅkara says the paths are eternal because the cycle of saṃsāra has no beginning. Rāmānuja says they correspond to the two kinds of practitioners. Many modern teachers read the verse ethically: every choice leans toward light or toward smoke. The two paths are not only after death; they are present in how we live now. Commentators note śāśvate, “eternal”: the two paths always exist. Madhusūdana says every being travels one or the other. The verse sums up the cosmology of the passage.

🌱 For today

Every choice leans toward one path or the other. Each choice today leans toward one path or the other. Choose light where you can.

↑ 8.26 · Two paths: freedom or return.

ŚLOKA 8.27 · DEEP DIVE

THE YOGI IS NOT CONFUSED

🔎 In context

The practical conclusion: don’t worry about times; be steady in yoga always.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators say the yogi need not wait for an auspicious moment; constant yoga makes every moment right. This verse lightens the whole passage. Commentators say: having explained the two paths, Kṛṣṇa tells Arjuna not to worry about auspicious times. The yogi who lives in constant yoga is not confused about where to go. Śaṅkara says knowing the paths, the yogi chooses the bright one by living rightly. Rāmānuja says the devotee, remembering the Lord, is not anxious about the time of death. Sarveṣu kāleṣu, “at all times”, echoes 8.7: remember me at all times. The practical advice is simple: live well now. Commentators note naite sṛtī jānan, “knowing these two paths”: knowledge removes anxiety. Madhusūdana says the yogi chooses light by living rightly. Many teachers say: do not worry about auspicious times; make every time auspicious by right living.

🌱 For today

Don’t wait for the perfect time. Live well now. Don’t wait for an auspicious moment to start living well. Every moment is the right time.

↑ 8.27 · Be steady at all times.

“Whatever fruit of merit is declared for the Vedas, sacrifices, austerities and gifts, the yogi who knows this goes beyond it all and reaches the supreme, primal abode.”

ŚLOKA 8.28 · DEEP DIVE

BEYOND ALL MERITS

🔎 In context

The yogi transcends all merits.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators: the merit of good works is good but limited; the yogi aims beyond merit, at the source itself. Four sources of merit are named: study, sacrifice, austerity, charity. Commentators note that each is good and brings reward; the yogi goes beyond all of them. Śaṅkara says knowledge of the Self surpasses all merit. Rāmānuja says knowledge of the Lord’s nature, as taught in chapters 7 and 8, surpasses them. The verse is in triṣṭubh metre, giving the chapter a strong close. The colophon names chapter 8 Akṣara-Brahma Yoga. Chapter 9 will reveal the “royal secret” of devotion. Commentators note ādyam, “primal”: the yogi reaches the original abode. Madhusūdana says the yogi surpasses all merit. The verse closes chapter 8 and prepares chapter 9, the royal secret of devotion.

🌱 For today

Good works are good. Love and knowledge of the source are better still. Good works are good. Knowing and loving their Source is better still.

↑ 8.28 · The yogi goes beyond all merit.

CHAPTER 8 ON ONE PAGE

Chapter 8 in five lines
Seven questions (8.1–4). Brahman, adhyātma, karma, adhibhūta, adhidaiva, adhiyajña, and death, answered in brief.
Death (8.5–8). What you remember at the end, you become; so remember me always, and fight.
The departure (8.9–15). Meditate on the sun-coloured Person; Oṃ at the end; for the constant devotee I am easy to reach.
Cosmic time (8.16–22). Even Brahmā’s world returns; beyond the unmanifest is my supreme abode, reached by undivided devotion.
Two paths (8.23–28). The bright path does not return, the dark one does; the yogi who knows is never deluded.
