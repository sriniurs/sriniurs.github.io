RENOUNCING AND DOING

BHAGAVAD GĪTĀ · CHAPTER 5 · KARMA SANNYĀSA YOGA
Like a lotus leaf on water · every śloka explained
HOW THIS WORKS: The whole chapter in one go for chanting, then every śloka on two pages: first the chant, a picture, what it says and the key words; then a deep dive into its context, how the great teachers read it, and what it means for us today.

WHERE WE ARE

In Chapter 4 Kṛṣṇa revealed the avatāra and showed how knowledge burns karma, ending with “cut doubt and stand up”.

Arjuna still asks: give up action, or do it? Chapter 5 shows they are not opposites. True renunciation is inner; act like a lotus leaf, untouched by water. Then the sage sees one Self in all beings, finds joy within, and knows the Lord as a friend.

ŚLOKA 5.1

WHICH ONE IS BETTER?
arjuna uvācasaṃnyāsaṃ karmaṇāṃ kṛṣṇa punaryogaṃ ca śaṃsasiyacchreya etayorekaṃ tanme brūhi suniścitam ‖1‖
Arjuna said: You praise the renunciation of action, Kṛṣṇa, and then yoga (action). Tell me for certain which one of these two is better.
WordMeans
sannyāsam karmaṇāmrenunciation of actions
punaḥ yogam ca śaṃsasiand again you praise yoga
yat śreyaḥ etayoḥ ekamwhich one of these is better
tat me brūhi su-niścitamtell me that, clearly decided
Renounce or act: which is better?

ŚLOKA 5.1 · DEEP DIVE

WHICH ONE IS BETTER?

🔎 In context

Chapter 4 praised both giving up action (in knowledge) and doing it (in yoga). Arjuna asks again for one clear answer. Chapter 5 is named Karma-Sannyāsa Yoga, the yoga of renouncing action. It is one of the shorter chapters, 29 verses, and resolves the tension between action and renunciation raised in chapters 3 and 4.

📜 Looking deeper

This is Arjuna’s second request for “one thing” (compare 3.2). Commentators note he still thinks in either-or terms; Kṛṣṇa will show the two are not truly opposed. Commentators note that Arjuna’s question echoes 3.2 almost exactly. Chapter 4 ended with knowledge burning karma (4.37) and with the call to take up yoga (4.42), so Arjuna is puzzled again. Śaṅkara uses this question to argue that the two cannot be practised together by one person. Rāmānuja says Arjuna wants to know which is easier. The question’s recurrence shows how hard this point is. Madhusūdana says Arjuna still thinks the two paths cannot be combined. Teachers often meet this in students: a wish for a single rule, when the truth needs balance.

🌱 For today

Many life choices look like either-or. Often a wiser view finds a way that includes both. If you have asked the same question twice, it is a real question. Keep asking until the answer fits.

↑ 5.1 · Renounce or act: which is better?

ŚLOKA 5.2

BOTH LEAD TO FREEDOM
śrībhagavān uvācasaṃnyāsaḥ karmayogaśca niḥśreyasakarāvubhautayostu karmasaṃnyāsātkarmayogo viśiṣyate ‖2‖
The Lord said: Renunciation and the yoga of action both lead to the highest good. But of the two, the yoga of action is better than the renunciation of action.
WordMeans
sannyāsaḥ karma-yogaḥ carenunciation and karma yoga
niḥśreyasa-karau ubhauboth bring the highest good
tayoḥ tubut of the two
karma-sannyāsātthan renouncing action
karma-yogaḥ viśiṣyatekarma yoga is better
Both free; acting is better for most.

ŚLOKA 5.2 · DEEP DIVE

BOTH LEAD TO FREEDOM

🔎 In context

Kṛṣṇa’s answer: both lead to freedom, but karma yoga is better for Arjuna.

📜 Looking deeper

Śaṅkara explains “better” as easier and more suitable for one not yet ready for full renunciation. Rāmānuja reads karma yoga as superior in itself, being free of risk. Both agree it is the right path for Arjuna now. Commentators wrestle with why Kṛṣṇa calls karma yoga better if both lead to the same goal. Śaṅkara: “better” means better for Arjuna and for most people, because outer renunciation without inner readiness fails; true knowledge-renunciation is for the few. Rāmānuja: karma yoga is better because it is easier, does not risk the dangers of a half-renunciation, and already contains self-knowledge. Madhva: karma yoga done as worship is better than a renunciation that may hide pride. Tilak took this verse as the Gītā’s clear preference for an active life. Commentators note niḥśreyasa-karau, “leading to the highest good”: both paths reach freedom. Madhusūdana says karma yoga is better for one not yet ready for full renunciation. Some modern teachers compare it to choosing between climbing a mountain on foot or by a steeper direct route: both reach the top, but most people need the gentler path.

🌱 For today

For most of us, doing our duties in the right spirit is the surer path than leaving them. For most of us, the surer path is not to leave our work but to change how we do it.

ŚLOKA 5.3

THE TRUE RENUNCIANT
jñeyaḥ sa nityasaṃnyāsī yo na dveṣṭi na kāṅkṣatinirdvandvo hi mahābāho sukhaṃ bandhātpramucyate ‖3‖
“Know him as a constant renunciant who neither hates nor desires. Free from the pairs of opposites, mighty-armed one, he is easily freed from bondage.”
WordMeans
jñeyaḥ saḥ nitya-sannyāsīhe is to be known as ever a renunciant
yaḥ na dveṣṭi na kāṅkṣatiwho neither hates nor desires
nirdvandvaḥ hifree of opposites
sukham bandhāt pramucyateis easily freed from bondage
True renunciation is inner.

ŚLOKA 5.3 · DEEP DIVE

THE TRUE RENUNCIANT

🔎 In context

Renunciation is inside: one who neither hates nor craves is a renunciant, whatever he wears.

📜 Looking deeper

This redefines sannyāsa. It is not leaving home but leaving likes and dislikes. Commentators say a karma yogi with this inner freedom is already a sannyāsī. Nitya-sannyāsī, “always a renunciant”, is a striking phrase for a karma yogi who still works. Commentators note the shift from outer to inner: renunciation is not about robes or leaving home, but about likes and dislikes. The same definition returns in 6.1: the true sannyāsī and yogi is one who does his duty without depending on its fruit, not one who merely gives up the sacred fire. Śaṅkara agrees that such a karma yogi is a renunciant in spirit. Rāmānuja says this inner renunciation is the essence of both paths. Commentators note sukham, “easily”: the inner renunciant is freed without struggle. Madhusūdana says such a person is a renunciant even while working. Many householder saints, like Kabīr the weaver and Tukārām the grocer, lived this verse.

🌱 For today

You don’t need to wear orange. Drop hatred and craving, and you have renounced what matters. You can renounce without leaving home. Let go of one grudge or one craving today; that is real renunciation.

↑ 5.3 · True renunciation is inner.

ŚLOKA 5.4

ONLY CHILDREN SEE THEM AS DIFFERENT
sāṃkhyayogau pṛthagbālāḥ pravadanti na paṇḍitāḥekamapyāsthitaḥ samyagubhayorvindate phalam ‖4‖
“Children, not the wise, speak of Sāṅkhya and yoga as different. One truly established in either gains the fruit of both.”
WordMeans
sāṅkhya-yogau pṛthakknowledge and yoga as separate
bālāḥ pravadantichildren say
na paṇḍitāḥnot the wise
ekam api āsthitaḥ samyakestablished well in even one

ŚLOKA 5.4 · DEEP DIVE

ONLY CHILDREN SEE THEM AS DIFFERENT

🔎 In context

Knowledge and action are not rivals. Mastery of one brings the fruit of both.

📜 Looking deeper

Bāla, child, means the immature in understanding. Commentators note that the Gītā repeatedly reconciles paths that people set against each other. Sāṅkhya here means the path of knowledge and renunciation, as in 2.39, not the later philosophical school. Bāla, child, is a gentle word: not foolish, just not yet mature. Commentators see the Gītā’s typical reconciling spirit here. Śaṅkara says the karma yogi, through purity of mind, reaches the knowledge that the renunciant has; so the fruit is one. Rāmānuja says both lead to self-realisation. The verse has been used through history to calm quarrels between schools of practice. Ramakrishna’s teaching, “as many faiths, so many paths”, carries a similar spirit. Commentators note ekam apy āsthitaḥ samyak, “established properly even in one”: thoroughness in one path is enough. Madhusūdana says the fruit of both is liberation. Teachers often advise students to go deep in one practice rather than shallow in many.

🌱 For today

Don’t get stuck arguing which path is best. Walk one sincerely. Instead of arguing over which method is best, master one. Depth in one path brings what the others offer.

↑ 5.4 · Knowledge and action lead to one goal.

ŚLOKA 5.5

ONE WHO SEES TRULY
yatsāṃkhyaiḥ prāpyate sthānaṃ tadyogairapi gamyateekaṃ sāṃkhyaṃ ca yogaṃ ca yaḥ paśyati sa paśyati ‖5‖
“The state reached by the followers of Sāṅkhya is reached by the yogis too. One who sees Sāṅkhya and yoga as one truly sees.”
WordMeans
yat sāṅkhyaiḥ prāpyate sthānamthe state reached by Sāṅkhyas
tat yogaiḥ api gamyateis reached by yogis too
ekam sāṅkhyam ca yogam caknowledge and yoga as one

ŚLOKA 5.5 · DEEP DIVE

ONE WHO SEES TRULY

🔎 In context

The same goal for both.

📜 Looking deeper

“He who sees, sees”: a typical Gītā phrase for true vision (also 13.27). Commentators agree the goal is the same freedom. The phrase yaḥ paśyati sa paśyati, “he who sees, sees”, appears again in 13.27. Commentators call it the Gītā’s mark of real vision. Śaṅkara says those who renounce reach liberation directly through knowledge; karma yogis reach it through knowledge that arises from purity of mind. The destination is one. Rāmānuja says both reach self-realisation, though karma yoga is safer. Madhva says both lead to God when done with devotion. The verse does not erase differences in method; it denies a difference in the final goal. Commentators note sthānam, “state” or “place”: the goal reached by both. Madhusūdana says it is liberation. The verse is quoted to end quarrels between followers of different paths. The image of many rivers reaching one ocean is often used with it.

🌱 For today

Different roads, same destination. That vision brings peace between paths. Different people climb different paths. Seeing the shared summit makes room for respect.

↑ 5.5 · Who sees them as one, truly sees.

ŚLOKA 5.6

saṃnyāsastu mahābāho duḥkhamāptumayogataḥyogayukto munirbrahma nacireṇādhigacchati ‖6‖
“But renunciation is hard to attain without yoga, mighty-armed one. The sage joined in yoga soon reaches Brahman.”
WordMeans

yoga-yuktaḥ muniḥthe sage joined in yoga

ŚLOKA 5.6 · DEEP DIVE

HARD WITHOUT YOGA

🔎 In context

Trying to renounce without first practising karma yoga is painful.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators: karma yoga purifies the mind; with a pure mind, renunciation becomes natural and quick. Without it, renunciation is forced and fragile. Duḥkham here means “with difficulty” or “painful”. Commentators explain: renunciation without prior purification of the mind is like a fruit plucked before it ripens. It is sour, and it falls. Śaṅkara says karma yoga is the preparation that makes true renunciation natural. Rāmānuja says karma yoga itself brings one to the vision of the self quickly. History has many examples of seekers who renounced too soon and struggled. Many teachers advise service and duty first; renunciation, if it comes, comes ripe. Commentators note na cireṇa, “before long”: the yogi joined in karma yoga soon reaches Brahman. Madhusūdana says karma yoga purifies, and the pure mind quickly grasps the truth. The verse encourages those who feel their daily work is not spiritual enough.

🌱 For today

Prepare before you leap. Inner work makes outer change easier. Before making a dramatic change in life, check whether the inner ground is ready. Ripeness matters.

↑ 5.6 · Yoga first; renunciation follows.

ŚLOKA 5.7

THE SELF OF ALL BEINGS
yogayukto viśuddhātmā vijitātmā jitendriyaḥsarvabhūtātmabhūtātmā kurvannapi na lipyate ‖7‖
“Joined in yoga, pure in heart, master of himself and his senses, whose self has become the self of all beings: even acting, he is not stained.”
WordMeans

sarva-bhūta-ātma-bhūta-ātmāwhose self is the self of all beings

ŚLOKA 5.7 · DEEP DIVE

THE SELF OF ALL BEINGS

🔎 In context

Such a person sees himself in all, and his actions do not stain him.

📜 Looking deeper

One who feels the self of all beings as his own cannot act selfishly. Commentators say this is why his actions do not bind. Sarva-bhūtātma-bhūtātmā is a long compound: one whose self has become the self of all beings. Commentators find here the ethical heart of the Gītā’s vision. Śaṅkara: such a person knows the one Self in all, so cannot act selfishly. Rāmānuja: such a person sees all selves as equal in nature to his own, and treats them so. The order of qualities is a ladder: yoga, purity, self-mastery, sense control, and then the universal vision. The phrase na lipyate, “not stained”, connects to the lotus image of 5.10. Commentators note jitendriya, “senses conquered”: self-mastery comes before the universal vision. Madhusūdana says one who sees all beings as himself cannot act selfishly. Many find that genuine empathy changes behaviour more than rules do.

🌱 For today

When we feel others as ourselves, right action becomes natural. Treat others as you would treat yourself, not as a rule but as a way of seeing. Right action follows naturally.

↑ 5.7 · One with all, unstained by action.

ŚLOKA 5.8

“I DO NOTHING AT ALL”
naiva kiṃcitkaromīti yukto manyeta tattvavitpaśyan śṛṇvanspṛśañjighrannaśnangacchansvapan śvasan ‖8‖
“‘I do nothing at all,’ thinks the yogi who knows the truth: while seeing, hearing, touching, smelling, eating, walking, sleeping, breathing,”
WordMeans
na eva kiñcit karomiI do nothing at all
iti yuktaḥ manyetathus the yogi should think
tattva-vitknower of truth
paśyan śṛṇvan spṛśan jighranseeing, hearing, touching, smelling

The Self is the witness.

ŚLOKA 5.8 · DEEP DIVE

“I DO NOTHING AT ALL”

🔎 In context

The knower sees all activity as the senses at work, not the Self.

📜 Looking deeper

This continues into 5.9. It deepens 3.27: the guṇas act; the Self witnesses. Commentators stress it is a vision of truth, not a technique to dodge responsibility. Verses 8–9 list thirteen activities: the five senses, eating, walking, sleeping, breathing, speaking, letting go, grasping and blinking. Commentators explain that the knower sees all of these as the work of the senses and organs, products of nature, while the Self remains a witness. Śaṅkara builds on this to describe the knower’s freedom from all agency. Rāmānuja says the self, knowing it is distinct from the body, does not take the body’s actions as its own. Teachers warn against using this as an excuse: the vision comes from knowledge, not from denial. Commentators note tattva-vit, “knower of the truth”: the vision comes from knowledge, not pretending. Madhusūdana says the senses act, but the Self watches. Some meditation methods ask practitioners to note each action as it happens: walking, lifting, eating. This builds the witnessing awareness described here.

🌱 For today

Watch yourself eating or walking as if from a little distance. It brings calm. Try watching one ordinary act, such as walking or eating, as if from a quiet place behind the eyes. Notice the calm.

ŚLOKA 5.9

THE SENSES AMONG THEIR OBJECTS

“…speaking, letting go, grasping, opening and closing the eyes, he holds that the senses are moving among their objects.”
WordMeans
pralapan visṛjan gṛhṇanspeaking, releasing, grasping
unmiṣan nimiṣan apiopening and closing the eyes

Senses move among objects.

ŚLOKA 5.9 · DEEP DIVE

THE SENSES AMONG THEIR OBJECTS

🔎 In context

Even blinking is just the senses at work.

📜 Looking deeper

The list covers every activity, voluntary and involuntary. Commentators say the point is complete detachment: the Self is never the doer. Unmiṣan nimiṣan, opening and closing the eyes, is the smallest of acts, chosen to show that the vision covers everything. Commentators link the phrase indriyāṇīndriyārtheṣu vartante to 3.28, guṇā guṇeṣu vartante: the senses move among their objects, nature among nature. Śaṅkara stresses that this knowledge is not a pretence but the real seeing of one who knows the Self. Madhusūdana compares it to watching a play: the actors move, but the audience is not on stage. The image helps meditators observe sensations without identifying with them. Commentators note that the list includes involuntary acts, breathing and blinking, along with voluntary ones. Madhusūdana says the knower sees no difference: all are acts of nature. This removes pride in voluntary acts and anxiety about involuntary ones.

🌱 For today

Even ordinary moments can be seen with this calm, witnessing attention. When a strong reaction rises, say quietly: “the senses are meeting their objects”. It creates a small, useful space.

ŚLOKA 5.24 · DEEP DIVE

LIGHT WITHIN

🔎 In context

Happiness, joy and light all within.

📜 Looking deeper

Three “withins”: antaḥ-sukha, antar-ārāma, antar-jyoti. Commentators describe the self-sufficient sage. Brahma-nirvāṇa recalls 2.72. Three “withins”: happiness, delight and light. Commentators note the progression: sukha, a quiet joy; ārāma, a garden of delight; jyoti, inner light, knowledge. Brahma-bhūta, having become Brahman, appears again in 18.54. Śaṅkara reads it as complete identity with Brahman. Rāmānuja reads it as the self attaining a nature like Brahman’s, pure and free. Brahma-nirvāṇa recalls 2.72 and returns in the next two verses. The three “withins” make a fine meditation: rest in inner joy, inner delight, inner light. Commentators note antar-ārāma, “whose garden is within”: an image of an inner pleasure garden. Madhusūdana says such a person has no need of outer gardens. Ramana Maharshi, who lived for decades in the same small place, is often given as an example.

🌱 For today

Look for the light within. It is the steadiest lamp. Spend a few minutes today looking inward for something that is already joyful and bright. It is there.

↑ 5.24 · Joy, delight and light within.

DELIGHTING IN ALL BEINGS’ GOOD

“The sages whose sins are destroyed, whose doubts are cut, who are self-controlled and delight in the welfare of all beings, attain the peace of Brahman.”

ŚLOKA 5.25 · DEEP DIVE

DELIGHTING IN ALL BEINGS’ GOOD

🔎 In context

Inner peace and outer service go together.

📜 Looking deeper

Sarva-bhūta-hite ratāḥ, rejoicing in the good of all beings, appears again in 12.4. Commentators note the Gītā’s sage is not withdrawn but compassionate. Commentators note that the Gītā’s sage is not a cold hermit but a person of active goodwill. Śaṅkara says such sages avoid harming any being. Rāmānuja says they delight in the welfare of others because they see all as like themselves. The four marks form a path: purification (sins destroyed), clarity (doubts cut), self-control, and universal goodwill. Many modern teachers, including Vivekananda, emphasised this verse to link spirituality with service. Commentators note chinna-dvaidhāḥ, “doubts cut”: clarity is part of freedom. Madhusūdana says the sages who rejoice in all beings’ welfare reach peace. The verse links inner freedom with outer service.

🌱 For today

Peace grows when we delight in others’ good. Find joy in others’ good. It is one of the surest signs, and causes, of inner peace.

↑ 5.25 · Peace and love for all beings.

ŚLOKA 5.26

PEACE ON ALL SIDES

“For the self-controlled ascetics freed from desire and anger, whose minds are disciplined and who know the Self, the peace of Brahman is near on all sides.”
WordMeans
kāma-krodha-viyuktānāmfree from desire and anger
yatīnām yata-cetasāmof ascetics with controlled minds

ŚLOKA 5.26 · DEEP DIVE

PEACE ON ALL SIDES

🔎 In context

For such people, peace is all around, now and after death.

📜 Looking deeper

Abhitaḥ, on both sides: here and hereafter. Commentators say the knower is surrounded by peace in every direction. Abhitaḥ, “on all sides” or “near”, is read by commentators as both here and hereafter: such people live in peace and die in peace. Śaṅkara says liberation surrounds them in life and after death. Rāmānuja says the vision of the self is near for them. The verse repeats the freedom from desire and anger that runs through the Gītā (2.56, 3.37, 4.10, 5.23, 5.28, 16.21). It also sets the stage for the meditation preview of 5.27–28 and the full teaching on meditation in chapter 6. Commentators note yatīnām, “of ascetics”: those who strive. Madhusūdana says peace surrounds them in life and after death. The verse closes the description of the free person before the brief teaching on meditation.

🌱 For today

Peace is not far away. It is close, when desire and anger are quiet. Peace is closer than it seems. Quiet the inner noise of wanting and resentment, and it surrounds you.

↑ 5.26 · Peace is near on all sides.

ŚLOKA 5.27

A FIRST GLIMPSE OF MEDITATION

“Shutting out outer contacts, fixing the gaze between the eyebrows, making even the in-breath and out-breath moving within the nostrils,”

ŚLOKA 5.27 · DEEP DIVE

A FIRST GLIMPSE OF MEDITATION

🔎 In context

A preview of chapter 6’s meditation.

📜 Looking deeper

Three steps: withdraw from outer contact, focus the gaze, even the breath. Commentators link this to Patañjali’s pratyāhāra, dhāraṇā and prāṇāyāma. The verse continues into the next. Verses 5.27–28 preview the meditation of chapter 6 in miniature. Commentators note three steps. First, pratyāhāra, withdrawing the senses from outer contacts. Second, dhāraṇā, fixing attention, here at the point between the eyebrows, a classic focus in yoga traditions. Third, prāṇāyāma, evening the breath. Śaṅkara explains that the gaze is held there to prevent the eyes from wandering or falling asleep. Rāmānuja reads it as the posture of meditation on the self. The same focus point appears in 8.10, where the dying yogi fixes the breath between the eyebrows. Commentators note that the gaze between the eyebrows prevents both sleep and distraction. Madhusūdana says the breath, made even, calms the mind. Modern breathing practices often aim at the same balance of in-breath and out-breath.

🌱 For today

Even a minute of closed eyes and even breathing can reset the mind. Sit for one minute: eyes softly closed, attention between the brows, breath slow and even. That is a real meditation.

↑ 5.27 · Close the senses, even the breath.

ŚLOKA 5.28

EVER FREE
yatendriyamanobuddhirmunirmokṣaparāyaṇaḥvigatecchābhayakrodho yaḥ sadā mukta eva saḥ ‖28‖
“…with senses, mind and intellect controlled, intent on freedom, free from desire, fear and anger: such a sage is free forever.”
WordMeans
yata-indriya-manaḥ-buddhiḥsenses, mind, intellect controlled
muniḥ mokṣa-parāyaṇaḥthe sage intent on freedom
vigata-icchā-bhaya-krodhaḥfree of desire, fear, anger

Free of desire, fear and anger: free forever.

ŚLOKA 5.28 · DEEP DIVE

EVER FREE

🔎 In context

The meditator who has quieted desire, fear and anger is already free.

📜 Looking deeper

The triad desire-fear-anger again (2.56, 4.10). Commentators: freedom is not a future reward; it is this state. Sadā mukta eva, “ever free indeed”: commentators note the strong statement. Freedom is not a future prize but the present state of such a person. Śaṅkara reads this as liberation while living. Rāmānuja says such a person is as good as liberated. The triad icchā, bhaya, krodha, desire, fear and anger, appears once again. The order is the ladder of chapter 3 in reverse: senses, mind, intellect controlled; desire, fear and anger gone; freedom here. The full method follows in chapter 6. Commentators note mokṣa-parāyaṇa, “intent on freedom”: the meditator has one aim. Madhusūdana says such a one is free even before death. The verse prepares chapter 6’s full teaching on meditation.

🌱 For today

Each time fear or anger fades, you taste freedom. Each time desire, fear or anger fades, notice the lightness. That lightness is freedom, already tasted.

↑ 5.28 · Free of desire, fear and anger: free forever.

ŚLOKA 5.29

KNOW ME AS FRIEND
bhoktāraṃ yajñatapasāṃ sarvalokamaheśvaramsuhṛdaṃ sarvabhūtānāṃ jñātvā māṃ śāntimṛcchati ‖29‖
“Knowing me as the enjoyer of sacrifices and austerities, the great Lord of all worlds, the friend of all beings, one attains peace.”
WordMeans
bhoktāram yajña-tapasāmthe enjoyer of sacrifices and austerities
sarva-loka-maheśvaramgreat Lord of all worlds
suhṛdam sarva-bhūtānāmfriend of all beings

ŚLOKA 5.29 · DEEP DIVE

KNOW ME AS FRIEND

🔎 In context

The chapter ends with God as friend.

📜 Looking deeper

Suhṛt, good-hearted friend. Commentators note the shift: after all the discipline, peace comes from knowing the Lord as one who loves all beings. Many devotees hold this verse dear. Three titles of the Lord: enjoyer of all offerings, Lord of all worlds, friend of all beings. Commentators find the third the most moving. Suhṛt means a good-hearted friend, one who helps without expecting return. Rāmānuja says knowing the Lord as such a friend brings complete trust, and trust brings peace. Śaṅkara says the Lord is the inner Self of all, the source of all good. Madhva stresses the Lord’s sovereignty and kindness together. Chapter 5 ends not with discipline but with relationship. The colophon names it Karma-Sannyāsa Yoga. Commentators note that this is the first verse in the Gītā where Kṛṣṇa calls himself the friend of all beings. Madhusūdana says knowing the Lord as friend removes fear and brings peace. Many devotees recite this verse as a daily reminder that they are never alone.

🌱 For today

The Highest is not distant or harsh but a friend. That knowledge alone brings peace. The power behind the universe is, in this view, a friend. Let that thought soften whatever you are facing today.

↑ 5.29 · The Lord is the friend of all beings.

CHAPTER 5 ON ONE PAGE

Chapter 5 in five lines
The question (5.1–2). Renounce or act? Both lead to freedom; acting is better for Arjuna.
Inner renunciation (5.3–6). One who neither hates nor craves is a true renunciant; knowledge and yoga are one.
The witness (5.7–13). The knower sees the senses acting; like a lotus leaf, he is untouched; he rests in the city of nine gates.
Equal vision (5.14–20). Ignorance covers knowledge; the wise see the same Self in a scholar, a cow, a dog and an outcaste.
Peace (5.21–29). Joy and light within; withstand desire and anger; know the Lord as friend of all, and find peace.
