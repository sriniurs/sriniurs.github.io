WHERE WE ARE

In Chapter 3 Kṛṣṇa taught karma yoga: act, as an offering, for the world, and slay desire.

Chapter 4 reveals who is teaching. Kṛṣṇa says he gave this yoga to the Sun long ago, and that he takes birth age after age to restore dharma. Then he shows how action and knowledge join: in the right spirit every act is a sacrifice, and knowledge burns karma to ash.

AN ANCIENT YOGA

ŚLOKA 4.1 · DEEP DIVE

AN ANCIENT YOGA

🔎 In context

Kṛṣṇa reveals that the Gītā’s yoga is ancient. He taught it first to Vivasvān, the Sun; the Sun to Manu, father of humanity; Manu to Ikṣvāku, first king of the solar dynasty. Chapter 4 is named Jñāna-Karma-Sannyāsa Yoga, the yoga of knowledge and the renunciation of action. Vivasvān, the Sun god, is the father of Vaivasvata Manu, the Manu of our present age. Ikṣvāku, Manu’s son, founded the solar dynasty (Sūrya-vaṃśa) that later produced Rāma.

📜 Looking deeper

Ikṣvāku’s line is the dynasty of Rāma. The teaching thus begins with kings, not hermits: it is a yoga for people in the world. Commentators note the word avyaya, imperishable: the yoga does not change, though it may be forgotten. Commentators note several points. The yoga is called avyaya, imperishable, because its fruit, liberation, does not decay. The line of transmission passes through kings, to show that karma yoga suits rulers and householders, not only monks. Śaṅkara says the Lord teaches it to kṣatriyas so that they can protect the world with strength rooted in wisdom. Rāmānuja calls this the Lord revealing his eternal teaching role. Some modern readers link the Sun to the light of knowledge: the teaching begins with light itself. Commentators note that the line of teachers runs Kṛṣṇa, Vivasvān, Manu, Ikṣvāku: from the divine to the sun, to the first lawgiver, to the first king. Madhusūdana says this shows the teaching is meant for rulers and householders. Kālidāsa’s poem Raghuvaṃśa celebrates the kings of this solar line, praising them for ruling with self-control.

🌱 For today

Old wisdom is not out of date. The same truths are found again by each generation. The best wisdom is often old and has been tested by many generations. Look for teachings that have lasted.

WordMeans

ŚLOKA 4.2 · DEEP DIVE

HANDED DOWN, THEN LOST

🔎 In context

The teaching passed down through a line of royal sages, then faded over time.

📜 Looking deeper

Paramparā, an unbroken line of teachers, is how Indian traditions preserve knowledge. Commentators note that wisdom can be lost when it falls into the hands of the unworthy or simply through neglect. Rājarṣi, royal sage, is a ruler who is also a seer, like Janaka (3.20). Commentators explain why the teaching was lost. Śaṅkara says it fell into the hands of those weak in self-control, who could not keep it. Rāmānuja says people lost the ability to understand it. Madhva says the knowledge was hidden in times of decline. Whatever the reason, the verse admits something important: even true teaching can fade. That is why the Lord renews it (4.7–8). Indian traditions place great weight on paramparā, the living chain of teachers, for this reason. Commentators note mahatā kālena, “by long time”: even true teachings can fade over generations. Many reform movements in Indian history, from the Bhakti saints to modern teachers, saw their work as restoring a lost understanding. The Gītā itself presents Kṛṣṇa as doing this.

🌱 For today

Wisdom needs living carriers. Pass on what you have learned. If you have learned something valuable, teach it. A chain is only as strong as each link.

↑ 4.2 · Wisdom passes down, and can be lost.

ŚLOKA 4.3

BECAUSE YOU ARE MY FRIEND
sa evāyaṃ mayā te’dya yogaḥ proktaḥ purātanaḥbhakto’si me sakhā ceti rahasyaṃ hyetaduttamam ‖3‖
“That same ancient yoga I have told you today, because you are my devotee and my friend. This is the supreme secret.”
WordMeans

ŚLOKA 4.3 · DEEP DIVE

BECAUSE YOU ARE MY FRIEND

🔎 In context

Why does Arjuna receive it? Because he is devotee and friend.

📜 Looking deeper

The teaching needs a relationship. Commentators, especially Rāmānuja’s school, stress bhakta and sakhā: love and friendship open the door to the highest teaching. “Secret” does not mean hidden by rule, but rare and precious. Commentators find this one of the Gītā’s tenderest verses. Arjuna receives the teaching not because he is learned or a renunciant, but because he is bhakta, devoted, and sakhā, a friend. Rāmānuja’s school treasures sakhā: the Lord’s friendship is his grace in human form. Śaṅkara says “secret” means it is not for everyone, only for those ready. In 18.64, near the end, Kṛṣṇa will repeat: “you are dearly loved by me, so I tell you this for your good”. The Gītā is framed as a conversation between friends. Commentators note purātanaḥ, “ancient”: the teaching is old, yet given anew. Madhusūdana says the Lord gives it to Arjuna because of his devotion and friendship. Many teachers say spiritual knowledge is passed on through relationship, not only through books.

🌱 For today

We learn best from those we trust and who care for us. Relationship comes before teaching. We open up most to those we trust. If you want to learn deeply, build relationships of trust.

↑ 4.3 · Given to a devotee and friend.

ŚLOKA 4.4 · DEEP DIVE

“HOW COULD YOU HAVE?”

🔎 In context

Arjuna raises a logical problem: Kṛṣṇa is his cousin, born recently. How could he have taught the Sun?

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators say Arjuna asks so that the world may learn of the avatāra. His question leads to the most famous verses on divine incarnation. This is Arjuna’s fourth question. Commentators note it is a sincere puzzle: Arjuna has known Kṛṣṇa all his life as a cousin, born to Vasudeva and Devakī. Some, like Śaṅkara, suggest Arjuna asks to remove doubts others might have. Rāmānuja says Arjuna knows Kṛṣṇa is divine but wants to understand how the eternal Lord takes birth. The question is the doorway to one of the most important teachings in Hinduism: the avatāra. Good questions, the Gītā shows, unlock deep answers. Commentators note that Arjuna’s question is about time: how can one born recently have taught in ancient times? Madhusūdana says Arjuna knows Kṛṣṇa is divine but wants it explained for others. The question opens the Gītā’s teaching on divine incarnation.

🌱 For today

Good questions bring out deep answers. Do not be shy to ask the obvious. If something does not add up, say so. Honest puzzlement leads to understanding.

↑ 4.4 · A simple question opens a great answer.

ŚLOKA 4.5

MANY BIRTHS

ŚLOKA 4.5 · DEEP DIVE

MANY BIRTHS

🔎 In context

Kṛṣṇa answers: we have both been born many times. The difference is memory.

📜 Looking deeper

This affirms rebirth for both. Commentators note the contrast: the soul forgets past lives; the Lord remembers, because his births are not forced by karma but freely chosen. The verse confirms two kinds of birth: Arjuna’s, driven by karma, and Kṛṣṇa’s, by choice. Commentators note that Arjuna and Kṛṣṇa are often identified with the ancient sages Nara and Nārāyaṇa, who practised austerity together at Badarī. The Mahābhārata mentions this link several times. Śaṅkara says the Lord remembers because his knowledge is never covered. Rāmānuja stresses that the Lord’s births are real, not illusory. Most of us forget our past lives; the Gītā says this forgetting is part of being bound. Commentators note parantapa, “scorcher of foes”: Kṛṣṇa reminds Arjuna of his strength even as he points to his ignorance. Many Indian traditions teach that we have lived many lives; children who seem to remember past lives are a subject of folk tales and some modern research.

🌱 For today

We are older than this life, says the Gītā. Each life is a chapter, not the whole book. We are part of a longer story than we can remember. That can make us humbler and more patient with ourselves.

↑ 4.5 · We have all lived many times; the Lord remembers.

ŚLOKA 4.6

BY MY OWN POWER
ajo’pi sannavyayātmā bhūtānāmīśvaro’pi sanprakṛtiṃ svāmadhiṣṭhāya saṃbhavāmyātmamāyayā ‖6‖
“Though I am unborn and of imperishable nature, and Lord of all beings, governing my own nature, I come into being by my own power (māyā).”
WordMeans
ajaḥ api san avyaya-ātmāthough unborn, imperishable
bhūtānām īśvaraḥ api santhough Lord of beings
prakṛtim svām adhiṣṭhāyagoverning my own nature

ŚLOKA 4.6 · DEEP DIVE

BY MY OWN POWER

🔎 In context

How does the Unborn take birth? By his own power, not by karma.

📜 Looking deeper

Māyā here is read differently: Śaṅkara as the Lord’s power of appearance; Rāmānuja as his own will and wonderful power, his body being divine and real. All agree the avatāra is free, not bound. The verse holds two truths together: the Lord is unborn, yet takes birth. Commentators resolve the paradox in different ways. Śaṅkara: the birth is an appearance through the Lord’s māyā, his power, without any change in his real nature. Rāmānuja: the Lord takes a real, divine body made of pure sattva (śuddha-sattva), by his own will; māyā here means his wonderful knowledge and will. Madhva: the Lord’s body is not material at all; it is his own form of knowledge and bliss. All three agree that the avatāra is not bound by karma. Commentators note prakṛtiṃ svām adhiṣṭhāya, “governing my own nature”: the Lord is master of nature, not its servant. Madhusūdana says the Lord’s birth is like a magician’s appearance: real to the viewers, but the magician is not bound by it. Rāmānuja insists the Lord’s body is real and divine.

🌱 For today

The Divine enters the world by choice, out of care. That is the root of the avatāra idea. The Divine is not distant. In this view it chooses to come close, out of care for the world.

↑ 4.6 · The Unborn is born by his own will.

ŚLOKA 4.7

“WHENEVER DHARMA DECLINES”
yadā yadā hi dharmasya glānirbhavati bhārataabhyutthānamadharmasya tadā’tmānaṃ sṛjāmyaham ‖7‖
“Whenever there is a decline of dharma, Bhārata, and a rise of adharma, then I bring myself forth.”

ŚLOKA 4.7 · DEEP DIVE

“WHENEVER DHARMA DECLINES”

🔎 In context

The avatāra verse, among the best-known lines in Hinduism.

📜 Looking deeper

The Daśāvatāra tradition (see our Daśāvatāra series) grows from this idea. Commentators note yadā yadā, “whenever”: not once, but again and again, as needed. This verse and the next are perhaps the most widely known in Hinduism, often printed together and chanted. Glāni means fading, weakening, as a plant wilts. Commentators note that the Lord does not wait for dharma to vanish entirely; he comes when it declines. The Purāṇas list many avatāras; the classic ten (Daśāvatāra) include Matsya, Kūrma, Varāha, Narasiṃha, Vāmana, Paraśurāma, Rāma, Balarāma or Buddha (lists differ), Kṛṣṇa and Kalki. The Bhāgavata Purāṇa (1.3) lists twenty-two. Some teachers also read the verse inwardly: whenever our inner dharma weakens, the Divine rises within as conscience. Commentators note that the verse is often recited at times of social crisis as a promise that things will be set right. Many reformers and saints in Indian history have been seen by their followers as partial manifestations of the Divine. The verse is also inscribed in many temples and public buildings.

🌱 For today

No darkness is final. Hope is built into the Gītā. When things seem to be falling apart, remember: the story does not end there. Help comes, often through ordinary people.

↑ 4.7 · When dharma falls, the Lord comes.

ŚLOKA 4.8 · DEEP DIVE

“AGE AFTER AGE”

🔎 In context

Three purposes of the avatāra: protect, destroy evil, establish dharma.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators stress the first purpose: protection of the good, including their inner protection, comfort and guidance. Destruction of evil serves that. The refrain sambhavāmi yuge yuge is chanted everywhere. Commentators often ask: does the all-powerful Lord need to take birth to destroy the wicked? He could do it by will alone. Rāmānuja’s answer is famous: the main reason is paritrāṇa, to be near his devotees, who long to see him. Destroying evil is secondary. Madhusūdana says the Lord comes to show the path by example. Śaṅkara says he comes to protect the Vedic dharma that guides people to freedom. A yuga is an age; the four yugas are Satya, Tretā, Dvāpara and Kali. Kṛṣṇa came at the end of the Dvāpara. Commentators note yuge yuge, “age after age”: the Lord’s coming is not once but repeated. Madhusūdana says each age has its own form of help. Some modern teachers read the verse more broadly: whenever good people stand up for justice, the divine purpose is at work through them.

🌱 For today

Whatever we do to protect the good and stand for right shares in this divine work. Protecting good people is the first purpose here. Defending the vulnerable is part of that divine work, whoever does it.

KNOWING MY BIRTH FREES

🔎 In context

Understanding the avatāra truly is itself liberating.

📜 Looking deeper

Why? Commentators say knowing that the Lord takes birth freely, out of love, changes the knower’s heart: it draws him to the Lord and frees him from fear. Bhakti traditions treasure this verse. Divya, divine, marks the difference from ordinary births and deeds: they are free, pure, and for the world’s good. Commentators find a surprising promise here. Simply knowing the avatāra truly brings liberation. Why? Rāmānuja says such knowledge produces love, and love leads to the Lord. Śaṅkara says knowing the Lord as unborn and actionless is knowing the Self. This verse is the basis for the great love of the līlā stories in bhakti traditions. Hearing and singing of Kṛṣṇa’s childhood, Rāma’s life and Narasiṃha’s protection become a path in themselves. Commentators note tattvataḥ, “truly”: knowing the avatāra in truth, not just as a story. Madhusūdana says one who truly knows the Lord’s birth knows that he is free, and that knowledge itself frees. The festivals of Janmāṣṭamī and Rāma Navamī celebrate the divine births this verse praises.

🌱 For today

Learning the stories of the Divine is not just entertainment; it can transform us. Stories of the Divine are not just tales. Hearing them with understanding can change the heart.

MANY HAVE COME

Kṛṣṇa assures Arjuna that many have walked this path before.

The triad passion-fear-anger returns from 2.56. Commentators note the path: freedom from these, absorption, refuge, purification. It is not one person’s secret; “many” have reached it. You are not alone on the path. Many have walked it, and their lives show it can be done. Many before you have overcome fear, anger and craving. Their lives are proof that it can be done.

ŚLOKA 4.11 · DEEP DIVE

AS THEY APPROACH ME

🔎 In context

God meets each person in the way they come.

📜 Looking deeper

Many read this as the Gītā’s broad-mindedness: sincere seekers on many roads arrive at the same goal. Others read it more narrowly: the Lord rewards each according to their approach. Traditions differ on how far it extends. This verse is often quoted as the Gītā’s statement of religious openness. Ramakrishna and Vivekananda drew on it, and on the Ṛg Vedic line ekaṃ sad viprā bahudhā vadanti, “truth is one, the wise call it by many names” (1.164.46). Commentators read it in different ways. Śaṅkara: the Lord gives each the fruit they seek; those seeking liberation get liberation. Rāmānuja: the Lord responds to each devotee in the form and manner they love. Some traditional teachers read it more narrowly: all paths are under the Lord’s rule, but not all reach the same goal. The verse keeps both readings open. Vivekananda quoted this verse in his opening address at the Parliament of the World’s Religions in Chicago in 1893, together with a hymn that says: as rivers from different sources mingle in the sea, so different paths lead to the Lord. The verse became a symbol of Hindu openness.

🌱 For today

Respect other people’s sincere paths. The same Light may be seen through many windows. Respect the sincere seeking of others, even when their path looks different from yours.

↑ 4.11 · The Lord meets you as you come.

ŚLOKA 4.12 · DEEP DIVE

QUICK RESULTS

🔎 In context

Many people worship for quick worldly results, and get them.

📜 Looking deeper

This is not condemned, only noted. Commentators say such worship is valid but limited; the higher path aims at freedom, not just success. Chapter 7 (7.20–23) develops the theme. Commentators note that the Gītā does not forbid praying for success. It observes that such prayers work in their own way, because the world of action brings results quickly. But the results are limited and pass. Chapter 7 (7.20–23) explains that those who worship other deities for wishes get them, but such results are finite; those who worship the Lord reach him. Chapter 9 (9.23) adds that even those who worship other gods are, in truth, worshipping the Lord, though not in the right way. The Gītā is generous but clear about higher and lower aims. Commentators note kṣipram, “quickly”: rewards of action come fast in the human world. Madhusūdana says this is why people prefer ritual for results: it works. The Gītā does not deny this but points to a higher aim.

🌱 For today

Praying for success is human. The Gītā invites us to also seek something more lasting. It is natural to ask for help. Let your prayers also include something that lasts beyond this year’s success.

ŚLOKA 4.13 · DEEP DIVE

FOUR ORDERS, BY QUALITY AND WORK

🔎 In context

A much-debated verse on the four varṇas.

📜 Looking deeper

The Gītā says varṇa follows guṇa and karma, quality and work; it does not mention birth here. Many modern teachers read it as a description of temperaments, not a rigid caste system; many traditional commentators tied it to birth. We give both views; the debate continues. The four varṇas are Brāhmaṇa (teachers and priests), Kṣatriya (rulers and protectors), Vaiśya (traders and farmers) and Śūdra (workers and servers). Chapter 18 (18.41–44) describes each by its natural qualities. The meaning of this verse is one of the most argued questions in Hindu thought. Traditional commentators, including Śaṅkara and Rāmānuja, generally took varṇa as fixed by birth, shaped by past karma. Many modern teachers, including Vivekananda, Aurobindo and Gandhi in different ways, emphasised guṇa and karma, quality and work, and rejected birth-based discrimination. Dr. B. R. Ambedkar was sharply critical of the varṇa system and of this verse. We present these views without taking sides. Commentators note that the second half of the verse is often overlooked: the Lord is the maker of the order, yet the non-doer. Madhusūdana says the Lord creates without attachment, as the Gītā asks us to act. The debate about varṇa continues, but the teaching on detached action is clear.

🌱 For today

Each person has a nature and gifts. Finding work that fits them is part of a good life. Whatever one’s view of the old system, the verse invites us to honour each person’s nature and gifts, and the work that fits them.

ACTIONS DO NOT STAIN ME

The Lord acts without craving, so action leaves no stain.

The model to copy. Commentators say: knowing how the Lord acts teaches us how to act. The lotus-leaf image appears in 5.10. Commentators explain why the Lord is not stained: he acts with no ego and no craving. Limpanti, “smear” or “stain”, comes from lip, to anoint. Commentators note that the verse gives a practical way to freedom: know how the Lord acts, and act likewise. Madhusūdana says knowing that the Lord is untouched by action frees one from the fear of action. Many find this verse comforting when facing hard duties.

Act like the lotus leaf: in the water, not wet by it. Act without the glue of craving, and the stains of action will not stick.

THE ANCIENTS DID SO

Those who sought freedom long ago did not stop acting. Neither should Arjuna.

Mumukṣu, one who longs for freedom. Commentators say: even such seekers worked, because action in the right spirit purifies. Mumukṣu, one longing for freedom, is a technical term in Vedānta. Follow the example of the wise who came before. If you long for freedom, keep working. The way out is through, not around.

ŚLOKA 4.16 · DEEP DIVE

WHAT IS ACTION?

🔎 In context

Kṛṣṇa admits the question is subtle: even sages get confused.

📜 Looking deeper

The humility is striking. Defining action is not simple, because what looks like action may not bind and what looks like rest may. The next verses unfold the riddle. Kavi means a poet but also a seer, a wise one. Commentators praise the humility of admitting that even the wise struggle with this question. The next verses (4.17–23) give a careful analysis, and many consider them among the most philosophical in the Gītā. Śaṅkara’s commentary on these verses is long and detailed. He uses them to argue that the Self is actionless and the knower is free of action even while the body acts. Rāmānuja reads them as teaching that action done with knowledge of the self is really a form of knowledge. Commentators note mokṣyase aśubhāt, “you will be freed from ill”: understanding action is itself freeing. Madhusūdana says confusion about action causes most moral suffering. Clear thinking about what to do is a spiritual practice.

🌱 For today

Simple-looking questions can be deep. Be patient with them. Simple-sounding questions can be the deepest. Don’t rush to an answer; let the question open.

ŚLOKA 4.17 · DEEP DIVE

THE WAY OF ACTION IS DEEP

🔎 In context

Three things to understand: right action, wrong action (vikarma), and inaction.

📜 Looking deeper

Gahanā karmaṇo gatiḥ, “deep is the way of karma”, has become a proverb in Indian languages. Commentators say each act must be judged by motive, context and knowledge, not by appearance. Vikarma is read in different ways. Śaṅkara takes it as forbidden action. Others read it as special or varied action, or as action that is “against the grain”. Gahanā karmaṇo gatiḥ is a common saying in many Indian languages, used when things turn out in unexpected ways. Commentators explain that action is deep because the same outer act can bind or free, be right or wrong, depending on intention, knowledge and circumstance. A surgeon’s knife and a robber’s knife both cut; the act is not the whole story. Commentators note boddhavyam, “must be understood”, repeated three times: action, wrong action and inaction all need study. Madhusūdana says each has its own consequences. Courts of law also examine intention, circumstance and outcome, not just the act.

🌱 For today

Before judging an action, yours or another’s, look deeper. Appearances mislead. Judge actions, including your own, by intention and context, not just by how they look.

ŚLOKA 4.18 · DEEP DIVE

ACTION IN INACTION

🔎 In context

The Gītā’s riddle.

📜 Looking deeper

One reading: a sage acting without ego is really “not doing”; a lazy person idle but craving is really “doing” and binding himself. Śaṅkara reads it as seeing the actionless Self in all activity. Either way, the inside counts. This is one of the Gītā’s great riddles. Śaṅkara’s reading: the Self never acts; the body, mind and senses act. One who sees “inaction” (the actionless Self) in all action, and sees that even sitting idle the body and mind are “acting”, is wise. Rāmānuja’s reading: action done with knowledge of the self is really a form of knowledge (“inaction”); and knowledge itself is a kind of action. Many modern readers take a practical view: a selfless worker is “not doing” in the binding sense, while an idle person full of desire is “doing” a great deal. Commentators note kṛtsna-karma-kṛt, “one who does all actions”: the wise one is fully active. Madhusūdana says he does everything without the sense of doership. Some modern readers compare this to the state athletes and artists call flow, when action happens without self-consciousness.

🌱 For today

Check the inside of your actions: where is the ego doing, and where is it at rest? Look beneath the surface of busyness and stillness. True rest can happen while working; restless craving can happen while sitting still.

BURNT IN THE FIRE OF KNOWLEDGE

A wise person’s actions leave no karmic seed, as if roasted in fire.

A roasted seed cannot sprout. Commentators use this image: action burnt by knowledge cannot produce rebirth. Saṅkalpa, the scheming intention behind desire, is also dropped. Act without inner scheming for reward, and actions become light. Plan, yes; scheme, no. Act from clarity rather than from hidden wants, and your actions grow lighter.

DOING, YET NOT DOING

A free worker: fully active, yet inwardly not “doing” in the binding sense.

📜 Looking deeper

Nirāśraya, depending on nothing: not leaning on results or praise for happiness. Commentators say such a person’s work is pure offering. This verse describes the inner state of the free worker. Nitya-tṛpta, ever satisfied; nirāśraya, without support, meaning not leaning on anything outside for happiness or security. Commentators note the paradox at the end: fully engaged, yet doing nothing. Śaṅkara says this person, if he cannot renounce outwardly, still acts without any sense of doership. Rāmānuja says such action is really the practice of knowledge. Commentators note nirāśraya, “depending on nothing”: the free person does not lean on outer supports for happiness. Madhusūdana says such a person still acts, but freely. Work fully and need nothing from it. That is freedom in action. Work done from contentment, not need, feels very different. Try one task today from that place.

ŚLOKA 4.21 · DEEP DIVE

ONLY THE BODY ACTS

🔎 In context

Such a person acts only to keep the body going, without hoarding.

📜 Looking deeper

Śaṅkara reads this verse as describing the renunciant monk, doing only what the body needs. Others apply it to anyone who acts without greed. Parigraha, possessiveness, is the root of much trouble. Parigraha, possessiveness, is the opposite of aparigraha, non-possession, one of Patañjali’s five yamas (Yoga Sūtra 2.30). Commentators read “only bodily action” in two ways. All agree it ends Arjuna’s confusion about “mine” and “theirs”. This verse promises the end of the delusion of chapter 1: Arjuna will no longer see “mine” and “theirs”. Commentators read the vision differently. Śaṅkara: all beings in the Self, and the Self as one with the Lord, a vision of non-duality. Rāmānuja: all beings as like oneself in nature, and all as the body of the Lord. The Īśā Upaniṣad (verse 6) says: one who sees all beings in the Self and the Self in all beings does not hate. The Gītā develops this in 6.29–31. Commentators note na punar moham, “not again delusion”: knowledge, once gained, does not return to confusion. Madhusūdana says Arjuna will see all beings in himself and in the Lord. Śrī Vaiṣṇava teachers say this means seeing all beings as belonging to the Lord, as one’s own family.

🌱 For today

When we see others in ourselves, cruelty becomes impossible and kindness natural. When we see others as part of ourselves, harming them becomes impossible. That is the root of real kindness.

↑ 4.35 · See all beings in the Self.

THE RAFT OF KNOWLEDGE

ŚLOKA 4.36 · DEEP DIVE

THE RAFT OF KNOWLEDGE

🔎 In context

Hope for everyone: no past is too heavy.

📜 Looking deeper

A raft carries anyone who climbs on. Commentators note this is not permission to sin, but assurance that the past need not trap us. Plava, a raft or boat. The image of crossing the ocean of saṃsāra is central in Indian thought; the Lord is often called the boatman. Commentators stress that the verse offers hope, not licence. Śaṅkara says knowledge destroys the very sense of doership on which sin depends. Rāmānuja says the knowledge of the self washes away all past wrong. The bhakti traditions pair this verse with 9.30–31, where even a great wrongdoer who turns to the Lord with undivided devotion becomes righteous. The Gītā consistently refuses to write anyone off. Commentators note pāpa-kṛttamaḥ, “worst of sinners”: even the worst is not beyond hope. Madhusūdana says knowledge burns past sin as fire burns wood. Stories like Vālmīki’s, the robber who became a poet-sage, are often told with this verse.

🌱 For today

No one is beyond help. Begin now. No past is too dark to step out of. The way out begins with understanding and a decision to change.

↑ 4.36 · Knowledge carries even the worst across.

ŚLOKA 4.37

“As a blazing fire turns firewood to ashes, Arjuna, so the fire of knowledge turns all karma to ashes.”

ŚLOKA 4.37 · DEEP DIVE

THE FIRE OF KNOWLEDGE

🔎 In context

Knowledge burns karma.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators distinguish kinds of karma: past karma not yet ripened is burnt; karma already bearing fruit (the present body) runs its course, like an arrow already shot. Then the knower is free. Commentators distinguish three kinds of karma. Sañcita is accumulated karma not yet bearing fruit. Āgāmī is new karma being created. Prārabdha is the karma that has already begun to bear fruit and produced the present body. Knowledge burns the first two. Prārabdha continues until the body falls, as an arrow already shot keeps flying, or a potter’s wheel keeps turning after the push. Śaṅkara explains this in detail. It is why a realised person still lives, eats and ages, yet is free. Commentators note bhasmasāt, “to ashes”: the image is complete destruction. Madhusūdana says even the store of past karma is burned. The image of ash, vibhūti, is sacred in Śaiva practice, where it reminds devotees that all things end and only the Self remains.

🌱 For today

Understanding changes everything. The same past looks different in its light. Understanding can change how we relate to our whole past. What once haunted us can become ash.

↑ 4.37 · Knowledge burns all karma to ash.

ŚLOKA 4.38 · DEEP DIVE

THE PUREST THING

🔎 In context

Knowledge is the greatest purifier, and it is found within, in time.

📜 Looking deeper

Note “in time” and “in himself”: knowledge is not bought; it ripens through yoga. Commentators say practice prepares the ground; knowledge arises by itself. Pavitra, purifier, is used of holy rivers, sacred grass, mantras. Commentators say knowledge outranks them all because it removes the root of impurity, ignorance itself. The phrase kālena, “in time”, is important. Knowledge is not a sudden gift without preparation; it ripens through practice. Svayam and ātmani, “oneself” and “within oneself”, stress that it is discovered inside, not borrowed. Śaṅkara says practice of karma yoga prepares the mind, and knowledge dawns by itself. Rāmānuja agrees that yoga leads to self-knowledge. Commentators note svayam, “oneself”: knowledge is discovered within, not borrowed. Madhusūdana says it comes in its own time to one prepared by yoga. Many teachers say the most important things cannot be taught, only found.

🌱 For today

Be patient. Practice prepares; understanding arrives in its time. Some understanding cannot be rushed. Keep practising; it will arrive when the ground is ready.

↑ 4.38 · Nothing purifies like knowledge.

“The one with faith gains knowledge, devoted to it and master of his senses. Gaining knowledge, he soon reaches supreme peace.”

ŚLOKA 4.39 · DEEP DIVE

FAITH GAINS KNOWLEDGE

🔎 In context

Three conditions: faith, devotion, self-control. The result: knowledge and peace.

📜 Looking deeper

Śraddhāvān labhate jñānam is a beloved line. Faith here is trust that keeps one practising long enough to see for oneself. Śraddhā is a rich word. Śaṅkara defines it as trust in the words of scripture and teacher, based on understanding, not blind belief. The Taittirīya Upaniṣad (1.11.3) says śraddhayā deyam, “give with faith”. Chapter 17 explores three kinds of faith. Commentators note the three conditions here: faith, dedication and self-control. Faith opens the door, dedication keeps one walking, self-control keeps one from straying. The fruit, parā śānti, supreme peace, is the same peace promised in 2.71 and 18.62. Commentators note acireṇa, “soon”: peace follows knowledge quickly. Madhusūdana says faith, dedication and self-control are the three conditions. Each supports the others: faith gives energy, dedication gives focus, self-control gives steadiness.

🌱 For today

Trust the path enough to walk it. Seeing comes on the way. Faith is what keeps us practising long enough to see results. Give a good practice that chance.

↑ 4.39 · The one with faith gains knowledge.

“The ignorant, faithless, doubting self perishes. For the doubter there is neither this world nor the next, nor happiness.”

ŚLOKA 4.40 · DEEP DIVE

THE DOUBTER

🔎 In context

Chronic doubt destroys.

📜 Looking deeper

This is not against questioning, which the Gītā welcomes (4.34). It is against the paralysed mind that never commits to anything, and so gains nothing in this world or the next. Saṃśayātmā, “one whose very self is doubt”, describes a person paralysed by indecision. Commentators distinguish this from healthy questioning. Arjuna himself asks many questions; Kṛṣṇa praises inquiry in 4.34. The problem is doubt that never resolves, that refuses every answer. Śaṅkara says such a person loses this world (no success) and the next (no merit). Many readers find the verse psychologically sharp: chronic doubt makes commitment, and therefore happiness, impossible. Commentators note that the verse names three faults: ignorance, lack of faith and chronic doubt. Madhusūdana says the doubter is worst because he cannot even begin. Teachers often say: begin with what you can trust, and let experience test it.

🌱 For today

Question, then decide. Endless doubt is a trap. Doubt is useful when it leads somewhere. When it only circles, choose, act, and learn from the result.

↑ 4.40 · Endless doubt destroys.

ŚLOKA 4.41 · DEEP DIVE

NO BONDS FOR THE ONE WHO KNOWS

🔎 In context

Yoga, knowledge and self-mastery together free one from action’s bonds.

📜 Looking deeper

A summary of the chapter: action renounced through yoga (offered), doubt cut by knowledge, and self-possession. Commentators see karma yoga and jñāna yoga joining here. Commentators see this verse as joining the two threads of chapter 4: yoga (offering action) and knowledge (cutting doubt). Ātmavān, self-possessed, means steady in oneself. Śaṅkara reads “renounced through yoga” as renouncing the sense of doership. Rāmānuja reads it as offering all actions to the Lord. Either way, action without ego, guided by clear knowledge, does not bind. The chapter’s title, the yoga of knowledge and the renunciation of action, is summed up here. Commentators note ātmavantam, “self-possessed”: the one who has gathered himself. Madhusūdana says such a person is not bound by action. The verse sums up chapter 4: act with offering, see with knowledge, stay centred.

🌱 For today

Offer the work, clear the doubt, steady the self. Then work is light. Offer the work, clear the doubt, stay centred. That is the whole chapter in three steps.

CUT DOUBT, STAND UP

🔎 In context

The chapter ends with a sword and a command.

📜 Looking deeper

The sword cuts doubt, not people. The final word, uttiṣṭha, “stand up”, echoes 2.3. Commentators note the pattern: every chapter of teaching returns to the call to act. Jñānāsi, the sword of knowledge, is a vivid image. Commentators note that the doubt is hṛt-stha, seated in the heart, not just in the head: Arjuna’s confusion is emotional as well as intellectual. The double command ātiṣṭha (resort to yoga) and uttiṣṭha (stand up) ends the chapter with energy. Śaṅkara says the doubt is about whether to act or renounce. Rāmānuja says it is about the nature of the self. Chapter 5 will address the doubt between renunciation and action directly. The colophon calls chapter 4 Jñāna-Karma-Sannyāsa Yoga. Commentators note bhārata, “descendant of Bharata”: Kṛṣṇa ends by reminding Arjuna of his royal lineage. Madhusūdana says the sword of knowledge must be taken up and used. Chapter 5 will continue the discussion of renunciation and action.

🌱 For today

Cut through doubt and act. Understanding is meant to be lived. Some doubts live in the heart, not the head. Cut them with clear understanding, and then act.

CHAPTER 4 ON ONE PAGE

Chapter 4 in five lines
The lineage (4.1–5). Kṛṣṇa taught this yoga to the Sun; it passed down and was lost; he gives it again to Arjuna.
The avatāra (4.6–10). The Unborn comes age after age to protect the good and restore dharma.
How he works (4.11–15). He meets people as they come; he made the order of guṇa and karma; action does not stain him.
Action and inaction (4.16–32). See action in inaction; offer everything as Brahman; many disciplines are yajña.
Knowledge (4.33–42). Learn from seers by humility, questions and service; knowledge burns karma; cut doubt and stand up.
