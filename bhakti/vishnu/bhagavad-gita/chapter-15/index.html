The upside-down tree · every śloka explained
HOW THIS WORKS: The whole chapter in one go for chanting, then every śloka on two pages: first the chant, a picture, what it says and the key words; then a deep dive into its context, how the great teachers read it, and what it means for us today.

WHERE WE ARE

In Chapter 14 Kṛṣṇa explained the three guṇas and how devotion carries one beyond them.

Chapter 15 is short and deep, and many families recite it before meals. It pictures the world as an upside-down tree with roots in the Supreme, to be cut with detachment. Each soul is an eternal fragment of the Lord; he lights the sun, nourishes plants and digests our food; and he is Puruṣottama, beyond the perishable and the imperishable.

ŚLOKA 15.1

THE UPSIDE-DOWN TREE
śrībhagavān uvācaūrdhvamūlamadhaḥśākhamaśvatthaṃ prāhuravyayamchandāṃsi yasya parṇāni yastaṃ veda sa vedavit ‖1‖
The Lord said: They speak of the imperishable aśvattha tree, with roots above and branches below, whose leaves are the Vedic hymns. One who knows it knows the Veda.
WordMeans
ūrdhva-mūlam adhaḥ-śākhamroots above, branches below
aśvattham prāhuḥ avyayamthey call the aśvattha imperishable

ŚLOKA 15.1 · DEEP DIVE

THE UPSIDE-DOWN TREE

🔎 In context

The chapter opens with a striking image: the world as an upside-down tree. Chapter 15 is named Puruṣottama Yoga, the yoga of the Supreme Person. It is only 20 verses long, and is traditionally chanted before meals in many homes and monasteries, especially in South India and among ISKCON devotees.

📜 Looking deeper

The image comes from the Kaṭha Upaniṣad (2.3.1). Roots above: the world springs from the Supreme. Branches below: it spreads into our many experiences. The leaves, Vedic hymns, protect and nourish it as leaves protect a tree. Aśvattha may play on a-śvas-tha, “not lasting till tomorrow”: ever-changing, yet endless as a cycle. The Kaṭha Upaniṣad (2.3.1) says: ūrdhva-mūlo’vāk-śākha eṣo’śvatthaḥ sanātanaḥ. Śaṅkara explains aśvattha as “that which will not last until tomorrow” (a-śvaḥ-stha): the world is ever changing, though the process is endless. The peepal tree has a long sacred history in India. Images of it appear on seals of the Indus valley civilisation. The Buddha was awakened under one at Bodh Gayā. Women in many villages still tie threads around it and walk around it in prayer. Commentators say the Gītā takes this familiar tree and turns it upside down to show that the world’s roots are in the Supreme, not in the soil.

🌱 For today

Our lives branch out in many directions. Remember where the roots are. Our lives branch out in many directions. Remember where the roots are.

↑ 15.1 · Roots above, branches below.

ŚLOKA 15.2

BRANCHES AND BUDS
adhaścordhvaṃ prasṛtāstasya śākhāguṇapravṛddhā viṣayapravālāḥadhaśca mūlānyanusantatānikarmānubandhīni manuṣyaloke ‖2‖
“Its branches spread below and above, nourished by the guṇas, with sense-objects as buds; and its roots stretch downward too, binding to action in the human world.”
WordMeans
adhaḥ ca ūrdhvam prasṛtāḥ tasya śākhāḥits branches spread below and above
guṇa-pravṛddhāḥ viṣaya-pravālāḥfed by the guṇas, objects as buds

ŚLOKA 15.2 · DEEP DIVE

BRANCHES AND BUDS

🔎 In context

The tree grows by the guṇas; its buds are sense objects; secondary roots bind us to action.

📜 Looking deeper

Like a banyan, the tree sends roots down from its branches, holding it ever more firmly. Commentators read these as desires and habits created by action in the human world, which keep the tree growing. The verse is in triṣṭubh metre. The aśvattha is a fig, but commentators also use the banyan, whose aerial roots drop from branches and become new trunks. The secondary roots are vāsanās, latent desires and impressions, created by action. Śaṅkara says the higher branches are the worlds of gods, the lower ones animals. Rāmānuja says the human world is special, because here new karma is made. Commentators note the word karmānubandhīni, “binding to action”: the roots grow in the human world because only humans create new karma by choice. Madhusūdana says the tree grows by the water of desire. A banyan can grow so large that one tree becomes a forest; the Great Banyan in Kolkata’s botanical garden is an example. Habits spread the same way.

🌱 For today

Each habit puts down a new root. Notice which roots you are feeding. Each habit puts down a new root. Notice which roots you are feeding.

↑ 15.2 · Fed by the guṇas, rooted in desire.

ŚLOKA 15.3

na rūpamasyeha tathopalabhyatenānto na cādirna ca saṃpratiṣṭhāaśvatthamenaṃ suvirūḍhamūlamasaṅgaśastreṇa dṛḍhena chittvā ‖3‖
“Its true form is not perceived here, nor its end, nor its beginning, nor its foundation. Having cut this firmly rooted aśvattha with the strong axe of detachment,”
WordMeans
na rūpam asya iha tathā upalabhyateits form is not perceived here
na antaḥ na ca ādiḥ na ca sampratiṣṭhānor end, beginning, nor foundation
aśvattham enam su-virūḍha-mūlamthis firmly rooted aśvattha

ŚLOKA 15.3 · DEEP DIVE

CUT IT WITH DETACHMENT

🔎 In context

How to be free of the tree: cut it with detachment.

📜 Looking deeper

From inside, we can’t see the tree’s shape, beginning or end. But we can cut it. Commentators: the axe is asaṅga, non-attachment, sharpened by discrimination. Commentators note that the tree cannot be understood from inside: we see branches and leaves, but not the whole. Yet it can be cut. Asaṅga-śastra, the weapon of non-attachment, is sharpened by discrimination and dispassion. Śaṅkara says the axe is detachment born of firm desire for the Supreme. Rāmānuja says detachment comes from knowing the faults of worldly pleasures. Commentators note that the axe must be dṛḍha, strong: weak detachment cannot cut such deep roots. Teachers describe the axe as dispassion sharpened on the stone of discrimination. Some modern teachers say the axe is not violent rejection of the world but a firm, quiet letting go.

🌱 For today

You don’t need to understand everything to begin letting go. You don’t need to understand everything before you start letting go.

↑ 15.3 · Cut it with the axe of detachment.

ŚLOKA 15.4

THEN SEEK THAT GOAL
tataḥ padaṃ tatparimārgitavyaṃyasmingatā na nivartanti bhūyaḥtameva cādyaṃ puruṣaṃ prapadyeyataḥ pravṛttiḥ prasṛtā purāṇī ‖4‖
“…then that goal must be sought, reaching which none return: ‘I take refuge in that primal Person from whom the ancient stream of activity has flowed.’”
WordMeans
tataḥ padam tat parimārgitavyamthen that state must be sought
yasmin gatāḥ na nivartanti bhūyaḥgoing where, none return
tam eva ca ādyam puruṣam prapadyeI take refuge in that primal Person

ŚLOKA 15.4 · DEEP DIVE

THEN SEEK THAT GOAL

🔎 In context

After cutting, seek the source, and take refuge.

📜 Looking deeper

Note the prayer inside the verse: tam eva cādyaṃ puruṣaṃ prapadye, “I take refuge in that primal Person”. Commentators note detachment alone is not enough; one must turn toward the source. The words tam eva cādyaṃ puruṣaṃ prapadye are a prayer of surrender spoken inside the verse. Rāmānuja’s tradition values it as a model of prapatti. Commentators note the two movements: cutting (detachment) and turning (refuge). One without the other is incomplete. Detachment without a goal can become emptiness; devotion without detachment can become another attachment. Commentators note purāṇī pravṛttiḥ, “the ancient stream of activity”: the world-process flows from the Lord like a river from its source. Madhusūdana says one must seek the source after cutting the tree. The same idea of returning to the source appears in Taoist thought and in Plotinus, the Greek philosopher.

🌱 For today

Letting go is half; turning toward the good is the other half. Letting go is half the journey. Turning toward the good is the other half.

↑ 15.4 · I take refuge in the primal Person.

WHO REACHES IT

“Free from pride and delusion, having conquered the fault of attachment, ever dwelling in the Self, desires turned away, freed from the pairs called pleasure and pain, the undeluded reach that imperishable goal.”
WordMeans
nirmāna-mohāḥ jita-saṅga-doṣāḥfree of pride and delusion, attachment conquered
adhyātma-nityāḥ vinivṛtta-kāmāḥever in the Self, desires gone
dvandvaiḥ vimuktāḥ sukha-duḥkha-saṃjñaiḥfreed from opposites called pleasure and pain

ŚLOKA 15.5 · DEEP DIVE

WHO REACHES IT

🔎 In context

The qualities of those who reach the goal.

📜 Looking deeper

Pride (māna) comes first in the list. Commentators: humility is the doorway, again and again in the Gītā (13.7). Commentators note that pride, māna, is named first, as humility, amānitva, was named first in 13.7. Śaṅkara says freedom from pride and delusion makes one fit for the goal. Rāmānuja says these are the qualities of one devoted to the Lord. The list gathers marks from earlier chapters: freedom from attachment (2.48), desire (2.71), and opposites (2.45). Commentators note amūḍhāḥ, “the undeluded”: those who are no longer confused about what lasts and what does not. Śaṅkara says the qualities listed are the means to the goal. Rāmānuja says they are the marks of those who reach the Lord. The verse is in triṣṭubh, giving it a grand tone.

🌱 For today

Humility and freedom from craving: simple marks of real progress. Humility and freedom from craving are simple marks of real progress. Watch for them in yourself.

“Neither the sun, nor the moon, nor fire illumines it. Going there, they do not return. That is my supreme abode.”

ŚLOKA 15.6 · DEEP DIVE

WHERE NO SUN SHINES

🔎 In context

The supreme abode is self-luminous.

📜 Looking deeper

This echoes the Kaṭha and Muṇḍaka Upaniṣads: “There the sun does not shine, nor moon nor stars… by its light all this shines.” Commentators: the source of light needs no light. Kaṭha 2.2.15, Muṇḍaka 2.2.10 and Śvetāśvatara 6.14 all say: na tatra sūryo bhāti na candra-tārakam… tasya bhāsā sarvam idaṃ vibhāti, “there the sun does not shine, nor moon nor stars… by its light all this shines”. Commentators note that the source of light needs no light. Paramaṃ dhāma recalls 8.21. Śaṅkara takes it as the state of Brahman; Rāmānuja and Madhva as the Lord’s abode. Commentators note that the Lord’s supreme abode is described as self-luminous. Vaiṣṇava traditions picture it as Vaikuṇṭha, a realm of light where the freed souls serve the Lord. Advaita sees it as the state of pure consciousness. Both agree it needs no outer light.

🌱 For today

Some light needs no lamp: awareness itself. Some light needs no lamp. Awareness itself is that light.

↑ 15.6 · Where no sun is needed.

ŚLOKA 15.7

A FRAGMENT OF ME
mamaivāṃśo jīvaloke jīvabhūtaḥ sanātanaḥmanaḥṣaṣṭhānīndriyāṇi prakṛtisthāni karṣati ‖7‖
“An eternal fragment of myself, having become a living soul (jīva) in the world of the living, draws to itself the mind and the five senses, which rest in prakṛti.”
WordMeans
mama eva aṃśaḥ jīva-lokea fragment of me in the world of the living
jīva-bhūtaḥ sanātanaḥeternal, having become a jīva
manaḥ ṣaṣṭhāni indriyāṇithe senses with mind as sixth
prakṛti-sthāni karṣatiresting in nature, it draws

ŚLOKA 15.7 · DEEP DIVE

A FRAGMENT OF ME

🔎 In context

Each soul is an eternal fragment of God.

📜 Looking deeper

A key verse in Vedānta debates. Rāmānuja and Madhva: each soul is eternally a real part (aṃśa) of God, distinct yet dependent. Śaṅkara: the “fragment” is like space in a pot, apparently separate from all-space but really one. All agree the soul is eternal and divine in origin. Aṃśa, part or fragment, is central to the Vedānta debates. The Brahma Sūtra (2.3.43) says the soul is a part, aṃśa, of Brahman. Rāmānuja and Madhva use this to show the soul is eternally distinct. Śaṅkara reads it as a seeming part, like pot-space. Madhva calls the soul a pratibimba, reflection, of the Lord. All agree the soul is sanātana, eternal. Commentators note jīva-loke, “in the world of the living”: the fragment becomes a living being by taking on mind and senses. Some teachers compare the soul to a spark of fire, sharing the fire’s nature though small. The Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad (2.1.1) uses this image of sparks from a fire.

🌱 For today

You are a spark of the Divine. Remember your dignity, and others’. You are a spark of the Divine. Remember your own dignity, and see it in others.

↑ 15.7 · A fragment of me.

ŚLOKA 15.8

LIKE WIND CARRYING FRAGRANCE
śarīraṃ yadavāpnoti yaccāpyutkrāmatīśvaraḥgṛhītvaitāni saṃyāti vāyurgandhānivāśayāt ‖8‖
“When the lord (the soul) takes on a body and when he leaves it, he takes these (mind and senses) with him, as the wind carries scents from their places.”
WordMeans
śarīram yat avāpnotithe body he obtains
yat ca api utkrāmati īśvaraḥand when the lord departs
gṛhītvā etāni saṃyātitaking these, he goes

ŚLOKA 15.8 · DEEP DIVE

LIKE WIND CARRYING FRAGRANCE

🔎 In context

At death, the soul carries the subtle mind and senses to the next body.

📜 Looking deeper

A lovely image: the wind moves on but carries the fragrance of the garden. Commentators call this the subtle body (sūkṣma śarīra), carried from life to life. Īśvara here means the soul as master of the body. The image is gentle: the wind leaves the garden but carries its fragrance. Commentators explain the sūkṣma śarīra, subtle body, made of mind, intellect, senses and vital breaths. It carries the impressions of one life into the next. Śaṅkara and Rāmānuja agree on this picture. Commentators note that the soul is called īśvara, lord, here: it is master of its body, though not of the universe. Rāmānuja says this shows the self’s real agency within its sphere. The image of fragrance carried by the wind is a favourite of poets, and many Indian love songs use it.

🌱 For today

What we cultivate inside travels with us. What we cultivate inside travels with us. Grow fragrant habits.

ŚLOKA 15.9

ENJOYING THROUGH THE SENSES

“Presiding over the ear, the eye, touch, taste and smell, and the mind, he enjoys the objects of the senses.”

ŚLOKA 15.9 · DEEP DIVE

ENJOYING THROUGH THE SENSES

🔎 In context

The soul experiences the world through mind and senses.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators: the senses are instruments; the soul is the experiencer. This is the same picture as 13.20–21. Commentators note the order of senses: hearing, sight, touch, taste and smell, linked to space, fire, air, water and earth. The soul presides over them, adhiṣṭhāya, as a rider over horses (the Kaṭha image). Śaṅkara says the soul seems to enjoy through them. Rāmānuja says the soul truly experiences, through the senses, according to its karma. Commentators note upasevate, “enjoys” or “serves”: the soul uses the senses to experience the world. Madhusūdana says enjoyment is not wrong in itself; attachment to it binds. The verse completes the picture of the soul’s life in the body begun in 15.7.

🌱 For today

Your senses are instruments. You are the one who experiences. Your senses are instruments. You are the one who experiences through them.

ŚLOKA 15.10 · DEEP DIVE

THE DELUDED DO NOT SEE

🔎 In context

The soul’s journey is unseen except by the wise.

📜 Looking deeper

The “eye of knowledge” (compare 13.34) sees what the physical eye misses. Commentators: the soul is present in every experience, yet overlooked. The jñāna-cakṣus, eye of knowledge, recalls 13.34. Commentators note that the soul is present in every experience, yet overlooked, as we see objects but rarely notice our own seeing. Śaṅkara says the deluded identify with the body and cannot see the soul. Rāmānuja says the wise see the self as distinct from the body even in daily life. Commentators note utkrāmantam, “departing”: the soul leaving the body at death is invisible to ordinary eyes. Many cultures have stories of seeing the soul leave. The Gītā says only the eye of knowledge sees the soul’s presence at every moment, not only at death.

🌱 For today

Look beneath the surface of experience. Someone is experiencing. Look beneath the surface of experience. There is always someone experiencing.

↑ 15.10 · Seen only by the eye of knowledge.

ŚLOKA 15.11

THE FIRE OF DIGESTION

📜 Looking deeper

The four kinds of food: chewed, sucked, licked and swallowed. This verse is often chanted before meals, and many families recite all of chapter 15 at mealtime. Commentators: eating becomes worship. Vaiśvānara, “belonging to all people”, is the fire in all beings; the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad (5.9.1) describes it as the fire within that digests food, heard as a sound when the ears are closed. CHAPTER 15 ON ONE PAGE

Chapter 15 in five lines
The tree (15.1–4). The world is an upside-down aśvattha, roots above; cut it with detachment and seek the primal Person.
The abode (15.5–6). Reached by the humble and free; lit by no sun, moon or fire.
The soul (15.7–11). An eternal fragment of the Lord, carrying mind and senses from body to body like wind carries scent.
The Lord in all (15.12–15). The light in the sun, the sap in plants, the fire of digestion, seated in every heart.
Puruṣottama (15.16–20). Beyond the perishable and the imperishable; knowing him, one worships with the whole being.
