WHERE WE ARE

In Chapter 1 Arjuna saw his grandfathers and teachers across the field, dropped his bow and sank down in grief.

Chapter 2 is where the teaching starts, and many call it the Gītā in miniature. It holds almost every big idea of the book: the eternal Self, duty, action without craving for results, yoga as evenness of mind, and the portrait of the person of steady wisdom.

ŚLOKA 2.1 · DEEP DIVE

TEARS ON THE BATTLEFIELD

🔎 In context

Chapter 1 ended with Arjuna slumped in the chariot. Sañjaya picks up the scene: the great warrior is in tears. Now, for the first time, Kṛṣṇa will speak at length. The image is vivid: the world’s greatest archer, weeping in front of two armies. Chapter 2 is the longest teaching chapter after chapter 18, and is often called the Gītā in miniature.

📜 Looking deeper

Sañjaya again uses the word kṛpā, pity. Commentators note that pity which paralyses is different from compassion that acts. Kṛṣṇa is called Madhusūdana, the slayer of the demon Madhu: the one who removes demons will now remove the demon of confusion from Arjuna’s mind. Commentators link the three states named here: kṛpā, pity; aśru, tears; viṣāda, despair. Madhusūdana Sarasvatī calls them the marks of one who has mistaken the body for the self. Sañjaya does not yet call Kṛṣṇa the Lord; that title, śrī bhagavān, comes with the next verse, as if the friend becomes a teacher the moment he opens his mouth. The chapter has four movements: Arjuna’s surrender (2.1–10), the knowledge of the Self, sāṅkhya (2.11–30), the duty of a warrior (2.31–38), and the yoga of action and the person of steady wisdom (2.39–72).

🌱 For today

Tears are not shameful. Even the strongest people break down. What matters is what happens next: whether we stay sunk, or turn to someone who can help us see. When someone breaks down in front of us, the first gift is presence. Kṛṣṇa does not look away. He turns toward Arjuna.

ŚLOKA 2.2 · DEEP DIVE

“WHERE DID THIS WEAKNESS COME FROM?”

🔎 In context

The Gītā now calls Kṛṣṇa śrī bhagavān, the Blessed Lord, for the first time. His first teaching is not philosophy but a sharp question: where did this come from? Kaśmalam, the word for Arjuna’s state, means a dark faintness, a stain on the mind. Kṛṣṇa asks kutas, from where?, a teacher’s question that invites Arjuna to look at the source of his feeling rather than defend it.

📜 Looking deeper

Ārya here means noble in conduct, not a race. Kṛṣṇa says Arjuna’s state fits neither this world (honour) nor the next (heaven). Commentators see a skilled teacher: first shake the student out of self-pity, then teach. Śaṅkara’s commentary begins its real argument a few verses later, after Arjuna surrenders. Three reasons, from three angles: character, the next world, and reputation in this one. Kṛṣṇa begins on Arjuna’s own ground, with the values of a kṣatriya, before going deeper. Commentators admire this as good teaching: start where the student stands. The larger argument from the Self comes from 2.11. Rāmānuja notes that Kṛṣṇa calls the state viṣama, “at the wrong moment”: even right feelings can come at the wrong time.

🌱 For today

When we are sinking, a kind friend sometimes needs to be firm. Gentle words can wait; first, stand up. Asking “where is this coming from?” is a powerful question to put to our own moods. Feelings often lose their grip once we trace them.

ŚLOKA 2.3 · DEEP DIVE

“STAND UP!”

🔎 In context

Kṛṣṇa ends his rebuke with a command. He reminds Arjuna who he is: Pārtha, son of Pṛthā, and Parantapa, the one who burns his enemies. Klaibya means impotence or unmanliness in the old idiom; the sense is loss of nerve, not any judgement about gender. Parantapa, “scorcher of foes”, reminds Arjuna of his own reputation.

📜 Looking deeper

Swami Vivekananda said that if one reads only this verse, one gains the whole message of the Gītā: klaibyaṃ mā sma gamaḥ, do not yield to weakness. The word kṣudra, petty, is pointed: Kṛṣṇa calls the noble-sounding speech of chapter 1 small at heart. Vivekananda’s reading of this verse became famous through his lectures: strength, not weakness, is the first message of the Gītā. Commentators also note the tone. Kṛṣṇa’s first words are not comfort but challenge. Some find it harsh; most read it as the firm love of a friend who knows Arjuna can rise. Uttiṣṭha, “stand up”, returns at 2.37, 4.42 and 11.33: the Gītā is a long call to stand up. Arjuna does not obey yet; he needs to be convinced, not commanded. The teaching that follows is Kṛṣṇa’s patient attempt to do just that.

🌱 For today

Many of our weaknesses dress up as good reasons. Kṛṣṇa’s word to all of us: do not give in to weakness; you are stronger than you think. Sometimes what we call kindness is really avoidance, and what feels harsh is real care. A good friend says “get up” when we need to hear it.

ŚLOKA 2.4 · DEEP DIVE

“HOW CAN I SHOOT BHĪṢMA?”

🔎 In context

Arjuna replies. His grief now has names: his grandfather Bhīṣma, who raised him, and Droṇa, his teacher. Bhīṣma had brought up Pāṇḍu’s orphaned sons in Hastināpura; in the epic Arjuna later recalls how, as a child, he would climb into his lap and call him “father”, and how Bhīṣma would gently say, “I am your father’s father”. Droṇa had taught Arjuna archery and loved him above all his pupils, even above his own son.

📜 Looking deeper

In Indian tradition, elders and teachers are honoured almost as gods (“ācārya devo bhava”). To shoot arrows at feet one should touch in reverence feels monstrous. Arjuna plays on the names: Kṛṣṇa slays enemies (arisūdana), but these are not enemies. Pūjārhau, “worthy of worship”: an honoured elder’s feet are touched with flowers; Arjuna would have to touch them with arrows. The pun on madhusūdana and arisūdana, “slayer of Madhu” and “slayer of foes”, makes his point: you kill demons and enemies, but these are neither. The Taittirīya Upaniṣad (1.11.2) teaches mātṛ-devo bhava, pitṛ-devo bhava, ācārya-devo bhava: treat mother, father and teacher as gods. Arjuna’s anguish is the anguish of a well-taught student. Kṛṣṇa will not deny this reverence; he will show a duty that stands above it here.

🌱 For today

Facing those we respect in a conflict is among the hardest things. Respect and opposition can coexist, but it takes maturity to hold both. We can respect someone deeply and still have to stand against what they are doing. Doing it without losing respect is a mark of maturity.

↑ 2.4 · How can I fight those I worship?

ŚLOKA 2.5

“BETTER TO BEG”

ŚLOKA 2.5 · DEEP DIVE

“BETTER TO BEG”

🔎 In context

Arjuna would rather live as a beggar than kill his teachers. A begging life was forbidden to a kṣatriya, yet he would choose it. Bhaikṣyam, alms: begging was the proper life of a renunciant or student, not of a warrior-prince. For a kṣatriya to beg was a kind of social death.

📜 Looking deeper

The phrase artha-kāmān is read two ways: “though they are greedy for gain” (admitting Droṇa and Bhīṣma fight for wages) or as describing the pleasures Arjuna himself would win. Either way, he sees any victory as tasting of blood. Rudhira-pradigdhān, “smeared with blood”: Arjuna imagines every pleasure of victory stained. The word gurūn, teachers or elders, is repeated: gurūn ahatvā, “not killing the teachers”, and gurūn hatvā, “having killed the teachers”. The verse turns on that one word. Śaṅkara and Rāmānuja both take artha-kāmān with the teachers: though they fight out of interest, they are still my gurus. In the Bhīṣma Parva, Bhīṣma and Droṇa themselves say they are bound to Duryodhana by the food they have eaten. Arjuna is ready to excuse them; he cannot excuse himself.

🌱 For today

Sometimes we prefer to lose everything rather than hurt someone we love. The Gītā respects the feeling and asks us to think it through. We often forgive others more easily than ourselves. That can be generous, or a way of carrying a weight that is not ours.

↑ 2.5 · Every pleasure would taste of blood.

ŚLOKA 2.6 · DEEP DIVE

“WE DON’T EVEN KNOW WHICH IS BETTER”

🔎 In context

Arjuna’s confidence is gone. He no longer knows whether winning or losing is better. Arjuna admits not knowing kataran no garīyaḥ, “which is weightier for us”: winning or losing. The verse is in the longer triṣṭubh metre, which the Gītā uses for moments of high feeling.

📜 Looking deeper

This verse is the honest heart of his crisis: not cowardice but real moral uncertainty. That honesty makes him ready to learn. A mind that admits “I do not know” is open to teaching. Commentators see Arjuna’s dilemma sharpen from “I won’t” to “I don’t know”. The shift matters. In chapter 1 he argued for his view. Here he admits that he cannot see the answer. The Upaniṣads prize this moment. In the Kaṭha Upaniṣad, Naciketas will not be put off with riches and asks only for the truth. Arjuna, like Naciketas, sees that no outer gain will do. Śaṅkara calls such a state the doorway to the teaching: only one who knows he does not know can be taught.

🌱 For today

Admitting “I don’t know” is not weakness. It is the first step to finding out. Being stuck is uncomfortable, but it is honest. Many good decisions start with “I don’t know which way is right”.

↑ 2.6 · Honest doubt: “we do not know”.

ŚLOKA 2.7

“I AM YOUR STUDENT”

“My nature is struck by the fault of weakness; my mind is confused about dharma. I ask you: tell me for certain what is better. I am your student; I have taken refuge in you. Teach me.”

ŚLOKA 2.7 · DEEP DIVE

“I AM YOUR STUDENT”

🔎 In context

The turning point of the Gītā. In chapter 1 Arjuna argued. Now he admits he does not know, and asks for teaching. A friend becomes a disciple; the charioteer becomes a teacher. This is the moment the Gītā becomes a teaching. In Indian tradition, a teacher teaches when asked; the request is the key.

📜 Looking deeper

Kārpaṇya literally means miserliness, the state of the kṛpaṇa; the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad (3.8.10) calls one who dies without knowing the Imperishable a kṛpaṇa, a poor soul. Śaṅkara’s commentary begins in earnest here, from Arjuna’s surrender. The word prapanna, surrendered, is central for Rāmānuja’s tradition. Dharma-sammūḍha-cetāḥ, “my mind confused about dharma”: Arjuna does not lack courage or skill, but moral clarity. He asks for śreyas, the truly good, niścitam, “with certainty”. Commentators of every school treat this verse as the Gītā’s real beginning. Śaṅkara says the teaching that follows is for removing śoka and moha, grief and delusion. For the Śrī Vaiṣṇava tradition, prapannam, “surrendered”, foreshadows the final teaching of surrender at 18.66: the Gītā begins and ends with refuge. Modern readers see in it the humility every learner needs.

🌱 For today

Real learning starts when we stop defending ourselves and ask. Everyone needs a teacher, friend or mentor to whom they can say: I am stuck; help me. Find someone wise you trust, and be honest with them: “I am confused; help me think.” That one sentence can change the course of a life.

↑ 2.7 · Teaching begins when we ask honestly.

ŚLOKA 2.8

NOTHING WILL CURE THIS GRIEF

ŚLOKA 2.8 · DEEP DIVE

NOTHING WILL CURE THIS GRIEF

🔎 In context

Arjuna explains why he needs a teacher: no outer gain can cure his grief. Ucchoṣaṇam indriyāṇām, “drying up the senses”: grief is pictured as a drought of the spirit. Arjuna names the highest prizes he can imagine: an unrivalled kingdom on earth, and surāṇām ādhipatyam, rule over the gods.

📜 Looking deeper

An important insight: Arjuna sees that his problem is inside, and no outer prize can fix it. Commentators call this the beginning of discrimination (viveka). One who knows that the world cannot cure inner sorrow is ready for knowledge of the Self. Even Indra’s throne would not cure his grief. Commentators find here the first sign of real dispassion. Unlike 1.32, it is no longer just pain at the cost. It is the insight that outer things cannot touch an inner wound. The Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad (1.2.12) describes the seeker who, having examined the worlds won by action, sees that the uncreated cannot be gained by the created, and goes to a teacher. Arjuna is doing exactly that. The Gītā will later say that pleasures born of contact are duḥkha-yonayaḥ, wombs of sorrow (5.22).

🌱 For today

More money, power or status rarely heals inner pain. Arjuna sees it clearly; so can we. When something hurts deep inside, buying, winning or achieving rarely helps. It is worth asking what kind of help the wound really needs.

↑ 2.8 · No outer gain can heal inner grief.

ŚLOKA 2.9 · DEEP DIVE

“I WILL NOT FIGHT”

🔎 In context

Sañjaya reports Arjuna’s final word: “I will not fight.” Then silence. Na yotsya iti, “I will not fight”: two words in Sanskrit. Sañjaya, speaking to Dhṛtarāṣṭra, reports the most hopeful words the blind king could hear.

📜 Looking deeper

The irony is in the names: Guḍākeśa, conqueror of sleep, is sunk in the sleep of confusion; Parantapa, scorcher of foes, refuses to fight. Some note that Sañjaya is also gently telling Dhṛtarāṣṭra: there is still hope that the war may stop. It will not. Commentators admire the craft of this verse: Arjuna has asked to be taught (2.7), yet still announces his decision (2.9). The mind both surrenders and holds on. Kṛṣṇa does not argue with the decision directly; he answers the confusion beneath it. At 18.73 the Gītā will close the circle: kariṣye vacanaṃ tava, “I will do as you say”. Between “I will not fight” and “I will do as you say” lies the whole teaching. Some note that Arjuna’s silence is a gift too: only one who stops talking can listen.

🌱 For today

Silence after a hard decision can be the space where a better answer arrives. Arjuna has spoken; now he listens. Even after asking for help, we often cling to our own answer. Notice that in yourself, and give the help a real chance.

↑ 2.9 · He says “I will not fight” and falls silent.

ŚLOKA 2.10 · DEEP DIVE

AS IF SMILING

🔎 In context

The scene is set for the teaching: between the armies, Arjuna silent and grieving, Kṛṣṇa about to speak. Senayor ubhayor madhye, “between the two armies”, repeats 1.21 and 1.24: we are back in the same spot, but now the conversation turns inward.

📜 Looking deeper

Why smile? Commentators offer several readings: the smile of a teacher who knows the problem can be solved; gentle amusement at a warrior talking wisdom while weeping; or simply warmth, to put the student at ease. The setting matters too: the Gītā is spoken in the middle, between two sides, at the point of greatest tension. Commentators love this smile. Madhusūdana says it shows that Kṛṣṇa saw the problem as small compared to the remedy he held. Some Vaiṣṇava teachers read it as tenderness: the Lord smiles at his friend as a parent smiles at a frightened child. Others note the gentle irony that follows at once in 2.11: “you speak words of wisdom, yet grieve”. The setting is symbolic too. The Gītā is not taught in a forest hermitage or a temple. It is taught in the middle of life’s hardest moment, between two sides, where most of us actually need it.

🌱 For today

A calm smile in a crisis is not mockery. It says: this can be faced. Sometimes that alone helps. Wisdom is most useful where life is hardest, not in quiet retreats. The Gītā is a teaching for the middle of things.

↑ 2.10 · The teaching begins with a smile.

ŚLOKA 2.11

THE WISE DO NOT GRIEVE
śrībhagavān uvācaaśocyānanvaśocastvaṃ prajñāvādāṃśca bhāṣasegatāsūnagatāsūṃśca nānuśocanti paṇḍitāḥ ‖11‖

ŚLOKA 2.11 · DEEP DIVE

THE WISE DO NOT GRIEVE

🔎 In context

The teaching proper begins. Kṛṣṇa points out a contradiction: Arjuna speaks like a learned man, yet grieves like one who does not know. This verse begins Kṛṣṇa’s teaching proper, which runs, with Arjuna’s questions, to the end of chapter 18. Prajñā-vādān, “words of wisdom”, refers to Arjuna’s arguments about family dharma in chapter 1.

📜 Looking deeper

Śaṅkara takes this verse as the start of the Gītā’s teaching: grief and delusion come from not knowing the Self. A paṇḍita is defined here not by learning but by freedom from grief. The verse sets the theme for the rest of the chapter: what are we, really? Why not grieve for the living? Commentators explain: neither the body, which must pass, nor the Self, which cannot die, is a proper object of grief. Paṇḍitāḥ, the wise, are those whose knowledge has become vision. Śaṅkara’s introduction to his commentary takes this verse as the start of the Gītā’s central theme. Rāmānuja calls this section the teaching of the self, laying the ground for the teaching of action and devotion that follows. The famous discussion of the Self, 2.11–30, is the Gītā’s philosophical foundation.

🌱 For today

Quoting wise sayings is easy; living them is another matter. The test of understanding is how we meet loss. Knowing something and living by it are different. This verse asks gently: does what I know actually change how I feel and act?

↑ 2.11 · Grief comes from not knowing what we are.

ŚLOKA 2.12

ALWAYS WERE, ALWAYS WILL BE

“Never was there a time when I did not exist, nor you, nor these kings; nor shall any of us ever cease to be hereafter.”
WordMeans

ŚLOKA 2.12 · DEEP DIVE

ALWAYS WERE, ALWAYS WILL BE

🔎 In context

The first reason not to grieve: no one truly dies. I, you and these kings always were and always will be. Kṛṣṇa includes himself, Arjuna and all the kings on the field. The verse speaks of past and future: the self did not begin with this body and will not end with it.

📜 Looking deeper

The plural “we” has been read in different ways. For Śaṅkara (Advaita), it is a manner of speaking: the one Self appears as many through different bodies. For Rāmānuja and Madhva, Kṛṣṇa clearly affirms many eternal selves, really distinct from God and each other. Traditions differ; all agree the self is eternal. This is the first of many statements of the self’s eternity in chapter 2. The difference between the schools turns partly on this verse. Rāmānuja says “I, you, these kings” cannot be mere appearance: Kṛṣṇa, the truthful teacher, would not use a misleading plural. Śaṅkara replies that the plural follows ordinary speech about bodies, while the real Self is one. Madhva holds that God, selves and matter are eternally distinct. The Gītā’s words have supported all these readings for a thousand years. What all share is that no one Arjuna loves will truly cease.

🌱 For today

If the people we love truly are eternal, then death is a parting, not an ending. That can change how we grieve. Someone we love who has died has not become nothing. Many traditions say the same. The Gītā says it with unusual confidence.

↑ 2.12 · No one ever ceases to be.

ŚLOKA 2.13 · DEEP DIVE

CHILDHOOD, YOUTH, OLD AGE

🔎 In context

Kṛṣṇa gives an everyday picture. We already change bodies within one life: the child’s body is gone, yet “I” remain. Dehin, “the embodied one”, is the Gītā’s word for the self in a body. Kaumāram yauvanaṃ jarā, childhood, youth, old age: the three ages of a life.

📜 Looking deeper

The argument is from experience: the self that witnesses childhood, youth and age is not itself changing in the same way. Death, Kṛṣṇa says, is one more change of the same kind. The word dhīra, steady or wise, appears again and again in this chapter as the goal. Dehāntara-prāptiḥ, “gaining another body”, is the first mention of rebirth in the Gītā. The argument moves from the known to the unknown. We have seen the body change completely, cell by cell, and the self remain; why fear one more change? Commentators note that dhīra, steady, comes from a root meaning to hold, or from dhī, understanding. The steady one is the one who understands. The theme returns in 2.15, 2.54–72 (the sthita-prajña) and 14.24. Modern readers often meet this verse through its plain logic: if you are not your childhood body, perhaps you are not this body either.

🌱 For today

Look at an old photo of yourself as a child. The body has changed completely; something has not. That is what Kṛṣṇa is pointing to. When ageing or illness frightens us, it helps to remember how many bodies we have already outgrown. Something in us has stayed the same through all of them.

↑ 2.13 · Death is a change of body, not of Self.

ŚLOKA 2.14 · DEEP DIVE

HOT AND COLD COME AND GO

🔎 In context

The first practical lesson. Every feeling, from heat and cold to joy and sorrow, comes from contact between senses and objects, and every such contact passes. Mātrā-sparśāḥ, “contacts with matter” or “sense contacts”, bring śīta-uṣṇa-sukha-duḥkha, cold and heat, pleasure and pain. Kṛṣṇa calls Arjuna both Kaunteya and Bhārata, son of Kuntī and descendant of Bharata: the gentle and the noble side together.

📜 Looking deeper

Titikṣā, forbearance, becomes one of the classic six virtues of Vedānta. It is not suppression or numbness, but the patience that lets feelings pass without being swept away. Śaṅkara defines it as bearing pairs of opposites without anxiety or complaint. The six virtues of Vedānta in Śaṅkara’s Vivekacūḍāmaṇi are calm, self-control, withdrawal, forbearance, faith and focus; forbearance, titikṣā, comes from this verse. The Buddha also taught that sensations arise and pass; the Gītā’s word here is close in spirit. The point is not to stop feeling but to stop being ruled by every feeling. Commentators stress that endurance is a training. It begins with small things, such as heat, cold and hunger, and grows into steadiness in grief and joy.

🌱 For today

Next time you are angry, cold, hurt or thrilled, remember: this will pass. That one thought gives a little space to choose how to respond. Practise on small discomforts: a cold shower, a delayed meal, a rude remark. Letting these pass builds the strength for larger storms.

↑ 2.14 · Feelings come and go: endure, do not drown.

ŚLOKA 2.15 · DEEP DIVE

FIT FOR IMMORTALITY

🔎 In context

The reward of endurance: one who stays steady through pleasure and pain becomes fit for amṛtatva, immortality, which here means liberation. Puruṣarṣabha, “bull among men”, is one of Kṛṣṇa’s many honour-names for Arjuna. Na vyathayanti, “do not disturb”, from vyath, to waver or trouble.

📜 Looking deeper

Evenness (sama) is the Gītā’s great word, culminating in 2.48: “evenness is yoga.” Commentators read “immortality” as freedom from the cycle of birth and death. The path to it begins with something simple: not being thrown by every change. Sama-duḥkha-sukham, “equal in pain and pleasure”: the first statement of the Gītā’s great ideal of evenness. Commentators note the order: the one who is undisturbed (na vyathayanti), who is wise (dhīra), and who is even (sama). Together these make one fit, kalpate, for amṛtatva, freedom from death. Śaṅkara reads immortality as liberation through knowledge, Rāmānuja as the self’s release from bondage to matter. The ideal is developed in 2.38 (be the same in gain and loss, victory and defeat), 2.48, 6.7–9, 12.18–19 and 14.24–25.

🌱 For today

Equanimity is not coldness. It is a steadiness that lets us feel fully without losing ourselves. Evenness is learnable. Each time we do not overreact, we strengthen it a little.

↑ 2.15 · Steady in joy and pain: fit for freedom.

ŚLOKA 2.16 · DEEP DIVE

THE REAL NEVER CEASES

🔎 In context

A one-line philosophy. Whatever truly is cannot stop being; whatever can stop being was never fully real. Nāsato vidyate bhāvaḥ, nābhāvo vidyate sataḥ: “of the unreal there is no being; of the real there is no non-being”. This half-line is among the most commented upon in Indian philosophy.

📜 Looking deeper

This verse is a corner-stone of Vedānta, read differently by each school. For Śaṅkara, the changing world is asat in the sense of not ultimately real, and only Brahman-Self is sat. Rāmānuja reads asat as the perishable body and sat as the imperishable self, both real but different in nature. Tattva-darśibhiḥ, “by the seers of truth”: this is not speculation but what the wise have seen. Śaṅkara builds much of Advaita on this verse: whatever changes is not fully real, because the real does not change; only pure awareness is constant. Rāmānuja reads it more simply: the body is perishable (asat in that sense) and the self imperishable (sat). Madhva reads asat as the non-existent and stresses that God and selves are truly real. Gauḍapāda’s Māṇḍūkya Kārikā and later Advaita texts quote the line often. The verse also echoes the Chāndogya Upaniṣad (6.2.1), sad eva somya idam agra āsīt, “in the beginning, dear one, this was Being alone”.

🌱 For today

Ask of anything you hold onto: will this last? What does not last cannot be our final refuge. Put your trust in what lasts. Changing things are to be used and enjoyed, but not leaned on with our full weight.

THAT WHICH PERVADES ALL

Kṛṣṇa names the real: that which pervades all this. It cannot be destroyed by anyone. Avyaya, imperishable, literally “not running out”.

📜 Looking deeper

The word tatam, “spread through”, pictures the Self like thread running through beads, or space filling every pot. The same image returns in 7.7: “all this is strung on me like pearls on a thread.” Schools differ on whether the pervader is the individual self (Rāmānuja) or the one Brahman (Śaṅkara). Commentators often compare the Self to space: pots break, but the space inside them is untouched. The word tatam returns in 8.22, 9.4 and 11.38, where Kṛṣṇa says all is pervaded by him. 🌱 For today

Whatever we are at the deepest level cannot be harmed by events. Remembering this can give quiet courage. There is something in you that no event can touch. Knowing that does not stop the hard things, but it stops them from defining you.

BODIES END

Bodies end; the one who dwells in them does not. Therefore, Kṛṣṇa concludes, fight. Three adjectives describe the embodied one: nitya, eternal; anāśin, indestructible; aprameya, immeasurable, beyond the means of knowing.

📜 Looking deeper

“So fight” can sound abrupt after metaphysics. Commentators stress it is not a general command to violence; it is Arjuna’s specific duty, now freed of the false grief that blocked it. 🌱 For today

Clear thinking about what lasts can free us to do hard things that fear and grief were blocking. Fear and grief can freeze us from doing what we know is right. Clarity about what truly matters can unfreeze us.

NEITHER KILLER NOR KILLED

🔎 In context

Arjuna fears being a killer. Kṛṣṇa answers: the true Self neither kills nor is killed.

📜 Looking deeper

This verse closely echoes the Kaṭha Upaniṣad (1.2.19). Commentators warn against misusing it: it does not license harm, since bodies and actions still have moral weight. It speaks of the Self’s nature, to dissolve the fear and guilt that cloud Arjuna’s judgement. Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.2.19 has nearly the same words: hantā cen manyate hantum, hataś cen manyate hatam. The Gītā draws freely on the Upaniṣads; this is one of several verses taken almost whole. Commentators warn that the verse is about the Self, not about bodies or actions. The Gītā holds that actions have consequences (3.9, 4.17, 18.12), and that harming others is the mark of the āsurika nature (16.9). What 2.19 removes is a false identification: Arjuna thinks he will be the killer of Bhīṣma’s Self, and grieves for Bhīṣma as if he will cease. Both thoughts are mistakes about what a person is.

🌱 For today

Our deepest self is untouched by what happens to the body. That is comfort in loss, not an excuse for harm. This verse comforts the grieving. It is never a licence to harm. Read it as consolation, not as permission.

Each word denies a change: no birth, no death, no beginning, no end, no ageing (purāṇa, “ancient yet ever new”). 🌱 For today

This verse is a comfort in grief: the one we have lost was never only the body we knew. If you have lost someone, this verse can be read slowly, aloud. Many families have found comfort in it for centuries.

HOW COULD IT KILL?

Kṛṣṇa concludes this line of thought: one who knows the Self cannot think of himself as a killer in the ultimate sense. Katham sa puruṣaḥ, “how can that person…”: Kṛṣṇa asks a question rather than stating a rule.

📜 Looking deeper

Śaṅkara builds a major argument here: the knower of the Self does not see himself as the doer of any action, so for him the ordinary rules of action no longer bind. He reads this as a pointer to renunciation for the wise. Rāmānuja reads it as freedom from false grief while still acting. The verse is central to Śaṅkara’s argument that the knower of the Self is free of all sense of agency. For him it shows that the highest knowledge goes with renunciation of action. Rāmānuja disagrees: the knower still acts, but without the false notion that he is the body or that the self of another can be destroyed. Madhva reads it as a reminder that God is the real doer and the self a dependent agent. The verse is also cited in later debates on the ethics of action. 🌱 For today

Knowing who we are changes how we act. Not carelessly, but without the heavy “I” that adds fear and guilt. When we stop seeing ourselves as the centre of every outcome, we act with less fear and more care.

The best-known picture in the Gītā. We do not mourn an old shirt; the Self moves on to a new body. The verse is in triṣṭubh. It is perhaps the Gītā’s single best-known image, recited in films, novels and at countless funerals.

This is the Gītā’s clearest statement of rebirth. Commentators note that bodies are not always “old” at death; the image is about the attitude, not age. Changing clothes is ordinary. The Gītā invites us to see death with the same calm, as part of a longer journey. We change clothes without sadness. The Gītā hopes we can learn to see bodies, ours and others’, a little more like that.

Four elements that destroy every material thing cannot touch the Self, because it is not made of elements.

📜 Looking deeper

The verse is chosen for Arjuna: he stands among weapons, and the Self is beyond them. Commentators note that earth, water, fire and air each act on matter; the Self, like space, is subtler than all. Weapons are made of earth (metal); so all four elements are named. This is one of the most chanted Gītā verses. Commentators note its order: earth (weapons), fire, water, air. Space, the fifth element, is not named, because the Self is compared to space throughout the Upaniṣads. Śaṅkara says the elements act only on things that have parts; the Self has no parts, so they cannot touch it. Madhva adds that the self is not destroyed even in the cosmic dissolution (pralaya, 8.18–19). For Arjuna, surrounded by bows, spears and fire-tipped arrows, the verse is pointed comfort: none of these can touch what Bhīṣma and Droṇa truly are.

🌱 For today

Whatever harms the body cannot harm what we most deeply are. That can be a steadying thought in danger. Fear of harm is natural. Remembering that the deepest part of us is beyond harm can let us act bravely where it matters.

Kṛṣṇa repeats the point with more words for the Self’s nature. Acchedya, uncuttable; adāhya, unburnable; akledya, unwettable; aśoṣya, undryable. Then five positive words: nitya, eternal; sarva-gata, all-pervading; sthāṇu, stable; acala, unmoving; sanātana, everlasting.

Why repeat? Śaṅkara says the subject is so hard to grasp that the teacher comes at it again and again, from different sides. Sthāṇu means a post or pillar, firm-rooted; sanātana, everlasting, is the same word used for “Sanātana Dharma”. Sarva-gata, “gone everywhere”, is read differently by the schools. Madhva holds a similar view. Sthāṇu, a post, also means Śiva in other contexts. Teachers explain the many words not as decoration but as a meditation aid: each word removes one more wrong idea of what we are, until only the right one remains.

🌱 For today

Hard truths need repetition. Do not worry if something deep does not sink in at once. Read the list of words slowly, one at a time, as a meditation. Each one loosens a fear.

The Self cannot be seen, thought of as an object, or changed. Knowing this, grief has no ground. Avyakta, unmanifest; acintya, unthinkable; avikārya, unchanging. Tasmād evaṃ viditvainam, “therefore, knowing it thus”: this closes the first argument, which began at 2.11.

📜 Looking deeper

Avyakta: not perceptible by the senses; acintya: not an object of thought. Commentators note the paradox: we are asked to “know” what cannot be known as an object. Vedānta says it is known as the knower itself, by being it, not by grasping it. The Kena Upaniṣad (1.5–6) says: that which speech cannot express, but by which speech is expressed; that which the mind cannot think, but by which the mind thinks: know that to be Brahman. Commentators read 2.25 in that spirit. The Self is not unknown in the sense of missing; it is unknown as an object because it is always the subject. Śaṅkara’s whole method rests on this. Rāmānuja agrees the self cannot be grasped by the senses but holds that it is known as a distinct conscious being. Madhva says even the self’s full nature is known only by God’s grace.

Some truths are not grasped like facts. They are recognised quietly, like realising you are the one who is looking. Try this: notice that you are aware. That awareness itself cannot be pointed at like a chair. The Gītā says that is close to what you are.

The verse warns against thinking that hearing about it is the same as realising it. Four modes appear: seeing, speaking, hearing, and knowing. The first three are wonders; the fourth is rare. Rāmānuja says the self’s nature is wonderful because it is so unlike anything else we know. Madhusūdana adds that the teacher, the student and the teaching are all marvels. Some modern teachers say the very attitude of wonder is a sign one is on the right track. Hearing is necessary; the Gītā will say that the knowledge must also be realised (4.38, 7.2).

🌱 For today

Reading about the Self is a start, not the goal. Wonder is the right attitude: openness rather than the feeling that we already know. Keep a sense of wonder about life and about yourself. It keeps the mind open, and an open mind learns.

Seeing the same life in every being changes how we treat others. It is the root of real compassion. The life in you and the life in an ant are, in the Gītā’s view, alike in nature. Let that shape how gently we move through the world.

A second kind of argument begins: from duty. Arjuna is a kṣatriya, a protector; refusing a just war is abandoning those he is sworn to protect. A kṣatriya’s dharma was to protect the people, uphold justice and fight in a just cause.

📜 Looking deeper

The Gītā argues on several levels. Having spoken of the eternal Self, Kṛṣṇa turns to svadharma, one’s own duty in society. A war is dharmya, righteous, when it is fought to protect, after peace has been tried. Kṛṣṇa himself had gone to the Kauravas as a peace envoy before the war. This verse begins the second argument, from duty, 2.31–37. Commentators note the word api, “also”: duty is an additional reason, not the main one. The main reason is the knowledge of the Self. The Mahābhārata has a long discussion of just war: fight only after peace is tried, do not strike the unarmed or the fleeing, protect non-combatants. These rules were set before the battle (Bhīṣma Parva 1). Before the war, Kṛṣṇa had gone as envoy to plead for peace. Yudhiṣṭhira had offered to settle for five villages. 🌱 For today

Each of us has roles: parent, worker, citizen. Running from the duties of our role, however hard, lets down those who depend on us. Before a hard confrontation, ask: have I tried every peaceful way? If yes, standing firm may be the right duty.

Here “sin” means abandoning one’s duty, especially to the innocent who depend on one’s protection. Commentators link this to 3.35: one’s own dharma, even imperfect, is better than abandoning it. 🌱 For today

Not acting is also a choice with consequences. Walking away from a duty can harm others as much as a wrong act. Doing nothing can feel safe, but it is still a choice. Ask who will pay if you step back.

Again this is a worldly argument, deliberately used. This is a sharp psychological point. Others cannot see inside us; they see the act. Arjuna’s pity, however sincere, will look like cowardice to Karṇa, Duryodhana and the rest. Our motives may be pure, but others judge by actions. It is worth asking how a decision will be understood, not only how it feels. Our reasons feel clear from inside. Seeing how others will read our actions can reveal motives we had not noticed.

The criticism is aimed at ritualists who saw the Vedas only as means to heaven and wealth, not at the Vedas themselves; the Gītā elsewhere honours the Vedas (15.15). 🔎 In context

Kṛṣṇa describes such ritualists: driven by desire, aiming only at heaven, multiplying rites for enjoyment and power.

📜 Looking deeper

Even heaven, in this view, is temporary; it leads back to birth (9.21). The aim of the Gītā is freedom, not a better reward. Rituals for heaven, however splendid, keep one turning in the wheel. Commentators note kriyā-viśeṣa-bahulām, “full of elaborate rites”: the Vedic ritual system had become very complex by the Gītā’s time. Doing good for a reward is better than doing harm, but it still keeps us bargaining. The Gītā invites a freer motive. Ask of your goals: when I get this, then what? If the answer is always “something more”, look for a deeper aim.

YOUR RIGHT IS TO ACTION ALONE

🔎 In context

The most famous verse of the Gītā. Four instructions in two lines: act; don’t claim the result; don’t work only for the result; and don’t make this an excuse to stop.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators stress the last clause: this is not permission to be passive. Śaṅkara reads the verse as a discipline for those not yet ready for renunciation. Rāmānuja calls it the core of karma yoga. Tilak saw it as the Gītā’s central teaching for public life. The Gītā’s own definition of yoga: not postures, not breathing, but equanimity in action.

Two definitions of yoga stand side by side: samatvam here and karmasu kauśalam in 2.50. What skill? Commentators answer: the skill of acting so that action does not bind. Even “good deeds” bind if done with ego, by producing more karma. The phrase is now the motto of many institutions. Commentators note the pairing: in sorrow, unshaken; in pleasure, without craving. The same pair returns in 12.15 and 14.24. Rāga, bhaya, krodha, passion, fear and anger, recur in 4.10, where those free of them, purified by knowledge, come to Kṛṣṇa. Śaṅkara says the sage’s speech reflects this freedom. Patañjali lists rāga and dveṣa (attraction and aversion) and abhiniveśa (clinging to life, the root of fear) among the five afflictions (Yoga Sūtra 2.3). The Gītā and the Yoga Sūtra speak a shared language here. Teachers say these three are like weeds that must be pulled from the field before the seed of wisdom can grow. Modern psychology identifies the same three as powerful drivers of behaviour.

🌱 For today

Fear, clinging and anger: when one is gone, the others often weaken too. Watch for fear, clinging and anger in your speech today. Even noticing them begins to loosen them.

Celebrate gently and grieve gently. Don’t let either carry you away. Love without clinging is freer and lasts longer. Practise it in small things: enjoy, then let go.

ŚLOKA 2.70 · DEEP DIVE

LIKE THE OCEAN

🔎 In context

A great image. Rivers pour into the sea; it does not overflow, nor dry in summer. The verse is in triṣṭubh metre.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators say desires still “enter” the sage, through ordinary life, but they do not disturb him; they are absorbed. The contrast is with the kāma-kāmī, one who desires desires, always restless. The ocean image is one of the Gītā’s most beautiful. Rivers pour in from every side; the ocean does not rise or overflow. In dry seasons it does not shrink. Commentators say the sage is like that: experiences and objects come, but do not disturb his fullness. Śaṅkara says the sage gains peace because his joy does not depend on what flows in. Rāmānuja says it is because he enjoys the self. Kāma-kāmī, “desirer of desires”, describes someone who wants the wanting itself: always restless, never full. The contrast is between depth and thirst.

🌱 For today

Let wants come and go like rivers into a deep sea. Depth, not emptiness, is the goal. Be deep enough that life’s rivers can flow in without flooding you. Depth comes from inner contentment, not from more inflow.

ŚLOKA 2.71 · DEEP DIVE

WITHOUT “MINE”, WITHOUT “I”

🔎 In context

The cure for chapter 1: Dhṛtarāṣṭra began with “mine”; Kṛṣṇa ends with “without mine, without I”.

📜 Looking deeper

Two great words: nirmama and nirahaṅkāra. They are the heart of the Gītā’s ethics and return in 12.13 and 18.53. Commentators say “I” and “mine” are the root of all bondage. Nirmama, without “mine”, and nirahaṅkāra, without “I”, are the Gītā’s twin keys to freedom. They return in 3.30, 12.13 and 18.53. Commentators note the beautiful symmetry with chapter 1. The Gītā’s first speaker, Dhṛtarāṣṭra, said māmakāḥ, “mine” (1.1). Chapter 2 ends with nirmamaḥ, “without mine”. Śaṅkara takes this as the renunciant’s ideal: one who lives on what comes, owning nothing. Rāmānuja says it means not regarding the body and its belongings as oneself or one’s own. Gandhi saw in it the root of trusteeship: hold what you have for others, not as owner. Teachers say such a person walks through the world like the wind, touching everything and owning nothing. Commentators note carati, “moves about”: the sage is active, not withdrawn. Many saints lived this way, wandering, serving and teaching without home or possessions.

🌱 For today

Try saying “ours” instead of “mine”, and see what changes. That is a start. Try a day of noticing every “my” you say. Some are harmless; some carry a weight you can set down.

ŚLOKA 2.72 · DEEP DIVE

THE STATE OF BRAHMAN

🔎 In context

The chapter ends with the goal: the state of Brahman, freedom from delusion, and the peace of Brahman at death.

📜 Looking deeper

Brahma-nirvāṇa appears here first and returns in 5.24–26. Commentators note “even at the end”: the state reached late in life still frees. Chapter 2 began with Arjuna deluded; it ends with the promise of a state beyond delusion. Brahma-nirvāṇa is a rare compound, found in the Gītā here and in 5.24–26. Nirvāṇa means “blown out”, as a flame goes out. Buddhists use it for the end of craving, and the Gītā uses it with Brahman: the calm extinction of the ego in the fullness of Brahman. Commentators of all schools read it as liberation. Śaṅkara says even one who reaches this late in life is freed. Rāmānuja says the state is the self’s steady vision, leading to the Lord. The chapter colophon names it Sāṅkhya Yoga. Many teachers say that if one reads only chapter 2, one has the Gītā in brief.

🌱 For today

It is never too late. Peace reached even at the last moment is real peace. Whatever your age, the path is open. Each step toward a calm, unselfish mind is a step toward this peace.

↑ 2.72 · The goal: the peace of Brahman.
