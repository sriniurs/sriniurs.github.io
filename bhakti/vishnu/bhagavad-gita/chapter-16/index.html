DIVINE AND DEMONIC

BHAGAVAD GĪTĀ · CHAPTER 16 · DAIVĀSURA SAMPAD VIBHĀGA YOGA
Two natures, three gates · every śloka explained
HOW THIS WORKS: The whole chapter in one go for chanting, then every śloka on two pages: first the chant, a picture, what it says and the key words; then a deep dive into its context, how the great teachers read it, and what it means for us today.

WHERE WE ARE

In Chapter 15 Kṛṣṇa revealed himself as Puruṣottama, the Supreme Person.

Chapter 16 is about character. Kṛṣṇa lists 26 divine qualities, from fearlessness and truth to forgiveness and humility, and then describes the demonic nature from the inside: “this is mine, I killed him, who is equal to me?” He names the three gates of hell, desire, anger and greed, and tells Arjuna: you are born with the divine.

ŚLOKA 16.1 · DEEP DIVE

DIVINE QUALITIES · I

🔎 In context

Chapter 16 begins a famous list of 26 divine qualities. Chapter 16 is named Daivāsura-Sampad-Vibhāga Yoga, the yoga of distinguishing the divine and demonic endowments. Sampad means wealth: the qualities are a person’s true riches or debts.

📜 Looking deeper

Fearlessness heads the list. Commentators say it is first because fear blocks every other virtue; one who trusts in God and knows the Self has nothing to fear. These qualities return in many Hindu prayers and ethics texts. Śaṅkara says fearlessness comes first because one who knows the Self has nothing to fear; the Taittirīya Upaniṣad (2.9) says the knower of Brahman’s bliss fears nothing. Rāmānuja says it means freedom from fear of losing what one loves, by trusting the Lord. Gandhi called abhaya the first requirement of a satyāgrahī. Vivekananda called fearlessness, abhīḥ, the central message of the Upaniṣads, and told young Indians that weakness was the root of every sin. Teachers describe the fearless person as one who has jumped into the river of the Self and found that it does not drown. Commentators note that dāna, giving, also appears early in the list: the divine nature is open-handed.

🌱 For today

Courage, purity and honesty: begin with these and others follow. Courage, purity and honesty: begin with these, and the other qualities follow.

↑ 16.1 · First of all: fearlessness.

ŚLOKA 16.2 · DEEP DIVE

DIVINE QUALITIES · II

🔎 In context

More qualities: non-violence, truth, compassion, gentleness.

📜 Looking deeper

Apaiśuna, not back-biting, is a striking inclusion. Hrī, a healthy sense of shame before doing wrong. Commentators: these are everyday ethics, not only for monks. Ahiṃsā and satya, non-violence and truth, were the twin pillars of Gandhi’s life. Patañjali lists them as the first two yamas (Yoga Sūtra 2.30, 2.35–36). Apaiśuna, not slandering, and acāpala, not fidgeting or being fickle, are small everyday virtues. Commentators note how practical the list is: one can begin any of these today. Hrī, modesty, is the healthy shame that stops wrongdoing. Commentators note dayā bhūteṣu, “compassion for beings”, and aloluptva, “freedom from greed”, side by side: compassion grows as greed shrinks. The Jain tradition, with its strict non-violence, sees these as one virtue. Many Hindu households begin the day by feeding birds or cows, a small daily practice of compassion for beings.

🌱 For today

Don’t speak ill of others. That one practice changes relationships. Don’t speak ill of anyone today. That one practice changes relationships.

ŚLOKA 16.3 · DEEP DIVE

DIVINE QUALITIES · III

🔎 In context

The list of 26 ends with forgiveness and humility.

📜 Looking deeper

Note tejas, vigour, sits beside kṣamā, forgiveness: the divine nature is strong and gentle at once. Commentators call these qualities a “wealth” (sampat): the true treasure. Twenty-six qualities in three verses. Commentators note the balance of tejas, energy and radiance, with kṣamā, forgiveness: the divine nature is not weak. Adroha, not wishing harm to anyone. Nātimānitā, “not too much pride”: some self-respect is fine. Many Hindu schools teach these qualities to children; Vinoba Bhave wrote on them at length. The list matches the marks of knowledge in 13.7–11. Commentators note that the word abhijāta, “born to”, suggests these qualities are a natural inheritance for some, the fruit of past lives. But Madhusūdana adds that they can also be cultivated. Benjamin Franklin famously kept a chart of thirteen virtues and worked on one each week; the list here could be used the same way.

🌱 For today

Strength and forgiveness belong together. Strength and forgiveness belong together. Practise both.

ŚLOKA 16.4 · DEEP DIVE

DEMONIC QUALITIES

🔎 In context

Six demonic qualities, the opposite of the divine list.

📜 Looking deeper

The “demonic” (āsurī) is a nature, not a species. Commentators: anyone can show these traits; anyone can change. Hypocrisy comes first, as fearlessness did for the divine. Six demonic qualities against twenty-six divine. Commentators note hypocrisy comes first, as fearlessness did on the divine side. Asura in the Vedas originally meant a powerful lord; later it came to mean the opponents of the gods. In the Gītā it is a character, not a species. The Chāndogya Upaniṣad (8.7–12) tells how Indra and the asura Virocana both studied with Prajāpati. Virocana left thinking the body was the self; Indra stayed and learned the truth. Commentators note pāruṣya, harshness of speech, as a demonic quality: words can wound like weapons. The Mahābhārata gives Duryodhana’s insults to Draupadī in the dice hall as a terrible example, and they became one of the causes of the war. Darpa, arrogance, was the downfall of Rāvaṇa in the Rāmāyaṇa.

🌱 For today

Watch for hypocrisy and harshness in yourself first, before seeing it in others. Watch for hypocrisy and harshness in yourself first, before you see them in others.

YOU ARE BORN WITH THE DIVINE

🔎 In context

Kṛṣṇa reassures Arjuna.

📜 Looking deeper

A tender moment: Arjuna, hearing the list, may worry about himself. Kṛṣṇa says “do not grieve” (mā śucaḥ, also in 18.66). Commentators: the very worry is a sign of the divine nature. Mā śucaḥ, “do not grieve”, appears here and in 18.66, the Gītā’s final word of comfort. Commentators find this moment tender. Arjuna, hearing the demonic list, may fear he has some of these traits, after his anger at the Kauravas. Kṛṣṇa reassures him. Śaṅkara says Arjuna’s readiness to fight in a just war is not demonic, because it is duty. Rāmānuja says the Lord’s love speaks here. Commentators note that Kṛṣṇa calls Arjuna Pāṇḍava here, son of Pāṇḍu, a reminder of his noble lineage. Madhusūdana says Arjuna’s worry itself proves his divine nature, since the demonic do not worry about being demonic. Many readers find this verse comforting in times of self-doubt.

ŚLOKA 16.6 · DEEP DIVE

TWO KINDS OF BEINGS

🔎 In context

Kṛṣṇa now describes the demonic nature in detail.

📜 Looking deeper

Why describe it at length? Commentators: to recognise it and avoid it, in ourselves above all. Commentators say the divine nature has been described throughout the Gītā: in the sthita-prajña (2.55–72), the devotee (12.13–20) and the guṇātīta (14.22–25). The demonic nature is now described in 16.7–20 so that it can be recognised. The Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad (1.3.1) says gods and asuras are both children of Prajāpati, competing in the world. Many teachers say both are within each person. Commentators note that Kṛṣṇa speaks of two kinds of creation, bhūta-sargau. Śaṅkara says the same person can show both natures at different times. The Chāndogya Upaniṣad (1.2) tells how gods and demons fought, and the demons spoiled every sense with evil, but could not touch the vital breath. The deepest life in us remains pure.

🌱 For today

Knowing the shape of a trap helps you avoid it. Knowing the shape of a trap helps you avoid it. Study your own lower tendencies with honesty.

ŚLOKA 16.7 · DEEP DIVE

NO TRUTH, NO PURITY

🔎 In context

The demonic lack a moral compass.

📜 Looking deeper

Pravṛtti and nivṛtti: action and restraint. Commentators: the demonic nature cannot tell when to act and when to hold back. Pravṛtti and nivṛtti are also the two great paths of Hindu life: active engagement and withdrawal. Here they mean knowing when to act and when to restrain oneself. Commentators say the demonic nature lacks this discernment. Śaṅkara says they do not know right and wrong as taught by scripture. Rāmānuja says they do not distinguish the means to good from the means to harm. Commentators note śauca, purity, and ācāra, right conduct, as basic marks missing in the demonic nature. Indian tradition gives great weight to ācāra, daily conduct, often more than to belief. The Manusmṛti calls right conduct the highest dharma. Without it, says the Gītā, truth too disappears.

🌱 For today

Knowing when to say no is as important as knowing when to say yes. Knowing when to say no is as important as knowing when to say yes.

ŚLOKA 16.8 · DEEP DIVE

“THE WORLD HAS NO GOD”

🔎 In context

The demonic worldview: no meaning, no God, only desire.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators link this to materialist schools of the time (Lokāyata). The Gītā’s concern is ethical: a worldview with desire as the only purpose leads to cruelty. The Lokāyata or Cārvāka school taught that only the senses give knowledge, the body is the self, and pleasure is the goal. A famous line attributed to them: “while you live, live happily; borrow and drink ghee; once the body is ash, how can it return?”. Commentators of all schools criticised this view. The Gītā’s concern is ethical: a view of the world with only desire at its centre tends toward cruelty and greed. Commentators note that the Gītā does not attack all materialist thought, but the conclusion that desire alone matters. Many later Indian thinkers engaged seriously with Cārvāka arguments, and some modern scholars see them as an early form of scepticism. The Gītā’s concern is practical: a world without meaning tends to become a world without restraint.

🌱 For today

What we believe about the world shapes how we treat it. What we believe about the world shapes how we treat it. Notice your own deepest assumptions.

↑ 16.8 · “Desire is all there is.”

🔎 In context

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators say such a view, put into action, harms others. Śaṅkara says they become enemies of the world because they act only for themselves. The epic gives examples: Duryodhana’s schemes, Śakuni’s tricks, Kaṃsa’s killing of infants. The verse warns that ideas have consequences. Commentators note naṣṭātmānaḥ, “lost souls”: the demonic have lost touch with their own Self. Madhusūdana says this is the deepest loss, deeper than any outer harm. History shows how ideas that deny all values can lead to great cruelty. The Gītā warns that the world itself suffers when such views gain power.

ŚLOKA 16.10 · DEEP DIVE

INSATIABLE DESIRE

🔎 In context

Desire that cannot be filled drives them.

📜 Looking deeper

Duṣpūra, insatiable: compare 3.39. Commentators: the demonic nature is not about having desires but being ruled by endless ones. Duṣpūra, hard to fill, recalls 3.39, where desire is a fire that cannot be filled. Commentators note aśuci-vrata, impure vows: the demonic also make resolutions, but for selfish ends. Śaṅkara says they adopt wrong ideas because of delusion. The story of King Yayāti, who borrowed his son’s youth to continue enjoying and still found no end to desire, is a classic illustration. Commentators note mohād gṛhītvā, “seizing through delusion”: wrong views are grasped, not reasoned. Śaṅkara says delusion makes them hold false ideas firmly. Modern psychology speaks of confirmation bias, the tendency to hold on to what we already believe. The Gītā’s remedy is sattva: clear seeing, humility and good company.

🌱 For today

Notice when “enough” never comes. That is the warning sign. Notice when “enough” never comes. That is the warning sign.

ENDLESS ANXIETY
