THREE KINDS OF FAITH

BHAGAVAD GĪTĀ · CHAPTER 17 · ŚRADDHĀTRAYA VIBHĀGA YOGA
You are what you have faith in · every śloka explained
HOW THIS WORKS: The whole chapter in one go for chanting, then every śloka on two pages: first the chant, a picture, what it says and the key words; then a deep dive into its context, how the great teachers read it, and what it means for us today.

WHERE WE ARE

In Chapter 16 Kṛṣṇa described the divine and demonic natures and said: let scripture be your guide.

Arjuna asks: what about sincere people who don’t follow scripture? Kṛṣṇa answers with the three guṇas: faith, food, sacrifice, austerity and giving each come in sāttvika, rājasika and tāmasika forms. This is the Gītā’s most practical chapter, ending with the sacred words oṃ tat sat.

FAITH WITHOUT SCRIPTURE?
arjuna uvācaye śāstravidhimutsṛjya yajante śraddhayā’nvitāḥteṣāṃ niṣṭhā tu kā kṛṣṇa sattvamāho rajastamaḥ ‖1‖
Arjuna said: Those who set aside the rules of scripture yet worship with faith, Kṛṣṇa: what is their standing? Is it sattva, rajas or tamas?
WordMeans
ye śāstra-vidhim utsṛjyathose who set aside scripture’s rules
yajante śraddhayā anvitāḥyet worship with faith
teṣām niṣṭhā tu kā kṛṣṇawhat is their standing, Kṛṣṇa?

ŚLOKA 17.1 · DEEP DIVE

FAITH WITHOUT SCRIPTURE?

🔎 In context

Chapter 16 ended: let scripture be your guide. Arjuna asks about sincere people who don’t know or follow scripture. Chapter 17 is named Śraddhā-Traya-Vibhāga Yoga, the yoga of the three kinds of faith. It follows directly from 16.23–24, where Kṛṣṇa made scripture the guide. Arjuna immediately thinks of the many sincere people who never read scripture at all.

📜 Looking deeper

A thoughtful, humane question: what about ordinary believers who worship sincerely but outside the rules? Kṛṣṇa’s answer is not a simple yes or no; he says faith itself has three kinds. Commentators find this chapter very practical. Śaṅkara understands Arjuna to mean people who do not know the scriptural rules, not those who reject them. Rāmānuja adds that the question is about the worth of faith itself. Kṛṣṇa does not answer with “good” or “bad”. He says faith takes on the colour of the person’s nature, and then spends the rest of the chapter showing how to tell the colours apart. Many modern readers see this as one of the most humane passages in the Gītā, because it takes the ordinary believer seriously. Commentators note that Arjuna calls Kṛṣṇa by name here, a sign of earnest concern. Madhusūdana says the question is about those who neglect the rules through ignorance or laziness, but still worship sincerely. The answer will not be a simple verdict but a tool for self-examination.

🌱 For today

Sincerity matters, but so does the quality of what we put our faith in. Your sincerity matters. So does the quality of what you trust. Look at both.

↑ 17.1 · Sincere but outside the rules?

ŚLOKA 17.2

FAITH IS THREEFOLD
śrībhagavān uvācatrividhā bhavati śraddhā dehināṃ sā svabhāvajāsāttvikī rājasī caiva tāmasī ceti tāṃ śṛṇu ‖2‖
The Lord said: The faith of embodied beings, born of their own nature, is of three kinds: sāttvika, rājasika and tāmasika. Hear about it.
WordMeans
tri-vidhā bhavati śraddhāfaith is of three kinds
dehinām sā svabhāva-jāof the embodied, born of their nature
sāttvikī rājasī ca evasāttvika and rājasika
tāmasī ca iti tām śṛṇuand tāmasika: hear it
Faith has three colours.

ŚLOKA 17.2 · DEEP DIVE

FAITH IS THREEFOLD

🔎 In context

Faith takes the colour of one’s nature. The guṇas were explained in chapter 14 as forces in the mind. Here Kṛṣṇa turns them into a practical test that can be applied to anything people do. The chapter will apply it to faith, worship, food, sacrifice, austerity and giving.

📜 Looking deeper

This chapter applies the guṇas of chapter 14 to faith, food, sacrifice, austerity and giving. Commentators call it the Gītā’s practical guide to daily life. Commentators note the phrase svabhāva-jā, born of one’s own nature. Faith is not chosen in a vacuum; it grows out of temperament and past habits (saṃskāras). Śaṅkara says the nature formed by past lives decides the kind of faith one has now. Rāmānuja says the guṇa that dominates in a person colours his faith. This is hopeful as well as sobering: since nature can be changed by food, company and practice, faith can be refined. Commentators note that faith is named first among the things coloured by the guṇas, before food, sacrifice, austerity and charity. One traditional image compares faith to water, which takes the colour of the ground it flows over. The same longing for the sacred can become pure, restless or dark depending on the heart it passes through.

🌱 For today

What we trust in reflects who we are. Notice the quality of your faith. What you trust reflects who you are, and who you are can grow. Start with small daily choices.

↑ 17.2 · Faith has three colours.

ŚLOKA 17.3

YOU ARE YOUR FAITH
sattvānurūpā sarvasya śraddhā bhavati bhārataśraddhāmayo’yaṃ puruṣo yo yacchraddhaḥ sa eva saḥ ‖3‖
“The faith of each person is in keeping with his inner nature, Bhārata. A person is made of faith; whatever his faith is, that he is.”
WordMeans
sattva-anurūpā sarvasyaaccording to the inner nature of each
śraddhā bhavati bhāratais faith
śraddhā-mayaḥ ayam puruṣaḥthis person is made of faith
yaḥ yat-śraddhaḥ saḥ eva saḥas his faith is, so is he
You are what you have faith in.

ŚLOKA 17.3 · DEEP DIVE

YOU ARE YOUR FAITH

🔎 In context

A famous line: a person is made of faith. This verse is one of the most quoted in the Gītā. It says that a person is not only what he thinks, but what he deeply trusts. The same idea appears in the Chāndogya Upaniṣad (3.14.1): kratumayaḥ puruṣaḥ, a person is made of resolve; as one resolves in this world, so one becomes hereafter.

📜 Looking deeper

Śraddhā-mayo’yaṃ puruṣaḥ: we become what we deeply trust in. Commentators note “sattva” here means one’s inner being, not only the guṇa. The Chāndogya Upaniṣad says something similar: a person is made of resolve (kratu). Here sattva is read by Śaṅkara as the mind or inner being, not the guṇa. Rāmānuja reads it as the inner organ shaped by past impressions. Vivekananda often quoted the line to young people. His point was that people become what they believe themselves to be, so they should believe in their strength. Modern psychology speaks of self-fulfilling beliefs and mindsets in a similar spirit. Commentators note sattvānurūpā, “in keeping with one’s inner being”: faith is not borrowed but grows from within. Madhusūdana says one can know a person by their faith. William James, the American psychologist, wrote that belief helps create the fact; the Gītā’s claim is older and deeper, that faith shapes the person.

🌱 For today

What you trust most is shaping who you become. Notice what you most deeply trust in. Without noticing, you are becoming it.

↑ 17.3 · You are what you have faith in.

ŚLOKA 17.4

WHOM THEY WORSHIP
yajante sāttvikā devānyakṣarakṣāṃsi rājasāḥpretānbhūtagaṇāṃścānye yajante tāmasā janāḥ ‖4‖
“The sāttvika worship the gods; the rājasika, yakṣas and rākṣasas; and the others, the tāmasika, worship ghosts and hosts of spirits.”
WordMeans
yajante sāttvikāḥ devānthe sāttvika worship gods
yakṣa-rakṣāṃsi rājasāḥthe rājasika, yakṣas and rākṣasas
pretān bhūta-gaṇān ca anyethe others, ghosts and spirits
yajante tāmasāḥ janāḥthe tāmasika worship
We worship what we resemble.

ŚLOKA 17.4 · DEEP DIVE

WHOM THEY WORSHIP

🔎 In context

What people worship reveals their guṇa. Yakṣas are nature spirits associated with wealth; Kubera is their king. Rākṣasas are beings of force and violence, such as Rāvaṇa in the Rāmāyaṇa. Pretas are restless spirits of the dead, and bhūtas are elemental spirits.

📜 Looking deeper

Yakṣas are beings of wealth and power; rākṣasas of force. Commentators: the object of worship mirrors desires: light, power, or dark fears. Commentators explain that the object of worship mirrors the worshipper’s desires. Those who want clarity and goodness turn to the gods, the shining powers. Those driven by ambition worship power and wealth. Those ruled by fear and darkness turn to ghosts and spirits, often in the hope of harming others or protecting themselves by magic. Śaṅkara notes that even within one tradition, people can worship the same deity with very different motives. The verse invites honest self-examination rather than judging others. Commentators note that the verse does not condemn worshippers but describes them. Śaṅkara says knowing the kind of worship tells one the guṇa at work. Village shrines to local spirits, the worship of wealth and power, and the worship of the gods are all found in Indian religious life. The Gītā offers a way to see which is which.

🌱 For today

Look at what you most admire. It tells you about yourself. Look at what you most admire or pray for. It tells you a great deal about yourself.

↑ 17.4 · We worship what we resemble.

ŚLOKA 17.5

HARSH AUSTERITIES
aśāstravihitaṃ ghoraṃ tapyante ye tapo janāḥdambhāhaṅkārasaṃyuktāḥ kāmarāgabalānvitāḥ ‖5‖
“Those who practise terrible austerities not enjoined by scripture, given to hypocrisy and ego, driven by desire, attachment and the strength of force,”
WordMeans
aśāstra-vihitam ghoramterrible, not enjoined by scripture
tapyante ye tapaḥ janāḥthose people who practise austerity
dambha-ahaṅkāra-saṃyuktāḥwith hypocrisy and ego
kāma-rāga-bala-anvitāḥwith desire, attachment and force
Harsh austerity for show.

ŚLOKA 17.5 · DEEP DIVE

HARSH AUSTERITIES

🔎 In context

Some torture themselves for show. Ancient India had many kinds of extreme ascetics: some stood on one leg for years, some lay on beds of thorns, some fasted to starvation. The epics also tell of demons like Rāvaṇa and Hiraṇyakaśipu who did fierce austerities to win powers they later misused.

📜 Looking deeper

The Gītā rejects harsh, self-harming asceticism (compare 6.16–17). The sentence continues. The Gītā consistently rejects self-torture. In 6.16–17 it praises moderation in food and sleep. The Buddha, after years of extreme fasting, also rejected it and taught the middle way. Commentators note the motives named here: hypocrisy, ego, desire, attachment and the pride of strength. Śaṅkara says such austerity, done against scripture and from ego, is demonic in nature. The sentence continues into the next verse, which gives the reason. Commentators note ghora tapas, “terrible austerity”, not sanctioned by scripture. Madhusūdana lists examples: fasting unto death without cause, piercing the body, standing in fire. Indian law today forbids many dangerous practices. The Gītā’s rejection of them was a moral stand long before modern law.

🌱 For today

Discipline should strengthen, not harm. Beware of austerity as ego. Discipline should make you stronger and kinder. If it only makes you proud or ill, it has gone wrong.

↑ 17.5 · Harsh austerity for show.

ŚLOKA 17.6

TORMENTING ME WITHIN
karṣayantaḥ śarīrasthaṃ bhūtagrāmamacetasaḥmāṃ caivāntaḥśarīrasthaṃ tānviddhyāsuraniścayān ‖6‖
“…senseless, tormenting the elements in the body and me who dwells within the body: know them to be of demonic resolve.”
WordMeans
karśayantaḥ śarīra-sthamtormenting what is in the body
bhūta-grāmam acetasaḥthe group of elements, senseless
mām ca eva antaḥ-śarīra-sthamand me, dwelling within the body

ŚLOKA 17.6 · DEEP DIVE

TORMENTING ME WITHIN

🔎 In context

Harming the body harms God within it. The body, in the Gītā’s view, is a city where the Lord dwells (5.13, 13.22, 15.15). To torture it is therefore to torment the Lord within.

📜 Looking deeper

A remarkable teaching: the body is a temple. Commentators: self-torture is not holiness; it disrespects the Lord dwelling within. Commentators are struck by this teaching. Śaṅkara says such ascetics trouble the body made of the elements, and also the Lord who witnesses within. Rāmānuja says they disregard the Lord’s command to care for the body as an instrument of worship. Many later teachers said the same. The Tirumantiram of Tirumūlar says that by caring for the body he cared for the soul, since the body is the temple of God. This is not a licence for indulgence, but a call to respect. Commentators note bhūta-grāmam, “the group of elements”, that make up the body. Śaṅkara says tormenting them torments the Lord within, who is their witness. Many modern teachers apply this to self-harm of any kind: neglecting health, overwork, harsh self-criticism. Respect for the body is respect for its indweller.

🌱 For today

Treat your body with respect. God lives there. Care for your body as you would for a temple. The Divine lives there.

↑ 17.6 · Self-harm harms God within.

ŚLOKA 17.7

FOOD, SACRIFICE, AUSTERITY, GIFT
āhārastvapi sarvasya trividho bhavati priyaḥyajñastapastathā dānaṃ teṣāṃ bhedamimaṃ śṛṇu ‖7‖
“Even the food that is dear to each is of three kinds, and so are sacrifice, austerity and charity. Hear the distinction among them.”
WordMeans

tri-vidhaḥ bhavati priyaḥis of three kinds, as dear

Food too has three kinds.

ŚLOKA 17.7 · DEEP DIVE

FOOD, SACRIFICE, AUSTERITY, GIFT

🔎 In context

Kṛṣṇa will classify food, sacrifice, austerity and giving. After faith and worship, Kṛṣṇa turns to the most ordinary act of daily life: eating. Then come sacrifice, austerity and giving, the three religious duties praised in 18.5.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators enjoy that food begins the list: the Gītā cares about daily life. What we like to eat reveals our nature, and shapes it. Commentators enjoy that food begins the list. Indian tradition has always linked food and mind. The Chāndogya Upaniṣad (6.5.4) says the subtlest part of food becomes mind, and (7.26.2) that when food is pure, the mind becomes pure. Śaṅkara says the foods one likes reveal one’s guṇa. Āyurveda developed this into detailed guidance. The teaching works both ways: what we like shows who we are, and what we eat shapes who we become. Commentators note that āhāras tv api sarvasya, “even food, of everyone”, is the first example: what we eat is the most frequent choice we make. Gandhi experimented with diet throughout his life, convinced that food shaped the mind. The Gītā’s three classes of food influenced his thinking.

🌱 For today

Even your food choices are part of your spiritual life. Look at your plate today as part of your inner life, not separate from it.

↑ 17.7 · Food too has three kinds.

ŚLOKA 17.8

SĀTTVIKA FOOD
āyuḥsattvabalārogyasukhaprītivivardhanāḥrasyāḥ snigdhāḥ sthirā hṛdyā āhārāḥ sāttvikapriyāḥ ‖8‖
“Foods that increase life, purity, strength, health, happiness and cheerfulness, that are tasty, smooth, substantial and agreeable, are dear to the sāttvika.”
WordMeans
āyuḥ-sattva-bala-ārogyalife, purity, strength, health
sukha-prīti-vivardhanāḥincreasing happiness and cheer
rasyāḥ snigdhāḥ sthirāḥ hṛdyāḥtasty, smooth, substantial, pleasing
āhārāḥ sāttvika-priyāḥfoods dear to the sāttvika
Nourishing, fresh, cheering.

ŚLOKA 17.8 · DEEP DIVE

SĀTTVIKA FOOD

🔎 In context

Good food nourishes body and mind. Sāttvika food, as described in later Āyurvedic and yogic texts, includes fresh grains, fruits, vegetables, milk, ghee, nuts and honey, prepared simply and eaten in moderation.

📜 Looking deeper

Note the positive tone: tasty and pleasing food is sāttvika. Commentators list fresh grains, fruits, vegetables, milk and ghee. The aim is health and cheerfulness, not deprivation. Notice the positive tone of this verse: tasty, pleasing, substantial food is sāttvika. Commentators stress that the Gītā does not ask for bland or joyless eating. The six qualities named, increasing life, purity, strength, health, happiness and cheerfulness, are effects to watch for after a meal. Śaṅkara explains “smooth” as containing natural oils, and “substantial” as nourishing for a long time. The verse is also the basis of much traditional temple cooking, offered first to the deity as prasāda. Commentators note āyuḥ, life or longevity, named first among the effects of sāttvika food. Modern nutrition science stresses fresh vegetables, fruits, whole grains and moderation for long life, which agrees with much of this verse. Temple kitchens in South India still cook traditional sāttvika food for offering and distribution.

🌱 For today

Eat fresh, wholesome, tasty food. It shapes your mind as well as body. After a meal, notice: do I feel lighter, clearer, more cheerful? That is a good sign.

↑ 17.8 · Nourishing, fresh, cheering.

Commentators explain each word in the following verses: Oṃ (17.24), tat (17.25) and sat (17.26–27). Together they name Brahman and are used to sanctify acts. Tat points to Brahman, as in “tat tvam asi”. Commentators: saying “tat” is saying “not mine, but That’s”. Commentators explain that saying “tat” is like saying “not mine, but That’s”. It dedicates the action and its fruit to the Supreme. Śaṅkara says it is used by seekers of liberation who act without seeking reward. Rāmānuja says it reminds the doer that all belongs to the Lord. 🔎 In context

The chapter ends: without faith, acts are empty. Asat, the opposite of sat, means unreal or worthless. The chapter that began with faith (17.1) ends with faith.

📜 Looking deeper

This answers Arjuna’s opening question from the other side: faith is essential, and its quality matters. Commentators note the chapter begins and ends with śraddhā. Commentators note the careful symmetry: Arjuna asked about those who worship with faith but outside the rules; Kṛṣṇa ends by saying that without faith, even correct acts are empty.
