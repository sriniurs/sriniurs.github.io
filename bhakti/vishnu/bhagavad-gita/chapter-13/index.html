WHERE WE ARE

In Chapter 12 Kṛṣṇa described the devotee dear to him.

The final six chapters turn to knowledge. Chapter 13 makes one great distinction: the body (with mind and feelings) is the field; the Self is the knower of the field. Kṛṣṇa lists twenty marks of real knowledge, beginning with humility, then describes Brahman, the light of lights in every heart, and ends: who sees the Lord equally in all, truly sees.

ŚLOKA 13.1 · DEEP DIVE

THE FIELD AND ITS KNOWER

🔎 In context

The third part of the Gītā, on knowledge, begins. Body = field; the one aware of it = knower of the field. Chapter 13 is named Kṣetra-Kṣetrajña-Vibhāga Yoga, the yoga of distinguishing the field and its knower. It opens the last six chapters, often called the jñāna ṣaṭka.

📜 Looking deeper

Some editions add a verse before this, in which Arjuna asks about prakṛti, puruṣa, the field, the knower, knowledge and the knowable (shown as 13.0 in the In One Go pages). The Gītā Press text omits it, keeping the total at 700. The image of the field is apt: things grow in it according to what is sown, as actions bear fruit in the body. A field is where seeds are sown and crops reaped; the body is where actions are sown and their fruits reaped. Śaṅkara says the knower is pure consciousness, the witness. Rāmānuja says the knower is the individual self, distinct from the body. Commentators note the agricultural image: a field yields what is sown, according to care and season. Teachers develop the image, describing the body as a field ploughed by desire and harvested by karma.

🌱 For today

You have a body; you are aware of it. Notice the difference: the one aware is not the same as what it is aware of. Sit for a moment and notice: there is the body, and there is the one aware of it.

ŚLOKA 13.2 · DEEP DIVE

I AM THE KNOWER IN ALL FIELDS

🔎 In context

Kṛṣṇa says he is the knower in every body.

📜 Looking deeper

A key verse for Vedānta. Śaṅkara reads it as the identity of the individual knower with the Lord: one consciousness in all. Rāmānuja reads it as the Lord dwelling as the inner controller (antaryāmin) of each distinct self. Both agree real knowledge is knowing this distinction between body and knower. This verse is central to the debate between the schools. Śaṅkara: the individual knower and the Lord are one; difference is due to ignorance. Rāmānuja: the Lord is the inner self of every individual self, as the self is the inner self of the body. Madhva: the Lord dwells in every field as controller, eternally distinct from the individual knower. All agree knowledge of this is true knowledge. Ramana Maharshi often taught from this kind of verse. His method was to ask “who am I?” and trace the sense of “I” back to the knower that is never an object. Commentators note the phrase “in all fields”: the same knower in every body. For Advaita this is identity; for the theistic schools it is the Lord’s presence in all.

🌱 For today

The same awareness that looks through your eyes looks through every eye. That can change how you see others. The awareness that looks through your eyes looks through every eye. Let that change how you see others.

ŚLOKA 13.3 · DEEP DIVE

HEAR IT IN BRIEF

🔎 In context

Kṛṣṇa outlines what he will explain.

📜 Looking deeper

Six topics: the field, its nature, its changes, its origin, the knower, and his powers. Commentators note the systematic style of this chapter, closer to philosophy than to story. Commentators note that chapter 13 is the Gītā’s most philosophical chapter, close to the language of Sāṅkhya and the Upaniṣads. The outline here is followed in order: the field (13.5–6), its marks of knowledge (13.7–11), the knowable (13.12–17), and the relation of nature and spirit (13.19–34). Commentators note prabhāva, “power” or “glory”, of the knower: consciousness is not passive. It lights up the whole field. Śaṅkara says the powers of the knower are its limiting adjuncts, which make it seem to act. Rāmānuja says they are the self’s real qualities of knowing and acting, which the Lord supports.

🌱 For today

Clear questions make clear answers. Kṛṣṇa organises before he teaches. Before solving a problem, list its parts. Clarity of structure brings clarity of thought.

↑ 13.3 · Six things to understand.

ŚLOKA 13.4 · DEEP DIVE

AS THE SAGES SANG

🔎 In context

The teaching has been given before, in the Vedas and in reasoned texts.

📜 Looking deeper

“Brahma-sūtra” here may mean Bādarāyaṇa’s Brahma Sūtras or, more broadly, Upaniṣadic statements about Brahman. Commentators differ; Śaṅkara takes it as Upaniṣadic sayings. The Gītā places itself within a tradition of reasoned inquiry. The Brahma Sūtras of Bādarāyaṇa, the Upaniṣads and the Gītā form the prasthāna-traya, the three foundations of Vedānta; every major teacher wrote commentaries on all three. Rāmānuja takes brahma-sūtra as Bādarāyaṇa’s text. Some scholars think the Gītā predates the Sūtras in their present form; the Sūtras themselves seem to refer to the Gītā as smṛti. Commentators note chandobhiḥ, “in hymns”: the teaching is first sung, in the Vedas, then reasoned, in the Sūtras. The Indian tradition holds that scripture and reason should agree. Śaṅkara’s commentary on the Brahma Sūtras is one of the most influential works of Indian philosophy, and he cites the Gītā often within it.

🌱 For today

Good teaching agrees with both scripture and reason. Good teaching satisfies both reverence and reason. Look for both in what you learn.

ŚLOKA 13.5 · DEEP DIVE

WHAT THE FIELD IS MADE OF · I

🔎 In context

The field is made of the 24 principles of Sāṅkhya.

📜 Looking deeper

Count: 5 elements + ego + intellect + unmanifest nature = 8; 10 senses (5 of knowing, 5 of action) + mind = 11; 5 sense objects. Total 24. Commentators say even mind and ego are part of the field, not the knower. Classical Sāṅkhya, as in Īśvarakṛṣṇa’s Sāṅkhya Kārikā, counts twenty-five principles: twenty-four of matter and puruṣa, spirit. The Gītā lists the twenty-four as the field. The five senses of knowing are hearing, touch, sight, taste and smell; the five of action are speech, hands, feet, excretion and generation. Commentators stress that mind, intellect and ego are on the field’s side. Commentators note that avyakta, the unmanifest, is included in the field: even primal matter, the seed of the universe, is known and therefore not the knower. Modern readers sometimes compare this to the difference between the brain, which can be studied, and awareness itself, which does the studying. The Gītā places everything that can be observed in the field.

🌱 For today

Your thoughts and your sense of “I” are part of the field. The knower watches them. Your thoughts and your sense of “I” belong to the field. The knower watches them.

ŚLOKA 13.6 · DEEP DIVE

WHAT THE FIELD IS MADE OF · II

🔎 In context

Even feelings and awareness of the mind belong to the field.

📜 Looking deeper

Surprising: desire, pain, even cetanā (awareness as a mental state) are “field”. Commentators distinguish the mind’s awareness, a changing state, from the knower, pure unchanging consciousness. Saṅghāta, the aggregate, is the body as a combination of parts. The Nyāya school listed desire, aversion, pleasure and pain as marks of the self; the Gītā firmly places them in the field. Śaṅkara says they are known, so they cannot be the knower. Rāmānuja says they are the products of the field acting on the self. Commentators note dhṛti, steadfastness, listed as part of the field: even our strengths are modifications of nature. Śaṅkara says the knower is beyond both strengths and weaknesses. This can be freeing: one need not be proud of one’s steadiness or ashamed of one’s weaknesses; both are part of the field, to be cared for and used.

🌱 For today

Your moods are part of the field, not the knower. That gives some distance from them. Moods come and go; you notice them. That noticing is the first step to freedom from them.

ŚLOKA 13.7 · DEEP DIVE

WHAT KNOWLEDGE LOOKS LIKE · I

🔎 In context

Verses 13.7–11 list twenty marks of knowledge. Knowledge is a way of living, not only information.

📜 Looking deeper

This list is famous for defining knowledge by character. Humility comes first. Commentators say these are both means to knowledge and signs of it. Verses 7–11 list twenty marks of knowledge, and the list begins with humility. Amānitva is not thinking highly of oneself; adambhitva is not pretending to be what one is not. Ārjava, straightness, is honesty in thought, word and deed. Ācāryopāsana, serving the teacher, recalls 4.34. Gandhi and Vinoba made these qualities ashram vows. Commentators note that kṣānti, patience or forbearance, and ārjava, uprightness, are central virtues in many Indian lists. Śaṅkara’s Vivekacūḍāmaṇi names forbearance among the six qualities of the seeker. The order matters: humility first, because a proud mind cannot learn. Many teachers say this list is a better test of spiritual progress than any experience.

🌱 For today

Real knowledge shows in humility and kindness, not in cleverness. Real knowledge shows first in humility. Notice whether your learning makes you gentler.

ŚLOKA 13.8 · DEEP DIVE

WHAT KNOWLEDGE LOOKS LIKE · II

🔎 In context

Seeing life’s sorrows clearly is part of wisdom.

📜 Looking deeper

This recalls the Buddha’s four sights: old age, sickness, death and a renunciant. Commentators say clear seeing of suffering loosens attachment. The young prince Siddhārtha, the future Buddha, saw an old man, a sick man, a corpse and a renunciant, and left his palace. Commentators often draw the parallel. Śaṅkara explains anudarśana as reflecting again and again on these facts until they loosen attachment. Doctors and nurses who see suffering daily sometimes say this verse describes their education too. Commentators note that the verse includes anahaṅkāra, absence of ego, alongside seeing suffering. The two are linked: seeing that ageing, illness and death come to all removes the ego’s illusion of being special. The Buddha made the same observation the starting point of his teaching, and the Gītā here makes it part of knowledge.

🌱 For today

Facing ageing and illness honestly can make us wiser and kinder. Facing ageing and illness honestly makes us wiser and kinder. Don’t look away.

MANY WAYS TO SEE THE SELF

🔎 In context

Three paths to the same vision.

📜 Looking deeper

Meditation, knowledge and action: the paths of chapters 6, 2 and 3. Commentators: different temperaments, one goal. Three paths, each with a chapter of the Gītā: meditation (chapter 6), knowledge (chapter 2) and action (chapter 3). Commentators note the Gītā’s openness to different temperaments. Śaṅkara says the paths are for seekers at different stages. Rāmānuja says karma yoga leads to knowledge, and knowledge to meditation. The next verse adds a fourth path for those who simply trust. Commentators note that the three paths are named for seeing the Self, paśyanti: the goal is direct vision, not only belief. Śaṅkara says meditation is the direct means, knowledge and action the preparation. Rāmānuja says karma yoga leads to self-vision for most people. The Gītā honours every route that ends in seeing.

Find the path that suits you, and walk it. Find the path that suits your nature, and walk it steadily.

THOSE WHO HEAR AND WORSHIP

Even those who simply trust and follow cross over.

A generous verse for the ordinary believer. Commentators: listening to the wise with faith is itself a path. Commentators call this a verse for the ordinary believer. Śruti-parāyaṇa, “devoted to what is heard”: one who trusts and follows the wise. Śaṅkara says such people cross death gradually, through faith and practice. Sant Kabīr, who could not read or write, became one of India’s great spiritual poets by listening and practising. The verse honours the wisdom of the unlettered devotee.

You don’t need to know everything. Trust the wise and follow sincerely. You don’t need to know everything. Trust the wise, follow sincerely, and keep going.

ŚLOKA 13.26 · DEEP DIVE

FIELD MEETS KNOWER

🔎 In context

Every living thing is consciousness joined with matter.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators: the union is not like two objects joining, but like light falling on a surface, or a reflection. Life is where awareness meets nature. Commentators explain that the union is not physical joining. Śaṅkara calls it mutual superimposition: the qualities of the body are wrongly attributed to the Self, and the consciousness of the Self to the body, like a crystal appearing red near a red flower. Rāmānuja calls it a real but temporary association of self and matter, caused by karma. Commentators note that every being, moving and unmoving, from plants to people, is a union of field and knower. Jain philosophy also holds that plants and even elements have souls. The Gītā’s view gives every living thing dignity. Śaṅkara says the union is like a rope mistaken for a snake; Rāmānuja says it is real but temporary.

🌱 For today

Every living being is awareness in a body. Respect both. Every living being is awareness in a body. Respect both sides of that union.

WHO SEES TRULY

True vision: the same Lord in all.

“He who sees, sees” (compare 5.5). Commentators of all schools prize this verse as the essence of right vision. Yaḥ paśyati sa paśyati repeats 5.5. Commentators of all schools treasure this verse. Śaṅkara says the one Self is seen in all bodies. Rāmānuja says the Lord is seen as the inner controller of all. Madhva says the Lord is seen as present everywhere. The phrase “the imperishable in the perishing” recalls 2.18 and 8.20. Commentators note vinaśyatsv avinaśyantam, “the imperishable among the perishing”. Doctors, nurses and caregivers who sit with the dying sometimes say they sense something that does not die. The verse invites seeing that in every face, especially when bodies fail.

Look for the same Light in every face, including difficult ones. Look for the same light in every face, including the difficult ones.

ŚLOKA 13.28 · DEEP DIVE

NOT HARMING ONESELF

🔎 In context

Seeing God everywhere, one cannot harm.

📜 Looking deeper

To harm another is to harm the Self in them, and so oneself. Commentators also read: one who sees truly no longer “harms himself” by ignorance. Commentators read na hinasti ātmanā ātmānam in two ways. One: seeing the Lord in all, one does not harm others, who are oneself. Two: seeing truly, one no longer harms one’s own Self by ignorance, which is a kind of spiritual suicide. The Īśā Upaniṣad (3) speaks of those who “slay the Self” by living in darkness. Gandhi read this verse as a basis for non-violence. Commentators note that this verse gives an ethical reason for non-harm grounded in vision rather than rules. If the same Lord dwells in all, harm to another is harm to the Lord and to oneself. Śaṅkara says the one who sees thus reaches the highest goal. The idea strongly influenced Indian ethics of compassion.

🌱 For today

Harming others harms us too. Seeing unity makes kindness natural. Harming others harms us too. Seeing the unity makes kindness natural.

ŚLOKA 13.29 · DEEP DIVE

NATURE ACTS, THE SELF DOES NOT

🔎 In context

Another mark of true seeing: the Self is not the doer.

📜 Looking deeper

This repeats 3.27 and 5.8–9 in the language of field and knower. Commentators: it frees from pride and guilt alike. Commentators link this verse with 3.27, 5.8–9 and 18.16. Śaṅkara says the Self is never the doer; this knowledge frees from merit and sin. Rāmānuja says the self acts only through the body, which belongs to nature. The practical fruit is freedom from pride when things go well and from crushing guilt when they go badly, while still taking responsibility. Commentators note the double vision: seeing nature as the doer, and the Self as not the doer. Śaṅkara says this frees one from merit and sin. Rāmānuja says it frees one from pride. In daily life, many find it useful to see habits and moods as nature at work, which makes them easier to change without self-blame.

Watch actions happen, including your own, with a little distance. Watch your actions happen with a little distance. It frees you from both pride and needless guilt.

MANY IN ONE

🔎 In context

Seeing the many in the One is reaching Brahman.

📜 Looking deeper

Like branches spreading from one trunk. Commentators: this vision is liberation itself. The image of a tree: many branches, one trunk. Commentators note two movements: seeing the many resting in the One, and seeing them spread from the One. Śaṅkara says when all is seen as the Self, one becomes Brahman. ŚLOKA 13.31 · DEEP DIVE

NEITHER ACTS NOR IS STAINED

🔎 In context

The Self in the body is untouched by its actions.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators: because it has no beginning and no qualities, nothing can cling to it. The next verse gives an image. Na lipyate, “is not stained”, recalls the lotus leaf of 5.10. Commentators say that because the Self has no beginning, it has no cause that could change it; because it has no qualities, nothing can cling to it. Śaṅkara takes this as a description of the Self in every being. Rāmānuja applies it to the individual self in its pure nature. Commentators note śarīra-stho’pi, “though dwelling in the body”: freedom is not after death only, but now. Śaṅkara says the Self in the body is already free; ignorance hides it. Rāmānuja says the self is pure by nature, though bound by karma. The verse gives hope to anyone who feels stained by the past.

🌱 For today

At your core, nothing you have done can stain what you are. At your core, nothing you have done can stain what you are. Let that free you to do better.

ŚLOKA 13.32 · DEEP DIVE

LIKE SPACE

🔎 In context

Space is not dirtied by what fills it.

📜 Looking deeper

Smoke fills a room; when it clears, space is unchanged. Commentators love this image for the purity of the Self. Ākāśa, space or ether, is the subtlest of the five elements. Smoke, dust and fragrance pass through it; when they clear, space is unchanged. Commentators love the image. Gauḍapāda’s Māṇḍūkya Kārikā (3.3–7) uses the pot-space image: space inside a pot seems separate, but when the pot breaks, it is one with all space. Commentators note that space is called subtle, saukṣmya, and therefore untouched. The subtler something is, the less it is affected by gross things. Śaṅkara says the Self is subtler than space, so even less affected. Many meditation teachers use the image of the sky, through which clouds pass without staining it.

ŚLOKA 13.34 · DEEP DIVE

THE EYE OF KNOWLEDGE

🔎 In context

The chapter ends: seeing the difference frees.

📜 Looking deeper

The whole chapter rests on viveka, discrimination between the seen and the seer. Commentators say this “eye of knowledge” is the divine eye of chapter 11 turned inward. Viveka, discrimination between the seer and the seen, is the core practice of Advaita, as in Śaṅkara’s Vivekacūḍāmaṇi, “crest-jewel of discrimination”. Commentators note that the “eye of knowledge” links with the divine eye of chapter 11: there Arjuna saw the cosmos in God; here he learns to see God in himself. The colophon names chapter 13 Kṣetra-Kṣetrajña-Vibhāga Yoga. Commentators note bhūta-prakṛti-mokṣa, “freedom from the nature of beings”: this knowledge leads not only to understanding but to liberation. Śaṅkara says it is freedom from ignorance. Rāmānuja says it is freedom from the bondage of matter. The chapter ends where it began: with the field and its knower, now clearly distinguished.

🌱 For today

Learn to tell apart what you have from what you are. Practise telling apart what you have from what you are. It is the beginning of freedom.

↑ 13.34 · The eye of knowledge sets you free.
