BILVĀṢṬAKAM

Eight verses on one leaf offered to Śiva · explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: First the whole hymn for chanting in one go. Then the story and background, and two pages for each verse: the chant, a picture, what it says and the key words; then a deeper look, background facts, the inner teaching and the one thing to remember.

FOREWORD

Walk into any Śiva temple on a Monday and you will see them: little heaps of three-part green leaves on the liṅga. The bilva leaf costs nothing. It grows on a common tree. Yet for Śiva it is the dearest offering of all.

The Bilvāṣṭakam is a short, sweet hymn about that leaf. Each of its eight verses ends the same way, ekabilvaṃ śivārpaṇam, "one bilva leaf, offered to Śiva", and each finds a new reason why that one leaf is enough: its three leaflets, its freshness, its birth from Lakṣmī, the gods who live in its tree. Along the way it makes a big claim: one leaf given with love outweighs crores of elephants and hundreds of grand sacrifices.

May every reader find their own "one leaf": a small, sincere offering that is more than enough.

THE STORY · THE HUNTER IN THE BILVA TREE

A Mahāśivarātri tale from the Śiva Purāṇa
The story in 5 frames
An empty day. A poor hunter goes into the forest to feed his family, but finds nothing all day. He hasn't eaten either.
Up a tree. Night falls. He climbs a tree beside a pond to wait for animals coming to drink. It is a bilva tree, and at its foot, hidden in the grass, is a Śiva liṅga.
Leaves fall. Awake all night, he plucks leaves to clear his view, and water drips from his flask. Leaves and water fall on the liṅga below. Without knowing it, he is fasting, keeping vigil, and worshipping, all on Mahāśivarātri night.
The deer. Deer come by, one after another. Each asks to go home first to settle her family, promising to return. They keep their word and come back. Moved by their honesty, he lets them all go.
Grace. At dawn Śiva appears and blesses him. A night of accidental worship and one act of mercy have changed his life.

The leaf

Bilva, dropped without knowing.

The night

Mahāśivarātri: fasting and vigil.

The turn

Mercy for the deer.

ZAPPY: He didn't even know he was worshipping!

PROF. BOLT: That's the point of the story: if a leaf dropped by accident pleases Śiva, imagine one offered with love.

Śiva is easy to please: one leaf, one night, one kind act.

ABOUT THE BILVĀṢṬAKAM

A small hymn about a small leaf

🌿 The tree

Bilva, bael or Aegle marmelos: a hardy tree of the Indian plains, with three-part leaves and a hard-shelled fruit.

📜 The hymn

A traditional aṣṭakam of eight verses plus a phalaśruti. Its author is not known; it is sung across India.

The refrain. Every verse ends ekabilvaṃ śivārpaṇam, "one bilva, offered to Śiva". The verses take turns giving reasons: the leaf's shape (verses 1–3), its worth compared with great gifts (4–5), and its sacred tree (6–8).

Why Śiva loves it. Tradition calls Śiva Āśutoṣa, "quickly pleased". He doesn't need gold or grand rituals; a leaf, a little water and a sincere heart are enough. The Bilvāṣṭakam is that idea in song.

How it's used. At home or in the temple, a bilva leaf is placed on the liṅga at each refrain. It's a favourite on Mondays, in the month of Śrāvaṇa, and on Mahāśivarātri.

THREE LEAVES, THREE OF EVERYTHING

VERSE 1 · GOING DEEPER

THREE LEAVES, THREE OF EVERYTHING

The refrain. Every verse ends ekabilvaṃ śivārpaṇam: "one bilva leaf, offered to Śiva". The whole hymn is about how much a single leaf is worth.

Three of everything. The leaf has three leaflets (tridala). Nature has three guṇas: sattva (clarity), rajas (activity), tamas (inertia). Śiva has three eyes: sun, moon and fire. His weapon is the three-pronged trident. Offering a three-fold leaf says: all of three-fold nature, I give back to You.

Three births. Trijanma is read as past, present and future lives, or as the three states of waking, dreaming and deep sleep. One sincere offering clears all of it.

💡 Aegle marmelos

The bilva is the bael tree. Its fruit is used in Āyurveda for digestion, and it's planted near Śiva temples.

💡 The third eye

The fire-eye on Śiva's forehead is the one that burnt Kāma, the god of desire, to ashes.

One small thing, given wholeheartedly, carries everything. The size of the gift doesn't matter; the heart does.

ZAPPY: Why does everything here come in threes?

PROF. BOLT: Śiva has three eyes and a three-pronged trident, and rules the three guṇas. The bilva leaf has three leaflets. A perfect match.

Three leaflets on one stalk: the many held in one, offered to Śiva.

VERSE 2 · GOING DEEPER

FRESH, WHOLE AND TENDER

The right leaf. Tradition is careful: the three leaflets should be intact, with no holes or tears, young and tender, not withered. Acchidra, "without holes", is the key word.

The point of the rules. A bilva leaf costs nothing. Choosing it carefully is the real offering: attention. When we pick the best leaf instead of grabbing the nearest one, worship has already begun.

"I will worship". Kariṣyāmi, "I will do", is a resolve. Worship begins as an intention, before the hand even reaches the liṅga.

💡 Never stale

Tradition holds that bilva leaves, unlike flowers, don't lose their purity with age: an old leaf can be washed and offered again.

💡 When not to pluck

Many families avoid plucking bilva on certain days, often Mondays, the new moon and some lunar days, and use leaves gathered earlier.

Give your best, not your leftovers. Even something free becomes precious when it is chosen with care.

ZAPPY: Does it matter if the leaf has a hole in it?

PROF. BOLT: Tradition says choose a whole, fresh leaf. Not because Śiva is fussy: care in small things shows care in the heart.

Not just any leaf: whole, fresh and tender, our best and not our leftovers.

VERSE 3 · GOING DEEPER

NANDI'S LORD

Nandi's gaze. In almost every Śiva temple, Nandi the bull lies facing the sanctum, eyes fixed on the liṅga. He is the model devotee: patient, steady, always facing his Lord. Many devotees whisper their prayers into Nandi's ear.

Unbroken. Akhaṇḍa, "unbroken", describes the leaf, but it also describes the attention. A whole leaf for a whole heart; devotion without gaps.

Made pure. Śuddhyanti means "they become clean", like washing. The leaf works on the mind the way water works on the body.

💡 Famous Nandis

Huge stone Nandis sit at Lepākṣi, at the Bṛhadīśvara temple in Thanjavur, and on Mysuru's Cāmuṇḍī Hill.

💡 Nandikeśvara

Read as Śiva, "Lord of Nandi", or as Nandi himself, leader of Śiva's attendants.

Keep facing what matters, the way Nandi faces the shrine. Steady attention is itself purifying.

ZAPPY: Who's Nandi?

PROF. BOLT: Śiva's bull: his vehicle and his first devotee. He sits facing every Śiva shrine, always looking at his Lord.

An unbroken leaf for an unbroken devotion: like Nandi, always facing the Lord.

VERSE 4 · GOING DEEPER

MORE THAN A GREAT SACRIFICE

Gift and ritual. The verse names two classic acts of merit: gifting a śālagrāma (a rare sacred stone from the Gaṇḍakī river, worshipped as Viṣṇu) to a learned person, and performing a soma sacrifice, the grand Vedic ritual whose cups and tools fill Part 3 of our Śrī Rudram.

The bhakti idea. Devotion makes the expensive and the simple equal. In the Bhagavad Gītā (9.26) Kṛṣṇa says: a leaf, a flower, a fruit, a little water, offered with love, I accept. The leaf comes first in his list.

No rivalry. A stone sacred to Viṣṇu is praised in a hymn to Śiva. In the tradition the two are honoured together, and this verse quietly shows it.

💡 Śālagrāma

Ammonite fossils from the Gaṇḍakī river in Nepal, kept in homes and temples as a form of Viṣṇu.

💡 Gītā 9.26

patraṃ puṣpaṃ phalaṃ toyam: leaf, flower, fruit, water.

Don't wait to be rich before you give. The simplest offering, made sincerely, is complete.

ZAPPY: One leaf equals a whole Vedic sacrifice?

PROF. BOLT: That's the hymn's point. What the costliest rituals achieve, a simple leaf given with love achieves too.

One leaf balances the grandest rituals when the heart is in it.

VERSE 5 · GOING DEEPER

WORTH MORE THAN ELEPHANTS

Exaggeration with a purpose. Indian praise-poetry loves impossible numbers. "Thousands of crores of elephants" is meant to be absurd: the poet stacks up the biggest gifts the mind can hold, then sets one leaf against them.

Vājapeya. A soma sacrifice tied to power and kingship, its name often explained as "the drink of strength". Hundreds of them would take a kingdom's wealth.

Kanyādāna. Giving a daughter in marriage is counted among the highest gifts in the tradition. A crore of them is unimaginable merit; the verse still says one leaf matches it.

💡 Koṭi

A crore, ten million. Dantikoṭisahasrāṇi is ten thousand million elephants.

💡 Mahādāna

Classical texts list sixteen "great gifts", such as tulāpuruṣa, giving away one's own weight in gold.

Value isn't measured by size. What you give with your whole heart outweighs everything given for show.

ZAPPY: Crores of elephants? That's a lot of elephants.

PROF. BOLT: Deliberately huge. The poet piles up the biggest gifts imaginable, and still says one leaf is enough.

Stack up every great gift: one leaf, given with love, still balances the scale.

VERSE 6 · GOING DEEPER

BORN FROM LAKṢMĪ

The legend. A popular story tells that Lakṣmī worshipped Śiva every day with a thousand lotuses. One day a lotus was missing. Since poets compared her breasts to lotuses, she offered one; Śiva, moved, turned her offering into the bilva tree. Another tradition says it grew from drops of Pārvatī's sweat. Versions vary, but all make the tree a gift of the Goddess.

Lakṣmī's tree. The bilva is called Śrīvṛkṣa, "Śrī's tree", and its fruit śrīphala. The Vedic Śrī Sūktam already tells Lakṣmī: "the bilva is your tree".

Giving the tree. Prayacchāmi, "I give": the whole tree, not just a leaf. Planting a bilva near a home or temple is still considered a deeply meritorious act.

💡 Śrī Sūktam

Verse 6: vanaspatis tava vṛkṣo'tha bilvaḥ, "the bilva is your tree".

💡 Śrīphala

The bael fruit is called śrīphala, "the fruit of Śrī".

Fortune is best used as an offering. Wealth given to something higher turns into blessing.

ZAPPY: The tree was born from Lakṣmī?

PROF. BOLT: So the legend says: she offered Śiva something precious of her own, and it became the bilva. That's why it's called Śrīvṛkṣa, Lakṣmī's tree.

The bilva is Lakṣmī's gift to Śiva: fortune offered to the Auspicious.

VERSE 7 · GOING DEEPER

JUST TO SEE IT, JUST TO TOUCH IT

Sacred trees. India has many: the tulasī, the aśvattha (peepal), the banyan, the bilva. Darśan, seeing the sacred, is itself worship. The verse extends it to a tree: to see it and touch it is already to be blessed.

A play on Aghora. Aghora-pāpa means "dreadful sins", but Aghora is also the fierce face of Śiva in our Pañca-Brahma Mantras article. The tree of Śiva destroys even the dreadful.

Nature as a reminder. Today we know that time among trees calms the mind. The tradition built this in: make a tree a reminder of the divine, and every glance at it becomes a moment of remembering.

💡 Temple trees

Many Śiva temples have a bilva as their sthala-vṛkṣa, the temple's own sacred tree.

💡 Bael sherbet

Bael fruit sherbet is a traditional summer drink in North India.

Surround yourself with reminders of the good. What you see every day shapes what you become.

ZAPPY: Just looking at a tree can help?

PROF. BOLT: A tree that reminds you of Śiva turns your mind to Him. Seeing it is already a small act of remembering.

Even seeing and touching the bilva turns the mind to Śiva.

VERSE 8 · GOING DEEPER

BRAHMĀ, VIṢṆU AND ŚIVA IN ONE TREE

A borrowed pattern. The same words are used in a well-known prayer to the aśvattha, the peepal: "Brahmā at the root, Viṣṇu in the middle, Śiva at the top, salutations to the king of trees". Here the pattern is given to the bilva.

Growth as the cosmic cycle. Roots create, drawing life from the earth: Brahmā. The trunk holds and carries: Viṣṇu. At the tips, leaves fall and are renewed: Śiva, who dissolves so that new growth can come.

One offering reaches all. A leaf from such a tree, offered to Śiva, honours all three. The verse dissolves any idea of rival gods.

💡 Aśvattha

The peepal, Ficus religiosa: Kṛṣṇa says in the Gītā (10.26), "Among trees I am the aśvattha".

💡 Trimūrti

Brahmā creates, Viṣṇu sustains, Śiva dissolves: three faces of one reality.

Every living thing grows, holds on and lets go. See all three as sacred, not just the growing.

ZAPPY: All three gods in one tree?

PROF. BOLT: The creator at the roots, the sustainer in the trunk, the dissolver at the tips. The tree, like the universe, holds all three.

Root, trunk, tip: the whole Trimūrti in one tree.

PHALAŚRUTI · GOING DEEPER

THE FRUIT OF CHANTING

The phalaśruti. Most hymns end with a "hearing of the fruit": what the chanter gains. It's an encouragement to keep chanting, not a price list.

Sannidhi. "Presence, nearness". Traditionally this means before a liṅga, at a temple or home shrine. But nearness can also be inward: wherever the mind turns to Śiva, there is His sannidhi.

Śivaloka. Pictured as Kailāsa, Śiva's mountain world. For the Advaita tradition the deeper meaning is realising our own nature as Śiva, the theme of our Nirvāṇa Ṣaṭkam article.

💡 Aṣṭakam

A hymn of eight verses. Longer versions of the Bilvāṣṭakam, with extra verses, are also sung.

💡 When it's chanted

Especially on Mondays, through the month of Śrāvaṇa, and on Mahāśivarātri, with bilva offered at each refrain.

Being near the divine is a choice we can make anywhere: in a temple, at home, or quietly in the heart.

ZAPPY: 'In Śiva's presence': do I have to be in a temple?

PROF. BOLT: A temple, a home shrine, or simply remembering Him. Sannidhi means nearness, and you can bring Him near anywhere.

Chant it near Śiva, in a temple or in the heart, and walk free.
