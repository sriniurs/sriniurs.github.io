FOREWORD

"Who are you?" It sounds like the simplest question in the world. We answer it every day with a name, a job, a family, a town. The Nirvāṇa Ṣaṭkam is what a young boy is said to have answered instead, standing before the sage who would become his teacher. In six short verses he sets aside the mind, the body, the emotions, even religion and family, and arrives at one quiet conclusion: cidānanda-rūpaḥ śivo'ham, "I am consciousness and bliss; I am Śiva."

This small book opens up those six verses in simple English: what each one says, what its key words mean, and how its teaching can be used in ordinary life. It is for anyone who has heard "Śivo'ham, Śivo'ham" sung and wondered what it means, and for anyone meeting it for the first time.

May every reader find, beneath all the labels life gives us, the calm awareness this hymn points to. Om Namaḥ Śivāya.

THE STORY · "WHO ARE YOU?"

An eight-year-old's answer on the banks of the Narmadā
The traditional account in 5 frames
A boy leaves home. Young Śaṅkara leaves Kaladi in Kerala as a boy renunciant, searching for a guru.
North to the Narmadā. He walks all the way to the Narmadā, where the sage Govinda Bhagavatpāda lives in a cave, deep in meditation.
The question. The sage asks him: "Who are you?"
The answer. Instead of a name or a family, the boy replies with six verses describing the Self that is none of these things.
Accepted. Govinda Bhagavatpāda accepts him as his disciple. These verses become the Nirvāṇa Ṣaṭkam, also called the Ātma Ṣaṭkam.

The question

The oldest one in philosophy: "Who am I?"

The method

Neti neti: "not this, not this", until only the knower is left.

The answer

Cidānanda-rūpaḥ śivo'ham: I am consciousness and bliss.

ZAPPY: An eight-year-old said all this?

PROF. BOLT: That's the tradition. Whatever the age, the answer has kept seekers thinking for twelve centuries.

"Śiva" here means pure, auspicious awareness: the Self in everyone, not only the deity on Kailāsa.

ABOUT THE HYMN

Six verses, one question, one answer

📜 Names and author

Called the Nirvāṇa Ṣaṭkam ("six verses on liberation") and the Ātma Ṣaṭkam ("six verses on the Self"). Traditionally attributed to Ādi Śaṅkarācārya, the great teacher of Advaita Vedānta.

📍 Close to home: Śṛṅgeri

Śaṅkara is traditionally said to have founded the Śṛṅgeri Śāradā Pīṭham on the Tuṅgā in Chikkamagaluru district, one of the great centres of Advaita teaching to this day.

Advaita in six verses. Advaita means "not two". Its central claim is that the deepest Self in each person (ātman) and the ultimate reality (brahman) are one. This hymn makes the claim not with argument but with experience: it removes, one by one, everything we mistake for ourselves.

Why "Śiva"? Śiva literally means "auspicious". In śivo'ham, "I am Śiva", it points to pure awareness: auspicious, unchanging, the same in everyone. Devotees also hear in it Lord Śiva himself; both readings live together.

How it's used. It is chanted in meditation, at the start of Vedānta classes, and on Mahāśivarātri, and it has become one of the most widely sung Sanskrit hymns in the world.

VERSE 1 · GOING DEEPER

NOT THE MIND, NOT THE SENSES, NOT THE ELEMENTS

The question. Tradition says that an eight-year-old Śaṅkara, searching for a teacher on the banks of the Narmadā, met the sage Govinda Bhagavatpāda, who asked him, "Who are you?" Most of us would answer with a name, a family, a town or a job. The boy answered with six verses that strip away every one of those labels.

Four inner instruments. Vedānta calls the inner faculties the antaḥkaraṇa: manas (the mind that wavers and doubts), buddhi (the intellect that decides), ahaṅkāra (the "I-maker" that says "this is mine") and citta (memory, the store of past impressions). The very first words cross out all four: nāham, "I am not".

Senses and elements. Then the senses (ear, tongue, nose, eye) and the elements (space, earth, fire, air). The method is called neti neti, "not this, not this". Remove everything that can be observed, and what's left is the observer. That's the refrain: cidānanda-rūpaḥ śivo'ham, I am consciousness and bliss, I am Śiva.

💡 Neti neti

"Not this, not this": a teaching from the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad. You find the Self not by adding labels but by removing them.

💡 Where it happened

Tradition places the meeting at Omkareshwar on the Narmadā, where a cave linked to Govinda Bhagavatpāda is still shown below the Śiva temple.

Next time you say "I'm angry" or "I'm tired", try "anger is here" or "tiredness is here". That small shift is the first verse in practice: you are the one who knows the state, not the state itself.

ZAPPY: If I'm not my mind, what's doing the thinking?

PROF. BOLT: The mind is. You're the one who notices it thinking.

You are the awareness in which mind, senses and body appear, not any one of them.

VERSE 2 · GOING DEEPER

NOT THE BREATH, NOT THE BODY

Prāṇa and the five airs. Verse 1 removed mind and senses; verse 2 goes after life itself. Prāṇa is the life-breath, traditionally divided into five vāyus: prāṇa, apāna, vyāna, udāna and samāna, which govern breathing in, elimination, circulation, upward movement and digestion. Even these, he says, are not "I".

Seven tissues, five sheaths. Āyurveda counts seven dhātus that build the body. The Taittirīya Upaniṣad describes five kośas, sheaths, around the Self: of food, breath, mind, intellect and bliss. The verse crosses out both lists at once, even the innermost "bliss" sheath.

Organs of action. Finally the five karmendriyas: speech, hands, feet, and the organs of procreation and excretion. Verse 1 took away the senses that receive; verse 2 takes away the organs that act. What remains still says: cidānanda-rūpaḥ śivo'ham.

💡 Seven dhātus

Rasa, rakta, māṃsa, medas, asthi, majjā and śukra: plasma, blood, muscle, fat, bone, marrow and reproductive tissue, in Āyurveda's list.

💡 Five kośas

Annamaya (food), prāṇamaya (breath), manomaya (mind), vijñānamaya (intellect) and ānandamaya (bliss): the five sheaths of the Taittirīya Upaniṣad.

The body is a wonderful instrument and deserves good care. But identifying entirely with it brings fear of every ache and every birthday. Caring for the body without being only the body is a calmer way to live.

ZAPPY: Not even my breath?

PROF. BOLT: You can watch your breath. Whatever you can watch, you're not.

Breath and body are instruments you use, not the one who uses them.

VERSE 3 · GOING DEEPER

NOT LIKE OR DISLIKE, NOT EVEN LIFE'S GOALS

The inner weather. Dveṣa and rāga, aversion and attachment, are the two forces that pull the mind all day: towards what we like, away from what we don't. Lobha (greed), moha (delusion), mada (pride) and mātsarya (envy) fill out the classic list of inner enemies. They move in the mind, the verse says, but they are not who I am.

The four aims. Then a startling line: na dharmo na cārtho na kāmo na mokṣaḥ, not dharma, not wealth, not pleasure, not even liberation. These four puruṣārthas are the goals of a human life in Indian thought. Why set them aside? Because goals belong to someone who lacks something.

Already free. Advaita's point is that the Self was never bound, so liberation isn't something it gains. It is the recognition of what was always true. The ocean doesn't need to become wet.

💡 Six inner enemies

Kāma, krodha, lobha, moha, mada and mātsarya: the ṣaḍ-ripu. Verse 3 names most of them and adds rāga and dveṣa, liking and disliking.

💡 Puruṣārtha

The four aims of life: dharma, artha, kāma and mokṣa. They guide a good life; here they are set aside only at the level of the Self.

This isn't permission to drop duties or ethics. It's a reminder that a storm of anger or envy is passing weather, not your identity. You can let it pass without becoming it.

ZAPPY: Not even mokṣa? Isn't that the whole goal?

PROF. BOLT: You can't attain what you already are. The Self was never bound.

Emotions come and go, and even life's goals belong to the seeker, not to the Self.

VERSE 4 · GOING DEEPER

NOT MERIT OR SIN, NOT EVEN THE ONE WHO EATS

Beyond the ledger. Puṇya and pāpa, merit and sin, are the ledger of karma. Saukhya and duḥkha, pleasure and pain, are its results. The verse says the Self keeps no such account. It is the witness in which the ledger appears.

Even religion. More surprising: na mantro na tīrthaṃ na vedā na yajñāḥ, not mantra, not pilgrimage, not the Vedas, not sacrifice. Śaṅkara wrote great commentaries and many hymns, so he isn't dismissing them. He's saying they are means, not the end. A ladder isn't the roof.

The meal. The last line uses a homely triad: bhojana (eating), bhojya (food), bhoktā (the eater). Every experience has an experiencer, something experienced and the act of experiencing. The Self is none of the three; it is the light in which all three show up.

💡 Tripuṭī

Vedānta calls the knower–known–knowing triad the tripuṭī. Seeing past it is a hallmark of non-dual experience.

💡 The raft

A favourite image in Indian thought: a raft is for crossing the river. Once across, you don't carry it on your head.

Practise sincerely: chant, pray, go on pilgrimage. But don't turn the practice into a new badge for the ego. The goal of every ritual is the stillness this verse points to.

ZAPPY: Not the eater? But I just had lunch!

PROF. BOLT: The body ate. You were there watching it happen, and you'll be there when it's hungry again.

Even religious practice is a raft: precious for crossing, but not the shore.

VERSE 5 · GOING DEEPER

NO DEATH, NO CASTE, NO FAMILY

No death. Na mṛtyur na śaṅkā: no death, no fear. What is never born cannot die. The Bhagavad Gītā says the same of the Self: it is unborn and undying. Fear of death belongs to identification with the body, not to awareness itself.

No caste. Na me jāti-bhedaḥ: I have no distinction of caste or birth. From the mouth of a young brahmin boy, this is striking. At the level of the Self, birth-based divisions simply don't exist.

No family, no guru. Pitā naiva me naiva mātā: no father, no mother. Na bandhur na mitraṃ gurur naiva śiṣyaḥ: no relative, no friend, no teacher, no disciple. Said to the very teacher he hoped to join, it's bold, and tradition says it was exactly this clarity that made Govinda Bhagavatpāda accept him.

💡 Gītā echo

Bhagavad Gītā 2.20: the Self is never born and never dies; unborn and eternal, it is not killed when the body is killed.

💡 Compare Annapūrṇā

The Annapūrṇā Stotram ends "my mother is Pārvatī, my father Śiva". Here Śaṅkara says "no mother, no father". Two sides of one truth: in devotion, all is family; in the Self, nothing is separate.

Relationships matter, and this verse doesn't ask anyone to abandon them. It loosens the grip of fear and division: about death, about status, about who belongs. Underneath every label is the same awareness.

ZAPPY: No guru? He's saying this to the guru he wants to join!

PROF. BOLT: That's the cheek of it. And, the story goes, that's exactly why Govinda accepted him.

The Self was never born and never dies, and belongs to no caste or clan.

VERSE 6 · GOING DEEPER

FORMLESS, EVERYWHERE, EVER FREE

From "not" to "am". The first five verses used negation. The sixth begins with aham, "I am": nirvikalpa, free of all thought-divisions; nirākāra-rūpa, whose form is formless. Having removed every object, Śaṅkara now points straight at the subject.

Everywhere. Vibhutvāc ca sarvatra sarvendriyāṇām: being all-pervading, I am everywhere, the presence behind all the senses. Not a passenger inside a body, but the space of awareness in which bodies, senses and worlds appear.

Nothing to gain. Na cāsaṅgataṃ naiva muktir na meyaḥ: not attached, not even in need of liberation, not something that can be measured or known as an object. A popular sung version reads sadā me samatvaṃ na muktir na bandhaḥ, "I am always the same; neither liberated nor bound". Both say one thing: the Self was always free.

💡 Two versions

The last lines circulate in two forms: "na cāsaṅgataṃ naiva muktir na meyaḥ" and "sadā me samatvaṃ na muktir na bandhaḥ". Both deny bondage and liberation alike.

💡 Śṛṅgeri

Śaṅkara is traditionally credited with founding the Śṛṅgeri Śāradā Pīṭham in Karnataka's Chikkamagaluru district, one of the great maṭhas that teach this very Advaita.

You don't need to become something to be complete. Peace isn't far away; it's what remains when the mind stops grabbing labels. Sit quietly for a minute and notice the awareness that's already here. That is the "I" this hymn is about.

ZAPPY: So after all the 'nots', finally a 'yes'?

PROF. BOLT: Finally. 'I am formless, everywhere.' The sculptor chipped away everything that wasn't the statue.

After five verses of "not this", the sixth says what remains: formless, all-pervading, ever free.
