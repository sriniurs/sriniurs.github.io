ŚIVA'S FAMILY
AND SYMBOLS

THE MOON, THE SNAKE, THE ASH · AND THE HOUSEHOLD OF KAILĀSA
Why Śiva looks the way He does, and who lives with Him · explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: First a labelled picture of Śiva, then one page for each symbol: what it is, the story behind it, and what it teaches. Then the family: Pārvatī, Gaṇeśa, Kārttikeya and the wider family, and the four animals who should be enemies but live in peace.

FOREWORD

Look at any picture of Śiva and questions crowd in. Why a moon in His hair? Why a river? Why a snake around the neck, ash on the body, a blue throat, a third eye? Why does He sit on a bull, and why is His wife's mount a lion that could eat it?

Every detail has a story, and every story a meaning. The crescent moon is a curse that Śiva softened; the blue throat is the poison He held to save the world; the ash is what remains when everything else is burnt.

And Śiva, the great ascetic, is also a family man: husband of Pārvatī, father of Gaṇeśa and Kārttikeya, at the centre of a household of the most unlikely companions. This guide explains them all, one page at a time.

ŚIVA AT A GLANCE

Every detail has a story
A simplified drawing: images differ by region and tradition, and not every image shows every symbol.

ZAPPY: That's a lot of things on one god!

PROF. BOLT: And each one is a story. Turn the page and we'll take them one by one, from the head down.

THE THIRD EYE

Tri-netra, Tryambaka
A third eye in the middle of Śiva's forehead. His two eyes see the world; the third sees beyond it. When it opens, it burns.

The story. When Kāma, the god of desire, shot his flower arrow to break Śiva's meditation, Śiva opened His third eye and Kāma was burnt to ash. (See Part 2 of our Śiva Mahimna series.)

What it means. The third eye is the eye of wisdom (jñāna). It doesn't look at things; it sees through them. What it burns is not the world but illusion and desire.

Three eyes. Śiva is Tryambaka and Trinetra, "three-eyed". His eyes are often said to be the sun, the moon and fire.

💡 Tryambaka

The "three-eyed": the first word of the Mahāmṛtyuñjaya mantra.

💡 The dot

Many people wear a dot or mark between the brows: the place of the inner eye.

ZAPPY: Can the third eye really burn things?

PROF. BOLT: In the story it burnt Kāma. Its lesson is about us: when we see clearly, our desires lose their power.

Two eyes for the world, a third for the truth.

THE CRESCENT MOON

Candraśekhara, the moon-crowned
A thin crescent moon rests in Śiva's matted hair. He is called Candraśekhara, "moon-crested", and Somanātha, "Lord of the moon".

The story. The Moon, Candra, married the twenty-seven daughters of Dakṣa but loved only Rohiṇī. Dakṣa cursed him to waste away. The dying Moon worshipped Śiva at Prabhāsa, and Śiva softened the curse: the Moon would wane and wax for ever. Śiva placed the crescent on His head. (Our Somanātha guide tells it in full.)

What it means. The moon marks time, waxing and waning; Śiva wears it as an ornament because He is beyond time, Mahākāla. It also stands for the mind, which grows calm in His presence.

💡 Somanātha

The first jyotirliṅga, named for this story.

💡 Phases

The waxing and waning moon, ruled by Śiva's blessing, sets the Hindu calendar.

ZAPPY: Why only a thin crescent?

PROF. BOLT: The Moon came to Him at his weakest. Śiva wears the moon in its smallest phase: He holds what is fading.

Śiva wears time itself as a small ornament.

THE GAṄGĀ IN HIS HAIR

Gaṅgādhara, bearer of the Gaṅgā
The river Gaṅgā flows from the knot of Śiva's matted hair (jaṭā). He is Gaṅgādhara, "the bearer of the Gaṅgā".

The story. King Bhagīratha did long penance to bring the heavenly river down to earth, to purify the ashes of his ancestors. But her fall would have shattered the world. Śiva caught her in His hair and let her flow out gently, as the Gaṅgā.

What it means. Śiva takes the force that would destroy and makes it a blessing. Raw power becomes a river that feeds a land.

Other rivers. The Godāvarī, "the Gaṅgā of the South", is brought down by the sage Gautama in the Tryambakeśvara story.

💡 Bhagīratha

His effort gave us the phrase "bhagīratha prayatna", a superhuman effort.

💡 Jaṭā

The matted locks of the ascetic; Śiva's are said to fill the sky.

ZAPPY: Why not let her fall straight down?

PROF. BOLT: The force would have broken the earth. Śiva softened it. Great power needs someone to hold it gently.

He holds the force of heaven so it can bless the earth.

THE SERPENT

Nāgabhūṣaṇa, adorned with serpents
A serpent, often named Vāsuki, coils around Śiva's neck. Snakes also form His armlets, sacred thread and earrings.

The story. Vāsuki, king of serpents, served as the churning rope when gods and demons churned the ocean. Tradition says Śiva honoured him by wearing him as a garland.

What it means. The snake is death, fear and danger. Śiva wears it as jewellery: what terrifies us is an ornament to Him. The coiled snake also stands for kuṇḍalinī, the sleeping inner energy, mastered by the yogī.

Shedding skin. The snake sheds its old skin and lives on, a sign of renewal and of time's cycles.

💡 Vāsuki

King of serpents and the churning rope of the Samudra Manthana.

💡 Nāga Pañcamī

The festival when snakes are honoured, in Śrāvaṇa.

ZAPPY: Isn't He afraid of the snake?

PROF. BOLT: He's the Lord of death, Mṛtyuñjaya. What we fear, He wears. That's the point.

What we fear, He wears as an ornament.

THE BLUE THROAT

Nīlakaṇṭha
Śiva's throat is blue. He is Nīlakaṇṭha, "the blue-throated one".

The story. When the ocean was churned, the first thing to rise was Hālāhala, a poison that could destroy the world. Gods and demons ran to Śiva. He drank it. Pārvatī pressed His throat so it would not go down, and it stayed there, turning the throat blue.

What it means. Śiva takes in the world's poison so others don't have to. He neither swallows it (letting it harm Him) nor spits it out (letting it harm others): He holds it. The mark is a scar of compassion.

💡 Hālāhala

The world-ending poison from the churned ocean.

💡 In the Mahimna

Verse 14 calls this mark Śiva's greatest ornament.

ZAPPY: Why didn't He just spit it out?

PROF. BOLT: It would have poisoned the world. So He held it. Sometimes love means carrying what would hurt others.

He holds the world's poison, and wears the mark as an ornament.

THE TRIDENT AND THE ḌAMARU

Triśūla and ḍamaru
The triśūla, a three-pronged trident, and the ḍamaru, a small hourglass-shaped drum, often tied to it.

The trident. Its three prongs are read as many triads: creation, protection and dissolution; past, present and future; the three guṇas; will, knowledge and action. Śiva holds all three, and is beyond them.

The drum. The ḍamaru beats the rhythm of creation as Śiva dances. Tradition says that at the end of His dance it sounded fourteen times, and the grammarian Pāṇini heard in them the Maheśvara Sūtras, the sound-lists on which Sanskrit grammar is built.

Sound and form. Together they say: creation begins with sound and ends with the cutting away of illusion.

💡 Maheśvara Sūtras

Fourteen sound-lists that open Pāṇini's grammar, traditionally from Śiva's drum.

💡 Triśūla

Also carried by the Goddess as Durgā: the shared weapon of Śiva and Śakti.

ZAPPY: A drum invented grammar?

PROF. BOLT: That's the tradition! The fourteen beats of Śiva's drum became the sounds of Sanskrit. Sound comes before everything.

The drum begins creation; the trident cuts illusion.
The Maheśvara Sūtras story is tradition; Pāṇini's own text does not tell it.

ASH AND THE THREE LINES

Bhasma, vibhūti and the tripuṇḍra
Śiva smears His body with ash. Śaivas wear it too, as tripuṇḍra: three horizontal lines across the forehead.

The meaning of ash. Ash is what remains when everything else is burnt. It cannot burn again. Śiva wears it as a reminder that bodies and possessions end, and that only the Self remains.

The three lines. The tripuṇḍra is a Śaiva mark of three horizontal lines; the Kālāgni Rudra Upaniṣad explains them as triads such as the three sacred fires, the three syllables of Oṃ, the three guṇas and the three worlds. Another reading sees in them Śiva's three powers of will, knowledge and action.

Vaiṣṇava contrast. The Vaiṣṇava mark, the ūrdhva-puṇḍra, runs vertically.

💡 Vibhūti

"Glory, power": the name for sacred ash.

💡 Bhasma ārati

Mahākāla in Ujjain is famous for the dawn worship with ash.

ZAPPY: Ash sounds sad.

PROF. BOLT: It's actually freeing: once you know everything ends as ash, you hold it lightly. That's why Śiva wears it happily.

What remains when all is burnt: Śiva wears it as His robe.

RUDRĀKṢA AND THE TIGER SKIN

Beads of tears, and a hide won in battle
Rudrākṣa beads, the dried seeds of a tree, worn and used for japa; and the tiger skin Śiva sits on or wears.

Rudra's eye. Rudrākṣa means "eye of Rudra". A common tradition says Śiva sat in meditation for a very long time, and when He opened His eyes, His tears fell to earth and became rudrākṣa trees. The beads are counted for japa, often in strings of 108 beads plus one.

The tiger. In one story the proud sages of the Dāruka forest sent a tiger to kill Śiva. He killed it and wore its skin. The tiger is fierce desire and ego, tamed and sat upon.

The elephant hide. Śiva also wears the hide of the elephant demon Gajāsura, whom He defeated, as Gajāsura-saṃhāra.

💡 Mukhī

Rudrākṣa beads are classed by their "faces", the natural lines on them.

💡 Japa

Repeating a mantra, often Oṃ Namaḥ Śivāya, counted on beads.

ZAPPY: Tears became beads?

PROF. BOLT: In the story, yes: tears of compassion. Devotees wear them to keep that compassion close.

Tears turned to beads, a tiger turned to a seat: emotion and ego, mastered.
Legends of the rudrākṣa's origin differ between texts.

THE LIṄGA

The sign of the formless
The liṅga is Śiva's most common form of worship: a smooth upright stone on a base, the pīṭha or yoni.

A sign. Liṅga means "mark" or "sign": a sign of what has no form. It has no face or limbs because Śiva is beyond form.

The pillar of light. Its origin story is the endless column of fire that Brahmā and Viṣṇu could not measure: the Liṅgodbhava. The twelve jyotirliṅgas are the places where that light is said to have appeared (see our series).

Śiva and Śakti. The liṅga rising from the pīṭha is read as Śiva and Śakti, consciousness and energy, together. Water poured over it (abhiṣeka) flows out through the spout.

💡 Svayambhū

Self-manifested liṅgas, found rather than carved, are especially revered.

💡 Abhiṣeka

Bathing the liṅga with water, milk and other offerings while chanting, as in the Rudram.

ZAPPY: Why a stone with no face?

PROF. BOLT: Because Śiva is beyond any face. The liṅga points to the formless, like an arrow pointing to the sky.

A sign without a face for the One beyond all form.

KAILĀSA

Śiva's mountain home
Mount Kailāsa, in the Himālaya in Tibet, is Śiva's home in tradition, where He sits in meditation with Pārvatī.

The axis of the world. Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and followers of Bon all hold Kailāsa sacred. Four great rivers rise near it, and Lake Mānasarovar lies at its foot.

Never climbed. Out of reverence, there is no recorded ascent of its summit. Pilgrims walk around it instead, the parikramā.

Inner Kailāsa. Yogīs speak of Kailāsa within: the crown of the head, where Śiva and Śakti unite.

💡 Mānasarovar

The sacred lake at Kailāsa's foot, "made from Brahmā's mind".

💡 Rāvaṇa

The demon king tried to lift Kailāsa and was pinned by Śiva's toe.

ZAPPY: Nobody has climbed it?

PROF. BOLT: There's no recorded ascent; climbers have left it alone out of respect for all the faiths that hold it holy.

The mountain at the centre of the world, left unclimbed out of reverence.

NANDI

The bull who waits
Nandi, the white bull, is Śiva's mount (vāhana), His gatekeeper and His greatest devotee. In temples he sits facing the sanctum.

Always facing Śiva. In every Śiva temple Nandi sits outside the sanctum, gazing at the liṅga. He doesn't ask for anything; he waits. Devotees often whisper their prayers into his ear, to be passed on to Śiva.

Dharma. The bull stands for dharma, strength and steadiness. Riding the bull, Śiva is master of strength, not ruled by it.

Chief of the gaṇas. In many stories Nandi is the leader of Śiva's attendants and the guardian of Kailāsa.

💡 Vāhana

A god's "vehicle": an animal that carries and also symbolises the deity.

💡 Nandi Hills

The hills near Bengaluru are named after a Nandi shrine on top.

ZAPPY: Why does Nandi always face the door?

PROF. BOLT: He's the perfect devotee: patient, steady, eyes only on the Lord. That's why people whisper to him.

Patient, steady, eyes on the Lord: the model devotee.

PĀRVATĪ

Satī, Pārvatī, and the half that completes Him
Pārvatī, "daughter of the mountain", is Śiva's wife: Śakti, the power without which Śiva cannot act. Her other forms include Durgā, Kālī, Umā and Gaurī.

Satī first. Śiva's first wife was Satī, daughter of Dakṣa. Insulted at her father's sacrifice, she gave up her life; Śiva's grief and anger shook the worlds.

Pārvatī. Satī was reborn as Pārvatī, daughter of Himavān, king of the mountains. She won the withdrawn Śiva not by charm (Kāma's arrow failed) but by her own fierce penance.

Ardhanārīśvara. In the end Śiva made her half of His own body: the "Lord who is half woman". Śiva is consciousness, Śakti is energy; neither is complete alone.

💡 Śakti Pīṭhas

The places where parts of Satī's body fell, sacred to the Goddess.

💡 Śiva–Śakti

"Śiva without Śakti is śava (a corpse)", a famous saying.

ZAPPY: She won Him by penance, not beauty?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. Kāma's arrow failed; her tapas succeeded. She was His equal in strength of will.

Śiva is consciousness, Pārvatī its power: two halves of one.

GAṆEŚA

The elder son, Lord of the gaṇas
Gaṇeśa, son of Pārvatī and Śiva, with an elephant's head: remover of obstacles, lord of beginnings, worshipped first in every rite.

The story. Pārvatī shaped a boy from turmeric paste and set him to guard her door. When Śiva came home, the boy, not knowing Him, blocked the way. In the fight Śiva cut off his head. Seeing Pārvatī's grief, Śiva gave the boy the head of an elephant and made him chief of the gaṇas: Gaṇapati.

The mouse. His vehicle is a mouse, Mūṣaka: the small, restless mind or desire, mastered by wisdom.

The scribe. Tradition says Gaṇeśa wrote down the Mahābhārata as Vyāsa dictated it, breaking off a tusk to use as a pen.

💡 First worship

Rites begin with Gaṇeśa: "vakratuṇḍa mahākāya…".

💡 Gaṇeśa Caturthī

His birthday festival in Bhādrapada, huge in Maharashtra and Karnataka.

ZAPPY: Śiva cut off His own son's head?

PROF. BOLT: He didn't know who the boy was. The story ends with a new life and a new honour: Gaṇeśa is worshipped first, before every god.

Guardian at the door, remover of obstacles, first to be worshipped.

KĀRTTIKEYA

The younger son: Skanda, Murugan, Subrahmaṇya
Kārttikeya, also called Skanda, Kumāra, Ṣaṇmukha ("six-faced"), and in the south Murugan and Subrahmaṇya: the warrior son, riding a peacock and holding the spear (vel).

Born to win. The demon Tāraka could be killed only by a son of Śiva. Kāma's arrow, Pārvatī's penance and their marriage all led to this birth. Raised by the six Kṛttikā stars (the Pleiades), the child grew six faces to be fed by all six. He led the gods' army and defeated Tāraka.

The mango. A famous story: a single fruit would go to whichever son circled the world first. Kārttikeya flew off on his peacock; Gaṇeśa walked around his parents, saying "they are my world", and won. Hurt, Kārttikeya went south, to Palani.

Murugan. In Tamil Nadu he is one of the most loved gods, with six great shrines, the Ārupaḍai Vīḍu.

💡 Vel

The spear given to him by Pārvatī; in Tamil, "vel vel" is a cry of devotion.

💡 Mallikārjuna

In one legend Śiva and Pārvatī followed the hurt Kārttikeya to Śrīśaila (see our guide).

ZAPPY: Gaṇeśa won by walking around his parents?

PROF. BOLT: He said his parents were his whole world. It's the best-known lesson about honouring one's parents in the tradition.

Six faces, a spear, a peacock: the warrior who defeated Tāraka.
Whether Gaṇeśa or Kārttikeya is the elder differs between north and south India.

THE WIDER FAMILY

Daughters, sons and forms in regional traditions

The best-known family is Śiva, Pārvatī, Gaṇeśa and Kārttikeya, the Śiva Parivār of temple walls and family shrines. But many regions and texts add others.

🌸 Aśokasundarī

A daughter of Śiva and Pārvatī in the Padma Purāṇa, created from the wish-fulfilling tree to ease Pārvatī's loneliness; her name means "the beautiful one who ends sorrow". She married King Nahuṣa; she is largely absent from other major texts.

🐍 Manasā

The snake goddess of Bengal and eastern India, called Śiva's daughter in the medieval Manasā-maṅgal poems.

🌿 Ayyappa

In Kerala, Hariharaputra, "son of Hari and Hara": born of Śiva and Viṣṇu as Mohinī; worshipped at Sabarimala.

🔥 Vīrabhadra and Bhairava

Not children but fierce forms born from Śiva: Vīrabhadra destroyed Dakṣa's sacrifice; Kāla Bhairava guards Kāśī.

The gaṇas

Śiva's household also includes his gaṇas, a host of attendants of every shape, led by Nandi and later by Gaṇeśa, and devotees like the sage Bhṛṅgi.

ZAPPY: Śiva has a daughter?

PROF. BOLT: In the Padma Purāṇa, yes: Aśokasundarī. Many people learnt of her from television. Regional traditions add more family; the core four are the ones in every shrine.

These figures belong to particular texts and regions; accounts differ.

A FAMILY OF OPPOSITES

Why the animals of Kailāsa don't eat each other

The puzzle. Look at the family's animals: the snake around Śiva's neck would eat Gaṇeśa's mouse; Kārttikeya's peacock would eat the snake; Pārvatī's lion would eat Śiva's bull. In nature they are predator and prey. On Kailāsa they live side by side.

The teaching. A home, a society, a mind can hold opposites without violence when something higher holds them together. In Śiva's presence, natural enmity falls away.

Opposites in the family itself. The ascetic and the householder; the destroyer and the gracious one; the wise elder son and the warrior younger son; Śiva and Śakti as one body. The whole family is a lesson in harmony.

ZAPPY: Do the animals really get along?

PROF. BOLT: In the pictures, yes, and that's the point. If they can live together on Kailāsa, so can people with different natures.

Natural enemies, one household: harmony where something higher holds us together.
