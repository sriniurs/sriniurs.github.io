WHO IS SKANDA?

SUBRAHMAṆYA ṢAṢṬHĪ · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Śiva’s son, the six-faced god of the spear

FOREWORD

He is the youngest of the gods and the general of their army. The north knows him as Kārttikeya or Skanda, Tamil Nadu as Murugan, and Karnataka as Subrahmaṇya, often worshipped in the form of a serpent.

He was born for one job: to kill the demon Tāraka, whom no one but a son of Śiva could defeat. He was born in a bed of reeds, nursed by six star-mothers, given six faces, a spear by his mother and a peacock to ride.

This first part meets the god himself. Part 2 tells the Ṣaṣṭhī story of his great war; Parts 3 and 4 visit his two great serpent shrines in Karnataka, Kukke and Ghāṭi.

Om Śaravaṇabhava,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why so many names for one god?

PROF. BOLT: Because almost every region of India loved him in its own way. Each name keeps one story.

ZAPPY: Is he Śiva's son or Agni's?

PROF. BOLT: Both, depending on the book. The fire carried Śiva's seed; most tellings call him Śiva's son.

HIS NAMES

One god, many stories
Skanda“The one who leaps” or “attacks”: the spurt of Śiva’s energy
Kumāra“The youth”: he never grows old
Kārttikeya“Son of the Kṛttikās”, the six star-mothers who nursed him
Ṣaṇmukha“Six-faced” (Āṟumukham in Tamil)
Śaravaṇabhava“Born in the reeds”
Guha“The hidden one”, who lives in the cave of the heart
SubrahmaṇyaOften read as “dear to the brāhmaṇas” or “of Brahman”; its meaning is debated
MuruganTamil: “the beautiful”, the god of the hills; also Velan, “the spear-bearer”

ZAPPY: Which name should I use?

PROF. BOLT: Any of them. In Karnataka, 'Subbarāya' or 'Subrahmaṇya Svāmi' is what grandmother says.

WHY HE WAS BORN

A demon only a child could kill
The boon. The demon Tāraka won a boon from Brahmā: he could be killed only by a son of Śiva. Śiva, mourning Satī, was lost in meditation and had no wife. Tāraka felt safe.
The terror. Tāraka drove the gods from heaven. Indra and the gods went to Brahmā, who told them: only Śiva’s son can save you.
Kāma’s arrow. The gods sent Kāma, god of love, to wake Śiva. Śiva opened his third eye and burnt Kāma to ash.
Pārvatī’s tapas. Pārvatī won Śiva by her own fierce austerity, and they married. This is the story of Kālidāsa’s poem Kumārasambhava, “The Birth of the Prince”.
The spark. From Śiva came a blaze of energy so fierce that no one could hold it. Where it went next is the story of the birth.

BORN IN THE REEDS

Fire, river, lake and six stars
Agni carries it. The fire-god Agni took Śiva’s blazing seed, but even he could not bear its heat.
Gaṅgā cools it. He gave it to the river Gaṅgā, who carried it to a lake of reeds, Śaravaṇa, in the Himalaya.
Six boys. There it became six shining boys, each on a lotus. In the Tamil Kanda Purāṇam, six sparks from Śiva’s six faces go straight there.
Six mothers. The six Kṛttikās, the stars of the Pleiades, came down and nursed them. So he is Kārttikeya.
One child. Pārvatī came and embraced all six at once, and they became one child with six faces and twelve arms.

ZAPPY: Why six?

PROF. BOLT: Six mothers, six faces, six days of war, the sixth lunar day. With Skanda, everything comes in sixes.

THE SIX FACES

What Ṣaṇmukha sees
Six directionsNorth, south, east, west, above and below: he sees in every direction at once
Six mothersOne face for each Kṛttikā who nursed him
Six seasonsThe six ṛtus of the Indian year
Six qualitiesA common teaching: wisdom, dispassion, strength, fame, wealth and divine power
Six syllablesHis six-syllabled name, Śa-ra-va-ṇa-bha-va, chanted by devotees as “Om Śaravaṇabhava”
In artSix-faced images are firmly established after about the 6th century; earlier ones usually show one face

ZAPPY: Are these meanings in the old books?

PROF. BOLT: Some are, some are later teaching. The six mothers are the oldest reason.

THE VEL, THE PEACOCK, THE ROOSTER

His three signs
VelA spear with a leaf-shaped blade, given by his mother Pārvatī. Tamil devotees read its long shaft and broad tip as knowledge: deep, wide and sharp
PeacockHis mount, Paravāṇi in Tamil. In the Tamil telling, it is half of the defeated demon Śūrapadma; many read it as pride tamed
RoosterOn his banner (Cēval-koṭi); the other half of Śūrapadma, crowing at dawn
SnakeUnder the peacock’s feet in many images: the peacock eats snakes, yet in Karnataka the god himself is a serpent. More in Parts 3 and 4
“Vetrivel Muruganukku Arōharā!”: Victory to Murugan of the conquering spear.

THE TEACHER OF HIS FATHER

The meaning of Om
A question. The boy Skanda asked Brahmā, the creator, what Om (the Praṇava) means. Brahmā could not explain it.
The prison. Skanda locked Brahmā up and took over creation himself.
The father asks. Śiva came to free Brahmā and asked his son: “Then you tell me what Om means.”
Svāminātha. Śiva sat as a disciple and the boy whispered the meaning in his ear. So he is Svāminātha, “teacher of the Lord”, at Svāmimalai.
The point. Wisdom does not depend on age. This is a Tamil temple tradition; versions differ.

THE FRUIT OF WISDOM

Round the world, or round your parents?
One fruit. The sage Nārada brought a single golden fruit (a mango, in most tellings) to Śiva and Pārvatī. Who should have it?
The race. “Whoever goes round the world first,” said Śiva. Skanda leapt on his peacock and flew off.
Gaṇeśa’s answer. Gaṇeśa simply walked round his parents: “You are my world.” He won the fruit.
Palani. Skanda came back, felt cheated, gave up his finery and went to live alone on the hill of Palani, as a young renunciant with a staff.
Pazham nī. Pārvatī followed: “Pazham nī”, “you are the fruit.” Folk etymology hears “Palani” in it.

ZAPPY: So who was right?

PROF. BOLT: Both. Gaṇeśa shows love for parents; Skanda shows that the seeker must leave home. The fruit was the lesson.

DEVASENĀ AND VALLI

Two wives, two worlds
DevasenāDaughter of Indra (in the Skanda Purāṇa; Dakṣa’s in the Mahābhārata). Her name means “the army of the gods”: the general marries his army. In Tamil, Devayānai
ValliDaughter of a hill-tribe chief, who guarded millet fields with a sling. Murugan wooed her disguised as a hunter and an old man, with Gaṇeśa’s help as a wild elephant
The pairMany read them as the two powers: Devasenā, action (kriyā); Valli, desire or love (icchā); the vel, knowledge (jñāna)
KukkeKarnataka’s tradition places the wedding with Devasenā near Kukke on Mārgaśīrṣa Ṣaṣṭhī: see Part 3
NorthIn many northern traditions he stays a bachelor, and women traditionally kept out of some of his shrines

THE SIX ABODES

Āṟupaṭai Vīṭu of Tamil Nadu
TirupparaṅkuṉṟamNear Madurai: his wedding with Devasenā
TiruccendūrOn the seashore: victory over Śūrapadma; the great Sūrasaṃhāram of Skanda Ṣaṣṭhī
PaḻaniThe hill of the fruit: the renunciant boy with a staff (Daṇḍāyudhapāṇi)
SvāmimalaiNear Kumbakoṇam: the teacher of Om
TiruttaṇiNear Chennai: peace after the war, and his marriage to Valli
PaḻamudircōlaiNear Madurai, in the forest: Valli and Devasenā together

ZAPPY: Six again?

PROF. BOLT: Of course. The 'six camps' come from Nakkīrar's Tirumurukāṟṟuppaṭai, a guide for pilgrims well over 1,600 years old.

Many read the six as six chapters of his life: birth of wisdom, war, renunciation, teaching, peace, union.

HOW OLD IS HE?

From the Vedas to Kuṣāṇa coins
VedasA bright boy called Kumāra appears in the Ṛg Veda; the Taittirīya Āraṇyaka names “Ṣaṇmukha”
UpaniṣadThe Chāndogya Upaniṣad calls the sage Sanatkumāra by the name Skanda
GrammarPāṇini and Patañjali know Skanda; Kauṭilya’s Arthaśāstra too
EpicsThe Rāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata tell his birth, with Agni and Gaṅgā
CoinsKuṣāṇa and Yaudheya coins (1st–2nd c. CE) show him with spear and peacock
TamilSangam poems (early centuries CE) make Murugan god of the hills; the Tirumurukāṟṟuppaṭai gives six faces and twelve arms
PurāṇaThe Skanda Purāṇa, the largest Purāṇa, carries his name; an early version existed by the 6th c.
PoemKālidāsa’s Kumārasambhava (about 5th c.)

SUBRAHMAṆYA IN KARNATAKA

The god who is a serpent
Serpent formAlong the west coast and across Karnataka, Subrahmaṇya is worshipped as a nāga, a cobra, often seven-hooded
AnthillsThe anthill (huttu), the cobra’s home, is worshipped as his shrine
Nāga stonesStone serpent images under trees; installed after vows, often for children
Why?An old serpent worship of the coast and the Malnāḍ joined the cult of Skanda. Scholars and traditions differ on how
Great shrinesKukke Subrahmaṇya (Part 3) and Ghāṭi Subrahmaṇya (Part 4)
FestivalsNāgara Pañcamī and Subrahmaṇya Ṣaṣṭhī

Retold from tradition: the Praṇava story (Svāmimalai) and the fruit story (Palani) are Tamil temple traditions; Kālidāsa’s Kumārasambhava for Tāraka and Kāma.

Interpretations: the meanings of the six faces, the vel as knowledge, Valli and Devasenā as icchā and kriyā are common teachings, not fixed doctrine. Traditions differ on the meaning of “Subrahmaṇya”, on whether Gaṇeśa or Skanda is elder, and on how serpent worship joined Skanda’s cult. Meanings are our own plain-English renderings.
