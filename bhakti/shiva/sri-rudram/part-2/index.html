Namaḥ Śivāya, the river crossing and the Mahāmṛtyuñjaya · explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: First the whole of Part 2 for chanting in one go. Then the story and background, and two pages for each section: the chant, a picture, what it says and the key words; then a deeper look, background facts, the inner teaching and the one thing to remember.

FOREWORD

In Part 1, the Rudram bowed to Rudra's anger, calmed it, and then began finding him everywhere: in trees and roads, thieves and potters, rivers and islands.

Part 2 goes deeper. It finds Rudra in the eldest and the youngest, the cloud and the well, the bed and the fallen leaf. At its centre is the line that gave us namaḥ śivāya. Then the hymn turns into a family's prayer: keep our old and our young safe. It asks the Lord to hang his weapon on a tree and visit us as a guest, and it ends with the Mahāmṛtyuñjaya, the great prayer for freedom from death.

May the One who carries us across bring every reader safely to the far shore.

THE STORY · THE BOY WHO BEAT DEATH

Mārkaṇḍeya and Śiva Kālāntaka
The Purāṇic story in 5 frames
A hard choice. The sage Mṛkaṇḍu and his wife pray for a son. Śiva offers them a choice: a dull son who lives long, or a wise, devoted son who will live only sixteen years. They choose the wise one.
A devoted boy. Mārkaṇḍeya grows up loving Śiva, worshipping the liṅga every day.
Death arrives. On his sixteenth birthday, Yama, lord of death, comes for him. The boy throws his arms around the liṅga and won't let go.
Kālāntaka. Yama's noose falls around the boy and the liṅga together. Śiva bursts out of the liṅga and strikes death down: Kālāntaka, "the end of death".
Forever sixteen. Mārkaṇḍeya lives on, always sixteen. His story is tied to the Mahāmṛtyuñjaya and to the temple at Tirukkaḍaiyūr in Tamil Nadu.

The boy

Mārkaṇḍeya, later a great sage.

The threat

Yama and his noose (pāśa).

The answer

Mṛtyuñjaya, Śiva who conquers death.

ZAPPY: So hugging the liṅga saved him?

PROF. BOLT: Holding on to the Auspicious when everything is falling apart. That's what the end of the Namakam asks for too.

Part 2 ends with the prayer Mārkaṇḍeya lived: free me from death, not from the deathless.

ABOUT PART 2

The heart of the Rudram, and the way out of fear

🕉️ The Pañcākṣarī

na-maḥ-śi-vā-ya, five syllables, first found in anuvāka 8. With Om it becomes the six-syllable oṃ namaḥ śivāya.

🥒 The Mahāmṛtyuñjaya

Ṛg Veda 7.59.12, attributed to Vasiṣṭha, also in the Yajur Veda. Chanted at the end of the Namakam.

A ring. The Namakam begins and ends with verses (anuvākas 1, 10 and 11) and has the "namo" lists in between. The middle (anuvākas 2–9) finds Rudra in everything; the verse ends ask him, first and last, to lay down his weapons and heal.

The style keeps changing. Anuvākas 6 to 9 continue the "ca … ca" pairs from Part 1. Anuvāka 8 is the centre: Śambhu, Śaṅkara, Śiva. Anuvāka 9 ends in the hearts of the gods. Then the verse returns in anuvāka 10 as a family's prayer, and anuvāka 11 sweeps across earth, sky and heaven.

Where it leads. After the Namakam comes the Camakam, "and to me": having bowed to the One in everything, we ask for everything we need. That's Part 3.

ANUVĀKA 6 · GOING DEEPER

ELDEST AND YOUNGEST

Every age. The anuvāka opens with age: eldest and youngest, born first and born later, the middle one and the one not yet grown. In a family, a village or the cosmos, Rudra is both the oldest presence and the newest arrival. Nobody is too old or too young to be divine.

From field to forest. The farmer's world appears: urvarā, the fertile field, and khala, the threshing floor where grain is beaten from the husk. Then the forest and the thicket. The tamed and the wild again, held together.

Sound and echo. śravāya ca pratiśravāya ca: to sound, and to its echo. Commentators read it as the call and its answer, or the Veda and its repetition. Every word spoken and every word that comes back is Rudra.

💡 Ślokya · Avasānya

"Praised in verses, and at the end": commentators read "the end" as the Upaniṣads, the end of the Veda.

💡 Armour

Varmin, varūthin, bilmin, kavacin: armour, shelter, helmet, breastplate. The fierce archer is also the one who shields.

Respect the elders and the young alike. The same divine looks out of both faces.

ZAPPY: How can anyone be both the eldest and the youngest?

PROF. BOLT: If he's in everyone, he is the grandfather and the newborn at the same time.

Old and young, first and last, sound and echo: every stage of life is His.

ANUVĀKA 7 · GOING DEEPER

DRUM, CLOUD, WELL AND HOME

Water, everywhere. Count the kinds of water named: streams, channels, marshes, lakes, rivers, ponds, wells and pits. For people who lived by the monsoon, water was life, and Rudra is in every drop of it.

Weather. Rain and no rain, cloud and lightning, clear autumn sky and sunshine, wind and storm. Rudra began as a storm god; here every mood of the sky, gentle and destructive, is saluted as his.

Vāstupa. The anuvāka ends at home: vāstavya, the one who lives in the house, and vāstupa, its guardian. The fierce god of the wild is also the household god. The same word lives on in vāstu, the traditional science of building.

💡 Dūta

"Messenger". In a Tamil Śaiva story, Śiva himself goes as messenger for the poet-saint Sundarar.

💡 Vāstu Puruṣa

Traditional builders still honour the spirit of the site before laying a foundation.

Weather, water, work and home: the everyday is where the divine is met, not only the temple.

ZAPPY: Rain AND drought?

PROF. BOLT: The farmer prays in both. Rudra is in the flood and in the dry spell; that's how the Veda sees weather.

From the drum to the well to the front door: Rudra guards the house you live in.

ANUVĀKA 8 · GOING DEEPER

NAMAḤ ŚIVĀYA

The heart. Anuvāka 8 sits near the middle of the Namakam, and in the middle of it is the line namaḥ śivāya ca śivatarāya ca. From here comes the Pañcākṣarī, na-maḥ-śi-vā-ya, the five-syllable mantra; with Om, six syllables. Tradition calls the Rudram the heart of the Veda, and this line the heart of the Rudram.

Three names of peace. Śambhu (from whom joy, śam, arises), Śaṅkara (maker of śam), Śiva (auspiciousness itself). The fierce lord of the earlier anuvākas is now named with three words that all mean peace and blessing. Śivatara, "more auspicious", goes further: whatever good you can imagine, he is beyond it.

The river crossing. Then the picture becomes a river: the ford (tīrtha), the bank, the far shore (pāra) and this shore (avāra), the one who ferries us over. Life is the river; Śiva is both the boat and the far shore. Tīrtha, "crossing place", became the Indian word for a pilgrimage site.

💡 Soma

Read as sa-umā, "with Umā": Śiva together with Pārvatī. Others read it as the moon.

💡 Tāra

"The one who carries across": commentators explain it as Om, which ferries the soul over the ocean of birth and death.

Life is a river to cross. Holding on to the Auspicious, the far shore is never out of reach.

ZAPPY: So this is where 'Om Namaḥ Śivāya' comes from?

PROF. BOLT: Right here, in the middle of the Namakam. The most chanted words in Śaivism are a line of the Veda.

namaḥ śivāya ca śivatarāya ca: bow to the Auspicious, and to the even more Auspicious.

ANUVĀKA 9 · GOING DEEPER

IN DUST, LEAVES AND THE HEARTS OF THE GODS

Small places. This anuvāka walks into the most ordinary corners: the cowshed, the bed, the dust, the dew, the dry stick and the green shoot. It's as if the poet walks through a village pointing: here too, and here.

Leaf and fallen leaf. parṇyāya ca parṇaśadyāya ca: in the leaf, and in the fallen leaf. Growth and decay are one process, and Rudra is in both. For a hymn so concerned with death and healing, it's a gentle piece of wisdom.

The hearts of the gods. Near the end: namo vaḥ kirikebhyo devānāṃ hṛdayebhyaḥ, salutations to you who scatter blessings, the hearts of the gods. The Rudras are not just forces outside; they are the innermost core of the divine itself.

💡 Gṛhya · Talpya

In the house and in the bed: Rudra watches even over our rest and sleep.

💡 Kirika

"Scatterer": one who scatters gifts, the way clouds scatter rain.

No place is too small or too plain to be holy. The bed, the cowshed and the dust on the road are all in His care.

ZAPPY: A fallen leaf is divine?

PROF. BOLT: The green leaf on the tree and the dry one on the ground. Growing and decaying, both are Rudra.

From cowshed to bedroom, green leaf to dry, and finally the gods' own hearts.

ANUVĀKA 10 · VERSES 1–6 · GOING DEEPER

SPARE OUR FAMILIES

The healing form. Verse 2 echoes the first anuvāka, yā te rudra śivā tanūḥ, your gentle form, but now adds śivā viśvāha bheṣajī: gentle, and medicine every day. Rudra's gentle side isn't just the absence of anger; it actively heals.

The whole village. Verse 3 asks for viśvaṃ puṣṭaṃ grāme asminn anāturam: everyone in this village nourished and free of suffering, two-footed and four-footed. Community health in a single line.

Mā no mahāntam. Verses 5 and 6 are among the most heartfelt in the Veda: don't harm our old or our young, our father, our mother, our children, our cattle. Both verses also appear in the Ṛg Veda (1.114.7–8), in a hymn to Rudra by the sage Kutsa.

💡 Nīlalohita

"Blue and red": Rudra's two colours, the dark throat and the red body, sometimes read as night sky and dawn.

💡 Kṣayadvīra

"Ruler of heroes": an old Ṛg Vedic title of Rudra.

The deepest prayers are simple: health for the people we love. Saying it every day keeps our priorities straight.

ZAPPY: This sounds like any parent's prayer.

PROF. BOLT: It is. Three thousand years ago people asked exactly what we ask today: keep my family safe and well.

Old and young, father and mother, children and cattle: keep them all safe.

ANUVĀKA 10 · VERSES 7–12 · GOING DEEPER

COME WITH THE BOW UNSTRUNG

A guest, not a warrior. Verse 10 paints a lovely picture: put your weapon on the highest tree, wear your hide, and come to us. A fearsome warrior leaves his arms outside and arrives as a guest. Śiva's famous hide garment begins here, with kṛtti.

Pināka. He keeps just one thing: the Pināka, his bow. It becomes Śiva's signature weapon, and one of his names is Pinākin, "holder of the Pināka". In the Rāmāyaṇa, the great bow Rāma breaks to win Sītā is Śiva's bow.

Points turned away. The anuvāka ends: thousands of weapons are in your arms, Lord; turn their faces away from us. The prayer never asks Rudra to stop being powerful. It asks that the power point away from us, the same idea as anuvāka 1.

💡 Fearsome as a wild beast

Verse 8, stuhi śrutaṃ gartasadaṃ yuvānam, is also in the Ṛg Veda (2.33.11).

💡 Kṛttivāsa

"Clothed in a hide": a name of Śiva. Temple images show him wearing a tiger or elephant skin.

Strength becomes trust when it is set aside. The people we love most are the ones we meet without our armour.

ZAPPY: Hang the weapon on a tree and come over? Like a friend visiting?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly that. Leave the battle outside and come in wearing simple clothes.

Hang your weapon on the highest tree, put on your simple hide, and come to us.

ANUVĀKA 11 · GOING DEEPER

THOUSANDS OF RUDRAS

Unstrung a thousand yojanas away. The refrain teṣāṃ sahasrayojane'va dhanvāni tanmasi follows every verse: their bows we unstring a thousand yojanas away. The prayer travels round the universe (earth, sky, heaven, trees, food, roads, holy places) asking each power to lay down its bow far from us.

Food, wind and rain. The Rudras of the earth have food for arrows; of the sky, wind; of heaven, rain. The things we need most can also harm us: spoiled food, storms, floods. The same thing can be medicine or poison; it depends on the dose.

Ten in every direction. daśa prācīḥ daśa dakṣiṇāḥ…: ten to the east, south, west, north and upward. Commentators read the ten as the ten fingers joined in añjali: salutations with folded hands, all round. Then the last line hands hatred itself, ours and others', into Rudra's jaws.

💡 Yojana

An old measure of distance, many kilometres (estimates vary). A thousand yojanas simply means "as far away as possible".

💡 No more enemies

"The one we hate and the one who hates us": both are handed over. The prayer gives up our own hatred too.

Hand over the hatred: the hate we hold and the hate held against us. Carrying neither is the real protection.

ZAPPY: Their arrows are food, wind and rain?

PROF. BOLT: The same things that keep us alive can harm us. Bad food, a storm, a flood: those are Rudra's arrows too.

Food, wind and rain are Rudra's arrows: the gifts that feed us can also hurt us.

THE CLOSING · TRYAMBAKAM · GOING DEEPER

THE MAHĀMṚTYUÑJAYA AND THE HEALING HAND

The death-conquering mantra. Tryambakaṃ yajāmahe is Ṛg Veda 7.59.12, attributed to the sage Vasiṣṭha, and it also appears in the Yajur Veda. It closes the Namakam, and on its own it's chanted millions of times a day: for health, in illness, and beside the dying.

The ripe cucumber. The image is exact. An unripe cucumber has to be torn from the vine; a ripe one drops by itself. The mantra asks for the soul to ripen so that release comes naturally, without tearing. And it adds mā amṛtāt: not from immortality. Free me from death, never from the deathless.

My hand is the healer. Ayaṃ me hasto bhagavān (also Ṛg Veda 10.60.12): this hand of mine is blessed, it holds every medicine, its touch is auspicious. After so many verses asking Rudra to heal, the chanter discovers the healing power in their own hands.

💡 Tryambaka

"Three-eyed", or "of the three mothers" (earth, sky, heaven), by different readings.

💡 Rudra and Viṣṇu

namo rudrāya viṣṇave: the closing words salute Rudra as Viṣṇu. One divine, two names.

Ripen, don't cling. A life lived fully lets go gracefully when the time comes; until then, our own hands can heal.

ZAPPY: Why a cucumber, of all things?

PROF. BOLT: When it's ripe, it drops from the vine by itself. No tearing, no pain. That's how the mantra asks to be released.

Like a ripe cucumber from its vine: free from death, never from the deathless.
