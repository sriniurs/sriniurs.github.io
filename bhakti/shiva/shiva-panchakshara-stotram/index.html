FOREWORD

Oṃ namaḥ śivāya may be the most chanted words in all of Śaiva devotion. Five syllables, na-maḥ-śi-vā-ya, taken from the heart of the Yajur Veda's Śrī Rudram, repeated on rudrākṣa beads by millions every day.

Ādi Śaṅkara gave those syllables a song. In his Śiva Pañcākṣara Stotram, each of the five verses begins with one syllable and ends by saluting Śiva as that syllable. Sing the five verses, and you have sung the mantra, while seeing Śiva five ways: serpent-garlanded and ash-smeared; bathed in the Gaṅgā; the sun to Pārvatī's face; worshipped by the great sages; the mysterious Yakṣa who humbled the gods.

May every reader carry these five syllables close, and find them enough.

Oṃ Namaḥ Śivāya,

THE STORY · THE KING AND THE CROWS

Queen Kalāvatī and King Dāśārha, from the Skanda Purāṇa
The story in 5 frames
A burning touch. King Dāśārha of Mathurā marries Princess Kalāvatī of Kāśī. But when he reaches for her hand, he pulls back: her touch burns like fire.
The queen's secret. Kalāvatī explains: as a girl she received the five-syllable mantra of Śiva from the sage Durvāsā. Its power has made her pure as fire, and the king carries the stains of past wrongs.
To the sage. Humbled, the king goes with her to the sage Garga and asks to be made pure.
The crows. Garga has him bathe, places a hand on his head and gives him namaḥ śivāya. At once a flock of crows flies out of the king's body, his sins, and burns away in the air.
Made new. Now pure, the king takes his queen's hand without pain. The two live the rest of their lives devoted to Śiva.

The queen

Kalāvatī, who knew the mantra.

The sage

Garga, who taught the king.

The mantra

Namaḥ Śivāya.

ZAPPY: Five syllables did all that?

PROF. BOLT: That's the Purāṇa's way of saying: sincerely taken up, the simplest mantra can change a whole life.

Five syllables, sincerely received, made a stained king pure.

ABOUT THE PAÑCĀKṢARA

Five syllables, five verses

📜 The mantra

namaḥ śivāya first appears in the Śrī Rudram (anuvāka 8) of the Yajur Veda. With Oṃ it becomes the six-syllable oṃ namaḥ śivāya.

✍️ The hymn

Attributed to Ādi Śaṅkara: five verses, each beginning with one syllable, plus a phalaśruti.

Five elements. A popular teaching links the syllables to the five elements: NA earth, MA water, ŚI fire, VĀ air, YA space. Chanting the mantra is then a bow to the whole of creation. (The five great elemental Śiva temples of South India carry the same idea.)

The Śaiva Siddhānta reading. In the Tamil Śaiva tradition, ŚI is Śiva, VĀ his grace (Śakti), YA the soul, and NA and MA the veils that hide Śiva from the soul. As the chant moves on, the soul turns from its veils toward grace and Śiva.

How it's used. The mantra is chanted 108 times on rudrākṣa beads; the stotram is sung at pūjā, on Mondays, in Śrāvaṇa, and through Mahāśivarātri night.

NA · GOING DEEPER

NA · SERPENT-GARLANDED, THREE-EYED, PURE

The plan of the hymn. Śaṅkara builds it like an acrostic. Each verse starts with its syllable (nāgendra, mandākinī, śivāya, vasiṣṭha, yakṣa) and ends by naming it: tasmai nakārāya, "to Him who is NA". Chant the five verses and you have chanted namaḥ śivāya.

Ash. Bhasma is what remains when everything burns. Śiva wears it all over his body: a reminder that all forms end, and that what remains is pure. Devotees wear it as three lines on the forehead.

Eternal, pure, owning nothing. Nitya, śuddha, digambara: never born, never stained, wearing only the sky. The first syllable sets out who Śiva is: completely free.

💡 Tripuṇḍra

Three horizontal lines of vibhūti on the forehead: the mark of Śiva's devotees.

💡 Vāsuki

The king of serpents, the churning rope at the ocean's churning, coiled at Śiva's neck.

Ash is what remains when everything burns. Wearing it says: I know what lasts and what doesn't.

ZAPPY: What does 'tasmai nakārāya' mean?

PROF. BOLT: "To Him who is the syllable NA." Each verse gives Śiva one syllable of namaḥ śivāya. Five verses, five syllables.

Verse by verse, Śaṅkara spells out NA-MAḤ-ŚI-VĀ-YA. This is NA.

MA · GOING DEEPER

MA · BATHED IN THE GAṄGĀ, WORSHIPPED WITH FLOWERS

A pūjā in one verse. Water, sandal paste, flowers: the three basic steps of Śiva worship. Mandākinī is the Gaṅgā as she flows in heaven; even the liṅga's bath-water comes from the sky.

Nandi and the gaṇas. Nandīśvara-pramatha-nātha: Lord of Nandi and of the pramathas, Śiva's wild and loyal attendants. Śiva isn't alone; he is surrounded by devotees who serve him.

Heavenly flowers. Mandāra is one of the five wish-granting trees of Indra's heaven. The verse imagines Śiva worshipped with the finest flowers there are, and many more besides (bahupuṣpa).

💡 Pañca-vṛkṣa

Indra's five heavenly trees: mandāra, pārijāta, santāna, kalpavṛkṣa and haricandana.

💡 Pramatha

Śiva's wild attendants, the gaṇas, led by Nandi.

Even without a temple, worship can happen in the heart: the water of attention, the sandal of kindness, the flowers of good thoughts.

ZAPPY: This sounds like an abhiṣeka.

PROF. BOLT: It is one, in words: water, sandal, flowers. The verse is a whole pūjā.

Water, sandal, flowers: MA is the pūjā itself.

ŚI · GOING DEEPER

ŚI · THE SUN FOR GAURĪ'S LOTUS FACE

A love image. Gaurī-vadanābja-vṛnda-sūrya: the sun to the lotus of Gaurī's face. Lotuses open when the sun rises; Pārvatī's face blossoms when she sees Śiva. In the middle of a hymn to the Lord of yogis, a gentle line about love.

Dakṣa again. Dakṣādhvara-nāśaka: destroyer of Dakṣa's sacrifice. Satī gave up her life at that sacrifice; she was reborn as Pārvatī, Gaurī, and won Śiva back by her penance. The two halves of the line belong to one story.

ŚI at the centre. The middle syllable of the five. In Śaiva Siddhānta, ŚI stands for Śiva himself, with the others around him. It's fitting that this verse begins with the word śivāya.

💡 Gaurī

"The fair one": Pārvatī. In Karnataka, Gaurī is worshipped at Gaurī Habba, just before Gaṇeśa Caturthī.

💡 Vṛṣadhvaja

"Bull-bannered": Nandi on Śiva's flag, as seen on temple flagstaffs.

Love that lights up another's face is itself divine. Be someone's sun.

ZAPPY: Śiva is a sun to Pārvatī?

PROF. BOLT: As a lotus opens when the sun rises, her face lights up when she sees Him. It's a love poem in one line.

As the lotus blooms for the sun, Gaurī's face lights up for Śiva.

VĀ · GOING DEEPER

VĀ · WORSHIPPED BY THE GREAT SAGES

Three great sages. Vasiṣṭha, seer of the Mahāmṛtyuñjaya mantra; Agastya, the pot-born sage who drank the ocean and went south; Gautama, who, tradition says, brought the Godāvarī down at Tryambakeśvara. All of them bow at Śiva's head.

Śekhara. "Head, crest". It's the head of Śiva that bears the moon and the Gaṅgā, and that the sages worship: the highest point, honoured by the highest minds.

Three eyes. Candrārka-vaiśvānara-locana: whose eyes are moon, sun and fire. The moon cools, the sun lights, the fire transforms. The whole of nature's light looks out through Śiva.

💡 Agastya

The sage said to have brought Vedic learning south, honoured in Tamil tradition as the father of Tamil grammar.

💡 Tryambakeśvara

The jyotirliṅga near Nashik, at the source of the Godāvarī, linked with Gautama.

Even the wisest bow. Learning that ends in humility is real learning.

ZAPPY: Agastya was born in a pot?

PROF. BOLT: So the story goes: born from a pot, small in size, yet so mighty he drank up the whole ocean.

The greatest sages bow to Him; His eyes are moon, sun and fire.

YA · GOING DEEPER

YA · THE MYSTERIOUS YAKṢA

The Yakṣa story. In the Kena Upaniṣad, the gods win a battle and grow proud. A mysterious Yakṣa appears. Agni goes to it and boasts he can burn anything; the Yakṣa puts down a blade of grass, and Agni cannot burn it. Vāyu cannot blow it away. Then Umā appears and tells Indra: that was Brahman, from whom all your power comes.

The point. Every power we have, even the gods' power, is borrowed. Śaṅkara calls Śiva yakṣa-svarūpa: the very Brahman who humbled the gods.

The ring closes. The last syllable ends with digambara, "sky-clad", the same word as the first verse. NA and YA, beginning and end, meet in the one who owns nothing and contains everything.

💡 Kena Upaniṣad

"By whom?": it asks what power makes the mind think and the eye see.

💡 Umā Haimavatī

The Kena's Umā, "daughter of the Himālaya", is one of the earliest appearances of the Goddess as teacher.

Every ability we have is on loan. Remembering that keeps pride from growing.

ZAPPY: Why a Yakṣa?

PROF. BOLT: In the Kena Upaniṣad, Brahman appears to the proud gods as a mysterious Yakṣa. Agni can't burn a single blade of grass in front of it.

The Yakṣa humbles the gods: all power is borrowed from Him.

PHALAŚRUTI · GOING DEEPER

THE FRUIT OF CHANTING

A shared ending. The phalaśruti is the same as the Liṅgāṣṭakam's: reach Śiva's world, and rejoice together with Śiva. Śaiva hymns often share these closing lines.

The mantra itself. Namaḥ śivāya first appears in the eighth anuvāka of the Śrī Rudram, in the Yajur Veda. Śaṅkara's hymn unfolds those five syllables into five verses, so that singing the hymn is chanting the mantra.

Everywhere. In the Tamil Śaiva tradition, Māṇikkavācakar's Śivapurāṇam opens namaccivāya vāḻka, "long live na-ma-śi-vā-ya". From the Veda to the Tamil saints, the five syllables run through the whole Śaiva world.

💡 Tiruvācakam

Māṇikkavācakar's Tamil hymns to Śiva; the Śivapurāṇam opens with the five syllables.

💡 Japa

The Pañcākṣarī is traditionally chanted 108 times on rudrākṣa beads.

Five syllables are enough to carry a lifetime of prayer. Keep them close.

ZAPPY: Same ending as the Liṅgāṣṭakam?

PROF. BOLT: Yes: several Śiva hymns share this closing verse. The promise is the same: rejoice with Śiva.

Five syllables, and joy in Śiva's company.
