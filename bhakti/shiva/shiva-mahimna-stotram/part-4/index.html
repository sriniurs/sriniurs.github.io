FOREWORD

The praise ended with verse 32: if the ocean were ink and the earth paper, the goddess of learning still could not finish. What follows are the closing verses.

In verses 33–43 the poet signs his name, tells how he fell from his greatness and sang, promises blessings to those who chant the hymn, and offers it at Śiva's feet as a worship made of words.

And in the middle of them is perhaps the most beautiful line of all: "I do not know what You are, O Lord. Whatever You are, to That I bow, again and again."

THE STORY · THE GANDHARVA FORGIVEN

How the story ends, and how the hymn lives on
The story in 5 frames
The fall. Puṣpadanta, king of the gandharvas, steps on flowers offered to Śiva and loses his divine powers (Part 1).
The song. Fallen and visible, he sings the greatness of the Lord whose offering he trampled.
Forgiven. Pleased, Śiva restores his powers. The hymn itself, in verse 37, says he fell from his greatness and then composed it.
Handed down. The hymn spreads across India; the scholar Madhusūdana Sarasvatī comments on it, reading every verse for both Śiva and Viṣṇu.
Still sung. Śrī Rāmakṛṣṇa is said to have entered samādhi reciting a few verses; Swami Vivekananda quoted verse 7 in Chicago in 1893; families chant it every evening.

The fault

Pride and carelessness.

The cure

A hymn of humble praise.

The gift

The Mahimna, for everyone.

ZAPPY: So his mistake gave the world this hymn?

PROF. BOLT: That's how tradition sees it. A fall, a song, forgiveness, and a gift that has lasted for centuries.

ABOUT THE MAHIMNA

A hymn of thought and devotion

✍️ The author

Ascribed to Puṣpadanta; a later verse names him as the composer. Its real date is not known; it is an old and much-copied hymn.

🎵 The sound

Written in śikhariṇī, seventeen syllables a line with a pause after the sixth, and chanted to a much-loved traditional tune.

The shape. Verses 1–3 apologise for praising the unpraisable; 4–9 answer doubters and debaters; 10–24 tell Śiva's great deeds; 25–31 turn to Oṃ, the eight names and salutations; 32 is the famous "if the ocean were ink" verse; the rest are closing verses.

Read two ways. The 16th-century Advaita scholar Madhusūdana Sarasvatī wrote a commentary reading every verse as praise of both Śiva and Viṣṇu.

Beloved by saints. Śrī Rāmakṛṣṇa is said to have gone into samādhi while reciting only a few of its verses.

VERSE 33

THE SINGER SIGNS HIS NAME
asura-sura-munīndrairarcitasyendu-mauleḥgrathita-guṇamahimnonirguṇasyeśvarasya |sakala-gaṇa-variṣṭhaḥpuṣpadantābhidhānaḥruciramalaghuvṛttaiḥstotrametaccakāra ‖33‖
For the moon-crowned Lord, worshipped by demons, gods and the great sages, whose glory of qualities is woven into this hymn, though He Himself is beyond all qualities: the best of all the gaṇas, named Puṣpadanta, made this hymn in beautiful, stately metres.
The poet names himself: Puṣpadanta, foremost of Śiva's attendants, composed this hymn in lovely metres for the moon-crowned Lord, who is beyond all qualities yet praised through them.

VERSE 33 · GOING DEEPER

THE SINGER SIGNS HIS NAME

The signature. Indian poets often name themselves near the end of a work, a little like a painter's signature. Here the composer is called Puṣpadanta, foremost of Śiva's attendants, the gaṇas.

With and without qualities. The verse holds both ideas from the start of the hymn: Śiva is nirguṇa, beyond qualities, yet His qualities are "woven" into every verse. That is the whole method of the Mahimna: praising the One beyond praise.

A change of metre. From verse 31 the hymn has moved into longer, statelier metres (alaghu-vṛtta), and the poet points this out himself.

💡 Gaṇas

Śiva's host of attendants; their leader is Gaṇeśa, "Lord of the gaṇas".

💡 Gandharva and gaṇa

The story calls Puṣpadanta a gandharva; this verse counts him among Śiva's gaṇas.

Every true praise is signed by a person and carried by a people. A name at the end is a promise: someone lived this.

ZAPPY: The poet writes his own name in the hymn?

PROF. BOLT: Many Sanskrit and Indian poets did. It's how we know the tradition of Puṣpadanta isn't just a story: the hymn names him.

Puṣpadanta signs the hymn: a praise of the Lord beyond all qualities.

VERSE 34 · GOING DEEPER

THE FRUIT OF DAILY CHANTING

The phalaśruti. From here on the hymn gives its phalaśruti, "the hearing of the fruit": the traditional verses at the end of a hymn that describe the blessings of reciting it. Almost every great stotra has one.

The conditions. Notice what is asked first: parama-bhaktyā, with supreme devotion, and śuddha-cittaḥ, with a pure mind, ahar-ahar, every day. The promise rests on the practice.

Two worlds. Blessings are promised for both: closeness to Śiva after this life, and wellbeing in this one.

💡 Phalaśruti

The closing verses that tell the fruit of reciting a text.

💡 Dhūrjaṭi

"Bearing a heavy load of matted hair": an old name of Śiva.

The promise of a daily prayer is less in any single day than in the person you become by keeping it.

ZAPPY: Wealth and fame, just by chanting?

PROF. BOLT: The verse asks for devotion and a pure mind, every day. That kind of daily practice changes a life. The rest is the hymn's promise.

Chanted daily with a pure heart: blessings here and hereafter.

VERSES 35–36

NOTHING HIGHER
maheśānnāparo devomahimno nāparā stutiḥ |aghorānnāparo mantronāsti tattvaṃ guroḥ param ‖35‖dīkṣā dānaṃ tapastīrthaṃjñānaṃ yāgādikāḥ kriyāḥ |mahimnastava pāṭhasyakalāṃ nārhanti ṣoḍaśīm ‖36‖

VERSES 35–36 · GOING DEEPER

NOTHING HIGHER

Verse 35. Often chanted on its own, it names four things as supreme: God (Maheśa), praise (the Mahimna), mantra (Aghora) and the teacher (guru). Aghora is one of the five Pañca-Brahma mantras, "aghorebhyo'tha ghorebhyaḥ…" (see our Pañca-Brahma article).

Verse 36. A typical phalaśruti flourish: initiation, charity, austerity, pilgrimage, learning and sacrifices are not worth a sixteenth of reciting the hymn. "A sixteenth" (ṣoḍaśī kalā) is a classic Indian way of saying "not even a fraction".

Reading it rightly. Such verses praise the hymn by comparison; they are not telling us to stop pilgrimages or charity.

💡 Aghora

"Not terrible": the gracious-fierce face of Śiva and one of His five faces.

💡 Guru

The verse ends with the teacher, the one who shows the way to all the rest.

High praise of one practice is not contempt for the others. It is a way of saying: this, done with love, contains them all.

ZAPPY: Is the hymn really better than a pilgrimage?

PROF. BOLT: That's the phalaśruti's way of praising it. Every hymn says something like this about itself. Its point: never underestimate sincere praise.

Maheśa, the Mahimna, Aghora, the guru: none higher.

VERSE 37 · GOING DEEPER

THE FALL OF KUSUMADAŚANA

The story confirmed. This verse is the basis of the story in Part 1. It says the poet fell from his own mahiman, greatness, through Śiva's anger, and then praised Śiva's mahiman. The word that names the hymn appears on both sides of his fall.

Two names, one poet. Kusuma and puṣpa both mean "flower"; daśana and danta both mean "tooth". The poet's name is said two ways.

The fall as a gift. Without the fall, no hymn. Tradition treats Puṣpadanta's mistake as the reason the world has the Mahimna.

💡 Mahiman

"Greatness": the poet lost his own, and sang of Śiva's.

💡 Gandharvas

Celestial musicians of Indra's court, masters of song.

Losing our own greatness can be the start of seeing a greater one. A fall is sometimes the first verse of a hymn.

ZAPPY: So the story is in the hymn itself?

PROF. BOLT: This verse is where it comes from. He lost his greatness, and sang of Śiva's.

He fell from his own greatness, and sang of Śiva's.

VERSE 38 · GOING DEEPER

WITH FOLDED HANDS

The posture. Prāñjali, hands joined in añjali, and nānya-cetāḥ, mind on nothing else: the verse describes how to recite, not just how often.

Suraguru. "Guru of the gods" usually means Bṛhaspati; here many readers take it as Śiva Himself, the teacher of the gods, since He alone is called the cause of both heaven and liberation. Translations differ.

Kinnaras. Celestial musicians, half human and half horse or bird in old art. The singer who praised Śiva will himself be praised in song.

💡 Añjali

Joined palms: the gesture of offering and respect, as in namaste.

💡 Amogha

"Unfailing", "never in vain": a word often used of divine weapons and boons.

How we pray matters as much as what we say: hands joined, mind gathered.

ZAPPY: Who is the guru of the gods here?

PROF. BOLT: Usually Bṛhaspati, but here many read it as Śiva Himself, the cause of heaven and freedom. Either way, worship first, then recite.

Folded hands, a gathered mind: the hymn never fails.

VERSES 39–40 · GOING DEEPER

AN OFFERING OF WORDS

Worship made of words. Vāṅmayī pūjā: the hymn calls itself a pūjā. Flowers, water and lamps are offered in ritual worship; here the offering is speech.

Back to the flowers. In verse 31 the poet offered "word-flowers". Verse 40 completes it: the whole hymn is laid at Śaṅkara's feet.

Sadāśiva. "Ever-auspicious Śiva": the form of Śiva in whom all five faces and functions are united.

💡 Pūjā

Worship with offerings: flowers, water, light, food, and here, words.

💡 Sadāśiva

The "eternally auspicious" Śiva, often shown five-faced.

Words can be flowers too. A sincere sentence is an offering.

ZAPPY: A pūjā without flowers?

PROF. BOLT: A pūjā made of words. Puṣpadanta, who stole flowers, now offers only speech. That's his gift.

The whole hymn, laid at Śiva's feet as a pūjā of words.

VERSES 41–42

WHATEVER YOU ARE
tava tattvaṃ na jānāmikīdṛśo'si maheśvara |yādṛśo'si mahādevatādṛśāya namo namaḥ ‖41‖ekakālaṃ dvikālaṃ vātrikālaṃ yaḥ paṭhennaraḥ |sarvapāpa-vinirmuktaḥśiva loke mahīyate ‖42‖

I don't know what You truly are, Lord. Whatever You are, I bow to You. Whoever recites this hymn once, twice or three times a day is freed of all sin.

VERSES 41–42 · GOING DEEPER

WHATEVER YOU ARE

The humblest verse. Verse 41 may be the most beautiful in the whole hymn. After forty verses of praise, arguments, stories and salutations, the poet says simply: I don't know what You are. Whatever You are, I bow to That.

Full circle. Verse 1 began: no one knows the far shore of Your greatness. Verse 41 ends in the same place, but now the not-knowing has become a bow.

Times of day. Verse 42 says reciting at one, two or three of the day's junctions, morning, noon, evening, frees one from sin.

💡 Tādṛśāya

"To such a one", "to That as It is": a bow without definitions.

💡 Trikāla

The three daily junctions for prayer: dawn, noon and dusk.

The deepest prayer may be: I don't know who You are, and I bow anyway.

ZAPPY: After all that, he doesn't know?

PROF. BOLT: That's the wisdom of it. Knowing is limited; bowing isn't. "Whatever You are, I bow to That."

"Whatever You are, to That I bow."

VERSE 43 · GOING DEEPER

FROM PUṢPADANTA'S LOTUS MOUTH

Three ways to keep it. The last verse names three: kaṇṭha-sthita, "standing in the throat", learnt by heart; paṭhita, recited; and samāhita, held in a concentrated mind. Memory, voice and meditation.

Kilbiṣa-hara, hara-priya. A play on sounds: the hymn is hara (a remover) of sin, and priya (dear) to Hara, Śiva.

The colophon. Manuscripts end: iti śrī puṣpadanta-viracitaṃ śivamahimnaḥ stotraṃ samāptam: "thus ends the Śiva Mahimna Stotram composed by Śrī Puṣpadanta".

💡 Kaṇṭhastha

"In the throat": the Indian phrase for knowing a text by heart.

💡 Bhūtapati

"Lord of beings": all living creatures, and also the spirits who attend Śiva.

Learn it by heart, say it aloud, hold it in a quiet mind. A hymn kept this way keeps you in return.

ZAPPY: Is that the very end?

PROF. BOLT: The last verse, and then the colophon naming Puṣpadanta. A flower-thief's apology became one of India's best-loved hymns.

Learnt, recited, reflected on: the Lord of all beings is pleased.
