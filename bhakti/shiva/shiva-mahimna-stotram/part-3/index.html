FOREWORD

Parts 1 and 2 praised Śiva's greatness through argument and story. Part 3 goes deeper: from what Śiva does to what Śiva is.

Verses 23–32 move from the playful to the profound: Pārvatī as half His body, the Lord of the cremation ground who is still the most auspicious, the bliss the yogīs find within, the eight forms, the syllable Oṃ, the eight Vedic names, and a great salutation to the nearest and the farthest.

It ends with the hymn's most famous verse: if the mountains were ink and the earth paper, and the goddess of learning wrote forever, she still could not reach the end of His glory.

THE STORY · BHṚṄGI AND THE HALF-WOMAN LORD

How Śiva and Pārvatī became one body
The story in 5 frames
A single-minded sage. The sage Bhṛṅgi worships Śiva alone. At Kailāsa he walks around Śiva in reverence, but refuses to include Pārvatī.
Pārvatī draws close. To teach him, Pārvatī sits so close to Śiva that he cannot circle one without the other. Bhṛṅgi turns into a bee and tries to fly between them.
One body. Śiva then takes Pārvatī into His own body: the right half Śiva, the left half Pārvatī. This is Ardhanārīśvara, "the Lord who is half woman".
A lesson in strength. Bhṛṅgi still tries to come between them. Pārvatī withdraws from him the strength that comes from her, and he collapses into skin and bones.
The third leg. Bhṛṅgi realises his mistake and honours them both. Śiva gives him a third leg to stand on, and so he is shown in temple sculpture.

The sage

Bhṛṅgi, "the bee".

The form

Ardhanārīśvara: half Śiva, half Śakti.

The lesson

Śiva and Śakti cannot be separated.

ZAPPY: Why does Part 3 start with this story?

PROF. BOLT: Because verse 23 jokes about it: Pārvatī thinks Śiva is lovestruck because He made her half His body. The story tells why He did.

ABOUT THE MAHIMNA

A hymn of thought and devotion

✍️ The author

Ascribed to Puṣpadanta; a later verse names him as the composer. Its real date is not known; it is an old and much-copied hymn.

🎵 The sound

Written in śikhariṇī, seventeen syllables a line with a pause after the sixth, and chanted to a much-loved traditional tune.

The shape. Verses 1–3 apologise for praising the unpraisable; 4–9 answer doubters and debaters; 10–24 tell Śiva's great deeds; 25–31 turn to Oṃ, the eight names and salutations; 32 is the famous "if the ocean were ink" verse; the rest are closing verses.

Read two ways. The 16th-century Advaita scholar Madhusūdana Sarasvatī wrote a commentary reading every verse as praise of both Śiva and Viṣṇu.

Beloved by saints. Śrī Rāmakṛṣṇa is said to have gone into samādhi while reciting only a few of its verses.

VERSE 23

HALF OF HIS BODY
svalāvaṇyāśaṃsādhṛtadhanuṣamahnāya tṛṇavatpuraḥ pluṣṭaṃ dṛṣṭvāpuramathana puṣpāyudhamapi |yadi straiṇaṃ devīyamanirata-dehārdha-ghaṭanātavaiti tvāmaddhābata varada mugdhā yuvatayaḥ ‖23‖
O destroyer of the cities, the Goddess saw Kāma, who took up his bow trusting in her beauty, burnt before her in an instant like straw. If she still thinks You are a slave to a woman, because You, devoted to self-restraint, joined her to half Your body: ah, O giver of boons, young women are innocent indeed!

VERSE 23 · GOING DEEPER

HALF OF HIS BODY

A gentle joke. This is one of the playful verses. Pārvatī watched Kāma, the god of love who was helping her cause, burnt to ash by Śiva's third eye. Then Śiva made her half His own body as Ardhanārīśvara. She concludes He is lovestruck. The poet teases: You, who burnt love itself, a slave to love?

Two truths at once. Śiva is both: the yogī who is free of desire, and the Lord who is never apart from Śakti. The verse holds them together with a smile.

Ardhanārīśvara. The image of the Lord who is half woman expresses that Śiva and Śakti, consciousness and energy, are one.

💡 Ardhanārīśvara

"The Lord who is half woman": Śiva and Pārvatī in one body.

💡 Puṣpāyudha

"Flower-weaponed": Kāma, whose bow is sugarcane and arrows flowers.

Love that is free of craving is still love. The yogī and the lover can be the same heart.

ZAPPY: The hymn is teasing Pārvatī?

PROF. BOLT: Gently. She thinks He's ruled by love; He burnt love to ash. Both are true: He is free, and He is never without her.

The one who burnt desire keeps Pārvatī as half of Himself.

VERSE 24 · GOING DEEPER

INAUSPICIOUS, AND THE MOST AUSPICIOUS

The great paradox. Śiva's very name means "auspicious", yet He is pictured in the most inauspicious place of all, the burning ground. The verse grants the objection completely, "let all Your ways be inauspicious", and then turns it over.

Why the cremation ground? It is where every pretence ends: wealth, status, body. The Lord who dwells there is the one who remains when everything else is burnt. Ash is what is left when all that changes has gone.

Smartṝṇām. "For those who remember": the blessing is not in His surroundings but in our remembering Him.

💡 Bhasma

Sacred ash worn by Śaivas: a reminder that all bodies end as ash.

💡 Kāśī

The great cremation ghāṭ, Maṇikarṇikā, is in Śiva's own city; see our Kāśī guide.

What looks fearful may be the doorway to freedom. The place where everything ends is where the One who never ends is found.

ZAPPY: Skulls and ash, and He's the most auspicious?

PROF. BOLT: That's the hymn's point. Outer appearances don't decide holiness. Remembering Him does.

Inauspicious in looks, supremely auspicious to all who remember Him.

VERSE 25 · GOING DEEPER

THE YOGĪ'S INNER LAKE

From outer to inner. Verses 23–24 showed Śiva's strange outer appearance; verse 25 turns within. The yogī withdraws the senses, settles the mind in the heart, and controls the breath (prāṇāyāma).

Signs of bliss. Hair standing on end, tears of joy: the bodily signs of deep devotion and absorption, described in many traditions.

"That is You." Tat kila bhavān: the reality the yogī meets inside is not something other than Śiva. The God of the cremation ground and the light in the heart are one.

💡 Prāṇāyāma

Regulation of breath, a limb of yoga, used to steady the mind.

💡 Nirvāṇa Ṣaṭkam

Śaṅkara's "cidānanda-rūpaḥ śivo'ham" describes the same inner Śiva.

The deepest temple is inside you. Turn the mind inward, and the lake of nectar is already there.

ZAPPY: Why do the yogīs cry?

PROF. BOLT: From joy, says the hymn. What they find inside is so sweet it overflows. And that sweetness is Śiva.

The bliss the yogīs find within is Śiva Himself.

VERSE 26 · GOING DEEPER

YOU ARE EVERYTHING

Aṣṭamūrti. The eight forms of Śiva: the five elements (earth, water, fire, wind, space), the sun, the moon and the sacrificer or Self. Kālidāsa opens his play Śākuntalam with a prayer to this eightfold Śiva.

The poet's correction. Even this grand list is paricchinna, "limited". Saying "You are eight things" still leaves things out. So the poet turns it around: we know nothing that You are not.

Everything is Śiva. The verse moves from naming forms to the Upaniṣadic vision that all is the Lord.

💡 Aṣṭamūrti

The eight-formed Śiva; a famous temple tradition and Kālidāsa's opening prayer.

💡 Pañca-bhūta

The five elements; the South has a famous temple for each (a future series).

Look at the sun, the rain, the earth, your own self: you are looking at Him. There is nowhere He is not.

ZAPPY: Eight forms isn't enough?

PROF. BOLT: The poet says any list is too short. Name eight, name eight thousand: there's still nothing He isn't.

Not eight forms only: there is nothing He is not.

VERSE 27

THE SYLLABLE OṂ
trayīṃ tisro vṛttīstribhuvanamatho trīnapi surānakārādyairvarṇaistribhirabhidadhat tīrṇavikṛti |turīyaṃ te dhāmadhvanibhiravarundhānamaṇubhiḥsamasta-vyastaṃ tvāṃśaraṇada gṛṇātyomiti padam ‖27‖
Through its three letters, beginning with A, the word Oṃ names the three Vedas, the three states, the three worlds and the three gods; and with its subtle after-sound it points to Your fourth state, beyond all change. So, O giver of refuge, the word Oṃ praises You both as the whole and in Your parts.
Oṃ is made of A, U and M, which stand for every set of three: the Vedas, waking-dream-sleep, the three worlds, the three gods. The silence after it is the fourth state, beyond all. So Oṃ is the whole of Śiva.

VERSE 27 · GOING DEEPER

THE SYLLABLE OṂ

The Māṇḍūkya teaching. The Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad says the syllable Oṃ is all this. Its parts A, U and M are the waking, dreaming and deep-sleep states; the silence beyond them is turīya, "the fourth", pure consciousness.

Sets of three. The verse lines up every triad with A-U-M: the three Vedas, the three worlds (earth, sky, heaven), the three gods (Brahmā, Viṣṇu, Rudra).

Whole and parts. Samasta-vyasta: Oṃ praises Śiva in His parts (each letter) and as a whole (the full syllable and its silence).

💡 Turīya

"The fourth": the witness-consciousness underlying waking, dream and sleep.

💡 Praṇava

A name for Oṃ: the sacred syllable that begins and ends Vedic chanting.

Oṃ ends in silence. The fullest prayer is the quiet after the sound.

ZAPPY: Oṃ has four parts, not three?

PROF. BOLT: Three letters, and then the silence. That silence, the hymn says, is Śiva's own state.

A, U, M and the silence: Oṃ is the whole of Śiva.

VERSE 28 · GOING DEEPER

THE EIGHT NAMES

An old list. These eight names go back to the Vedic period: the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa tells how the newborn Rudra was given eight names, and the Atharva Veda and the Śrī Rudram use many of them.

Gentle and fierce. The names alternate: Bhava (existence) and Śarva (destroyer), Paśupati (protector) and Ugra (fierce), Mahādeva (great god) and Bhīma (terrible). Śiva is all of them at once.

Matching the eight forms. Tradition links the eight names with the eight forms of verse 26: Bhava with water, Śarva with earth, Rudra with fire and so on.

💡 Paśupati

"Lord of all creatures"; the name of the great temple in Kathmandu and of the Pāśupata school.

💡 Śrī Rudram

Many of these names recur in the Namakam; see our Rudram series.

Fierce and gentle, near and far: every name is a door. Use whichever opens your heart.

ZAPPY: Why eight names?

PROF. BOLT: An ancient Vedic set. Some are gentle, some fierce. Together they make one Lord.

Eight names from the Veda, one beloved Lord.

VERSE 29 · GOING DEEPER

NEAREST AND FARTHEST

Pairs of opposites. Every line bows to two extremes at once, using Sanskrit superlatives ("-iṣṭha", "-most"). Śiva is the nearest (within the heart) and the farthest (beyond the universe); smaller than an atom and larger than space; older than time and ever young.

An Upaniṣadic echo. The Kaṭha Upaniṣad says the Self is "smaller than the small, greater than the great"; the Īśa Upaniṣad that it is "far and near".

All, and beyond all. The last line holds the whole hymn: Śiva is everything (sarva), and also beyond everything (atisarva).

💡 Aṇoraṇīyān

"Smaller than the small, greater than the great": Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.2.20.

💡 Namaskāra

This verse is often chanted alone as a short prayer of salutation.

Look for Him far away and you'll find Him near. Make Him small and He grows beyond the sky.

ZAPPY: How can He be nearest and farthest?

PROF. BOLT: Nearest, because He's your own Self; farthest, because no mind can reach His end. The verse bows to both.

Nearest and farthest, all and beyond all: namaḥ.

VERSE 30 · GOING DEEPER

BEYOND THE THREE GUṆAS

Back to verse 4. In Part 1, verse 4 described one power working through three forms. Here the same idea becomes a chant: each line ends namo namaḥ.

The fourth line. After the three working forms comes the fourth: Śiva nistraiguṇya, beyond all three qualities, pure radiance. Like the silence after Oṃ in verse 27, the fourth is the truest.

A new metre. This verse changes rhythm (hariṇī), and verses 31–32 change again (mālinī): a sign that the hymn is moving toward its close.

💡 Mṛḍa

"The gracious": a Vedic name of Rudra, as in "mṛḍayā naḥ", be gracious to us.

💡 Nirguṇa

"Beyond qualities": the Absolute, beyond sattva, rajas and tamas.

We meet God in creation, in endings and in joy. Beyond them all, He is simply light.

ZAPPY: Four lines, four Śivas?

PROF. BOLT: Three faces at work, and one beyond all work. The last is the truest Śiva, the hymn says.

Creator, destroyer, gracious one, and the light beyond them all.

VERSE 31

AN OFFERING OF WORD-FLOWERS
kṛśa-pariṇati-cetaḥkleśavaśyaṃ kva cedaṃkva ca tava guṇa-sīmollaṅghinī śaśvadṛddhiḥ |iti cakitamamandīkṛtyamāṃ bhaktirādhādvarada caraṇayostevākya-puṣpopahāram ‖31‖
Where is this weak mind of mine, ruled by afflictions, and where is Your eternal glory, which crosses every limit of qualities? Though I was alarmed by this thought, devotion made me bold and laid at Your feet, O giver of boons, this offering of flowers made of words.
My mind is small and troubled; Your glory has no limit. That thought frightened me. But devotion gave me courage, and I have laid these word-flowers at Your feet.

VERSE 31 · GOING DEEPER

AN OFFERING OF WORD-FLOWERS

The circle closes. The hymn opened (verse 1) with the poet saying he cannot praise Śiva fully. Here, near the end, he says it again, but now as the giver of a gift: "word-flowers".

A flower thief. Remember the story: Puṣpadanta lost his powers for stealing flowers meant for Śiva. Now he offers Śiva flowers of his own: the verses of this hymn.

Devotion's courage. Bhaktir ādhāt: it was devotion, not his own skill, that put the offering at the Lord's feet.

💡 Kleśas

The afflictions of the mind in yoga: ignorance, ego, attachment, aversion, fear.

💡 Mālinī

The metre of verses 31–32: fifteen syllables, pausing after the eighth.

Our words are small, our minds are troubled. Offer them anyway. Love makes the offering worthy.

ZAPPY: He stole flowers, and now he offers word-flowers?

PROF. BOLT: That's the beauty of it. The thief's fault and the poet's gift meet in one image.

A small mind, a limitless Lord: devotion offers the flowers anyway.

VERSE 32

IF THE OCEAN WERE INK
asita-giri-samaṃ syātkajjalaṃ sindhu-pātresura-taruvara-śākhālekhanī patramurvī |likhati yadi gṛhītvāśāradā sarvakālaṃtadapi tava guṇānāmīśa pāraṃ na yāti ‖32‖
If a black mountain were the ink, the ocean the inkpot, a branch of the best of heavenly trees the pen, and the earth the paper; and if Śāradā, the goddess of learning, took these and wrote for all time: even then, O Lord, she would not reach the end of Your qualities.
Make a mountain into ink, the ocean into an inkpot, a branch of the wish-tree into a pen, the earth into paper, and let Sarasvatī write forever: she still could not finish describing Śiva's glories.

VERSE 32 · GOING DEEPER

IF THE OCEAN WERE INK

The most famous verse. This is the verse people quote when they want to say something is beyond description, and it is often recited on its own. Its images are vast and simple: the biggest ink, the largest pot, the best pen, the widest page, the greatest writer, and endless time.

Back to the beginning. Verse 1 said even Brahmā's words fall short. Verse 32 says even Sarasvatī, writing forever, could not finish. The hymn ends its praise as it began: in wonder.

Wish-tree pen. The sura-taru, "tree of the gods", is the Pārijāta or Kalpavṛkṣa, the wish-fulfilling tree of heaven.

💡 Śāradā

A name of Sarasvatī, goddess of speech and learning; Kashmir's Śāradā Pīṭha is named after her.

💡 Kalpavṛkṣa

The wish-fulfilling tree that rose from the churning of the ocean.

Some things can only be honoured by admitting they can't be told. Wonder is the last word of praise.

ZAPPY: Even Sarasvatī couldn't finish?

PROF. BOLT: Not if she wrote forever. That's how the poet ends his praise: not with a full stop, but with wonder.

All the ink in the world, written forever: still not the end of His glory.

PART 3 ON ONE PAGE

Verses 23–32 in five lines
Verses 23–24. The yogī who burnt Kāma keeps Pārvatī as half Himself; the Lord of the cremation ground is the most auspicious of all.
Verses 25–26. The bliss the yogīs find within is Śiva; sun, moon, elements and Self are His forms, and nothing exists that He is not.
Verses 27–28. Oṃ: A, U, M and the silent fourth; the eight Vedic names, from Bhava to Īśāna.
Verses 29–30. Salutation to the nearest and farthest, smallest and greatest; to Bhava, Hara, Mṛḍa, and Śiva beyond the three guṇas.
Verses 31–32. A small mind offers word-flowers; if the mountains were ink and the earth paper, Sarasvatī could not finish His praise.

ZAPPY: Is that the end of the hymn?

PROF. BOLT: The praise ends at verse 32. Part 4 has the closing verses: the fruit of chanting, Puṣpadanta named, and the blessing.
