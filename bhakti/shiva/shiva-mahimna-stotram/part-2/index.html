FOREWORD

In Part 1, Puṣpadanta admitted that no words can reach Śiva, answered the doubters, and saw all paths flowing to one ocean. Now he turns from argument to story.

Verses 12–22 are the great deeds: Rāvaṇa pressed down by one toe, Bāṇa raised by bowing, the poison held in the throat, Kāma burnt to ash, the dance that shakes the worlds, the Gaṅgā as a drop, the chariot made of the universe, Viṣṇu's eye, Dakṣa's broken sacrifice, and a hunter who still chases a deer across the night sky.

Each story ends with a line of wisdom: on pride, humility, compassion, self-control and faith. This is the part of the hymn children remember first.

THE STORY · THE THREE FLYING CITIES

Why Śiva is called Tripurāntaka, "ender of the three cities"
The story in 5 frames
Three cities. Three demon brothers, sons of Tāraka, win a boon: three flying cities, of gold in heaven, silver in the sky and iron on earth, built by the architect Maya.
The catch. The cities can be destroyed only by a single arrow, at the one moment in a thousand years when all three line up.
Terror. From their moving fortresses the brothers trouble gods and people. The gods turn to Śiva.
The chariot. The whole universe is made into His war-chariot: the earth its body, sun and moon its wheels, Brahmā the charioteer, Mount Meru the bow, Viṣṇu the arrow.
One arrow. When the cities align, Śiva destroys all three at once; many tellings say He did it with a smile alone.

The cities

Gold, silver, iron: heaven, sky, earth.

The archer

Tripurāntaka, Śiva with Meru as His bow.

Inner reading

Three cities = three bodies or three impurities.

ZAPPY: Why does the hymn keep calling Him 'destroyer of the cities'?

PROF. BOLT: Because Tripura is His great victory, and verse 18 tells it. Puramathana and Tripurahara both mean the same: the one who burnt the three cities.

ABOUT THE MAHIMNA

A hymn of thought and devotion

✍️ The author

Ascribed to Puṣpadanta; a later verse names him as the composer. Its real date is not known; it is an old and much-copied hymn.

🎵 The sound

Written in śikhariṇī, seventeen syllables a line with a pause after the sixth, and chanted to a much-loved traditional tune.

The shape. Verses 1–3 apologise for praising the unpraisable; 4–9 answer doubters and debaters; 10–24 tell Śiva's great deeds; 25–31 turn to Oṃ, the eight names and salutations; 32 is the famous "if the ocean were ink" verse; the rest are closing verses.

Read two ways. The 16th-century Advaita scholar Madhusūdana Sarasvatī wrote a commentary reading every verse as praise of both Śiva and Viṣṇu.

Beloved by saints. Śrī Rāmakṛṣṇa is said to have gone into samādhi while reciting only a few of its verses.

VERSE 12

ONE TOE ON KAILĀSA
amuṣya tvatsevā-samadhigatasāraṃ bhujavanaṃbalāt kailāse'pitvadadhivasatau vikramayataḥ |alabhyāpātāle'pyalasacalitāṅguṣṭhaśirasipratiṣṭhā tvayyāsīddhruvamupacito muhyati khalaḥ ‖12‖
That forest of arms, which had won its strength by serving You, he forced into a show of might even on Kailāsa, Your own dwelling. Then, when You idly moved the tip of Your big toe, he could not find a footing even in the netherworld. Surely the wicked, once they swell with power, lose their senses.
Rāvaṇa's arms got their strength from worshipping Śiva, and he used them to lift Śiva's own mountain. One lazy press of Śiva's toe, and he sank to the underworld. Power makes the proud foolish.

VERSE 12 · GOING DEEPER

ONE TOE ON KAILĀSA

The same arms. Verse 11 praised Rāvaṇa's devotion; verse 12 shows what he did with its reward. The strength Śiva gave him, he turned against Śiva's own home, trying to lift Kailāsa.

One toe. The poet's words are precise: alasa-calita, "idly moved". Śiva doesn't fight. He barely moves a toe, and Rāvaṇa finds no ground to stand on, even in the netherworld.

The moral. Upacito muhyati khalaḥ: the wicked, when they grow strong, lose their judgement. Our Śiva Tāṇḍava article tells what happened next: trapped under the mountain, Rāvaṇa sang.

💡 Pātāla

The lowest of the netherworlds in Purāṇic cosmology.

💡 Same story

The Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotram is said to be the song Rāvaṇa sang under this mountain.

Strength that came from grace, used against grace, collapses. Remember where your power came from.

ZAPPY: So Rāvaṇa used Śiva's own gift against Him?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. And Śiva didn't even get up. The verse is about how small pride is next to the Lord.

Strength from devotion, misused in pride: one toe was enough.

VERSE 13

BĀṆA, WHO BOWED
yadṛddhiṃ sutrāmṇovarada paramoccairapi satīṃadhaścakre bāṇaḥparijanavidheyatribhuvanaḥ |na taccitraṃ tasminvarivasitari tvaccaraṇayoḥna kasyāpyunnatyaibhavati śirasastvayyavanatiḥ ‖13‖
That Bāṇa, with the three worlds as his servants, put beneath him the wealth of Indra, high as it was: O giver of boons, that is no wonder in one who served Your feet. Whose rise does not come from bowing the head to You?
The demon king Bāṇa outshone even Indra's heaven. No surprise: he worshipped Śiva's feet. Bowing to Śiva lifts anyone up.

VERSE 13 · GOING DEEPER

BĀṆA, WHO BOWED

Bāṇāsura. Son of Bali, a thousand-armed devotee of Śiva who is said to have played the drum during Śiva's dance. Pleased, Śiva became the guardian of his city, Śoṇitapura.

The paradox. The last line is the heart: na kasyāpy unnatyai bhavati śirasas tvayy avanatiḥ, "whose rise does not come from bowing the head to You?" Bowing down is how one rises.

Contrast with Rāvaṇa. Verse 12 showed devotion turned to pride; verse 13 shows devotion that keeps bowing, and so keeps rising.

💡 Bāṇāsura

The thousand-armed king whose daughter Uṣā married Kṛṣṇa's grandson Aniruddha.

💡 Bali's son

His father Bali is the generous king humbled by Vāmana.

Bowing is not a loss of height. The head that bows to the highest is lifted highest.

ZAPPY: Bowing makes you rise? That sounds backwards.

PROF. BOLT: It's the poet's favourite kind of paradox. Pride sank Rāvaṇa; humility raised Bāṇa.

Bow the head to Śiva, and you rise.

When the churning of the ocean threw up a world-ending poison, Śiva drank it out of compassion. The blue mark it left on His throat is His most beautiful ornament.

VERSE 14 · GOING DEEPER

THE BLUE THROAT

The churning of the ocean. Gods and demons churned the ocean of milk for the nectar of immortality. First came Hālāhala, a poison that could end the world. Everyone ran to Śiva. He drank it, and Pārvatī held His throat so it would not go down. It stayed there, turning His throat blue: Nīlakaṇṭha.

The blemish that adorns. A mark on the throat would be a flaw on anyone else. On Śiva it is his glory, because it is the scar of compassion.

Before its time. Akāṇḍa: the world has a proper time to end; Śiva will not let it end by accident.

💡 Hālāhala

The poison from the churned ocean, before the nectar emerged.

💡 Pradoṣa

Tradition links Śiva's drinking of the poison to the pradoṣa twilight worship.

The marks we carry from protecting others are not scars to hide. They are the most beautiful thing about us.

ZAPPY: Why is His throat blue in pictures?

PROF. BOLT: From the poison. He held it in His throat to save the world. The hymn calls that mark beautiful.

A scar of compassion is an ornament, not a flaw.

VERSE 15 · GOING DEEPER

KĀMA BURNT TO ASH

The story. To make Śiva marry Pārvatī, so a son could be born to defeat the demon Tāraka, the gods sent Kāma, god of desire, to disturb Śiva's meditation. Kāma shot his flower arrow. Śiva opened His third eye, and Kāma was burnt to ash.

A pun. Kāma's name Smara means "remembering". The poet says he became smartavyātmā, "one whose self is only to be remembered": bodiless, alive only in the mind. Kāma is still called Anaṅga, "the bodiless".

The lesson. Desire wins everywhere, except against the one who is master of himself.

💡 Anaṅga

"The bodiless": Kāma's name after being burnt; Rati's prayers later won him life without a body.

💡 Kumārasambhava

Kālidāsa's great poem tells this story at length.

Desire's arrows hit almost everyone. The calm mind is the one target they cannot hit.

ZAPPY: Desire never misses?

PROF. BOLT: Never, says the hymn, among gods, demons or people. Except once: with Śiva. That's why He's the yogī's Lord.

Desire conquers all, except the master of himself.

VERSE 16

THE DANCE THAT SHAKES THE WORLDS
mahī pādāghātādvrajati sahasā saṃśayapadaṃpadaṃ viṣṇorbhrāmyadbhuja-parigha-rugṇa-graha-gaṇam |muhurdyaurdausthyaṃyātyanibhṛta-jaṭā-tāḍita-taṭājagadrakṣāyai tvaṃnaṭasi nanu vāmaiva vibhutā ‖16‖

VERSE 16 · GOING DEEPER

THE DANCE THAT SHAKES THE WORLDS

The cosmic dance. Śiva dances the tāṇḍava at the end of time and also, says the verse, for the world's protection. The images are huge: the earth sinking under His feet, planets knocked by His arms, the sky lashed by His hair.

Vāmā. Vāmā means "contrary", "crooked", even "beautiful": the poet plays on all of them. God's power works in ways that look like the opposite of what they are.

Viṣṇu's step. The sky is called "Viṣṇu's step" (padaṃ viṣṇoḥ), from the story of Vāmana measuring the worlds in three steps.

💡 Naṭarāja

Śiva as Lord of Dance; see our Śiva Tāṇḍava article.

💡 Vāmana

Viṣṇu's dwarf form, whose stride became the sky.

Sometimes protection feels like an earthquake. What shakes us may be what saves us.

ZAPPY: He dances to protect the world, and it shakes?

PROF. BOLT: That's the poet's wonder: divine care doesn't always feel gentle. The word vāmā means both "contrary" and "lovely".

Even His protecting dance shakes the worlds.

VERSE 17 · GOING DEEPER

THE GAṄGĀ AS A DROP

The sky river. The heavenly Gaṅgā is pictured as the Milky Way, its foam the stars. When she fell to earth, Śiva caught her in His hair, where she looked like a single drop.

Scale. The verse works by comparison: the river that rings the world is a droplet on His head. So how big is the head? The poet lets us do the arithmetic.

Bhagīratha. King Bhagīratha brought the Gaṅgā down to purify his ancestors' ashes; Śiva softened her fall. (Our Tryambakeśvara guide tells how Gautama brought her south as the Godāvarī.)

💡 Ākāśa-gaṅgā

The "sky Gaṅgā": the Milky Way in Indian tradition.

💡 Gaṅgādhara

"Bearer of the Gaṅgā": one of Śiva's names.

What looks huge to us is a drop to Him. What looks like a drop to Him can still ring our whole world.

ZAPPY: The whole Gaṅgā is a drop?

PROF. BOLT: On His head, yes. That's the poet's way of making us feel His size.

The river that rings the earth is a drop in His hair.

VERSE 18

To destroy the three demon cities, the gods built Śiva a chariot: earth, sun and moon, Mount Meru, Viṣṇu as the arrow. But Śiva didn't need any of it; He plays with these things, He doesn't depend on them.

VERSE 18 · GOING DEEPER

A CHARIOT MADE OF THE WORLD

Tripura. Three demon brothers built three flying cities, of gold, silver and iron, that could be destroyed only by a single arrow when they lined up once in a thousand years. The gods made Śiva a chariot of the whole universe. In the end, many tellings say, Śiva burnt them with a smile.

Straw. The poet calls the cities tṛṇa, "straw": the great war machine of the gods was hardly needed.

Play. Vidheyaiḥ krīḍantyaḥ: the Lord plays with the things that serve Him. He uses the world's instruments, but is never bound by them.

💡 Tripurāntaka

"Ender of the three cities": Śiva as the archer; a famous image in temple sculpture.

💡 Inner reading

Commentators read the three cities as three bodies or three impurities, burnt by knowledge.

God uses means, but is not limited by them. The tools serve the purpose; they are not the power.

ZAPPY: Why build a chariot He didn't need?

PROF. BOLT: For the gods' sake, and for the story. The verse says He was playing: the real power was His.

The whole universe as His chariot, and He needed none of it.

VERSE 19 · GOING DEEPER

VIṢṆU'S EYE AND THE DISCUS

The story. Viṣṇu vowed to worship Śiva with a thousand lotuses. Śiva, testing him, hid one. Rather than break his vow, Viṣṇu, whose eyes are called lotuses, plucked out one eye and offered it. Śiva restored the eye and gave him the Sudarśana cakra.

A beautiful link. The verse says the discus is Viṣṇu's devotion itself, "transformed". The weapon that protects the world is made of love.

Hari and Hara. Here Viṣṇu is Śiva's greatest devotee, as elsewhere Śiva is Viṣṇu's. Madhusūdana Sarasvatī's commentary reads the whole hymn for both.

💡 Sudarśana

"Beautiful to see": Viṣṇu's discus, protector of the worlds.

💡 Kamalākṣa

"Lotus-eyed": a name of Viṣṇu, which makes the offering natural.

What you give completely comes back as the strength to protect others.

ZAPPY: Viṣṇu worshipped Śiva?

PROF. BOLT: In this story, wholeheartedly, even giving his eye. Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava stories are full of each god honouring the other.

Viṣṇu's devotion became the discus that guards the world.

VERSE 20

THE WITNESS OF EVERY RITUAL
kratau supte jāgrattvamasi phalayoge kratumatāṃkva karma pradhvastaṃphalati puruṣārādhanamṛte |atastvāṃ samprekṣyakratuṣu phaladāna-pratibhuvaṃśrutau śraddhāṃ badhvādṛḍhaparikaraḥ karmasu janaḥ ‖20‖
When the sacrifice is asleep, over and gone, You stay awake to join the sacrificers to its fruit. For how can a rite, once it has perished, bear fruit without the worship of the Lord? A ritual ends and is gone; how can it bear fruit later? Because Śiva stays awake and delivers the fruit. Knowing that, people trust the Veda and keep up their rites.

VERSE 20 · GOING DEEPER

THE WITNESS OF EVERY RITUAL

An old puzzle. The Mīmāṃsā school said a ritual creates an unseen power, apūrva, that later brings its result, with no need for God. Puṣpadanta disagrees: a deed that is finished cannot by itself produce anything. Someone must remember it and give the fruit. That is the Lord.

The guarantor. Pratibhū: a surety, like someone who guarantees a loan. Śiva is the guarantee that every sincere act will bear fruit.

Why it matters. If He watches over results, then no honest effort is wasted, even if the reward comes long after.

💡 Apūrva

Mīmāṃsā's "unseen potency" left by a ritual, which later yields its result.

💡 Karma-phala

The fruit of action, which the Gītā asks us to offer to the Lord.

Nothing done sincerely is lost. Someone is awake when your work is finished.

ZAPPY: Why does a ritual need God to work?

PROF. BOLT: Because when it's over, it's gone. Puṣpadanta says someone has to carry it forward. That's Śiva, awake while the ritual sleeps.

The ritual ends; He stays awake and gives the fruit.

VERSE 21

DAKṢA'S BROKEN SACRIFICE
kriyādakṣo dakṣaḥkratupatiradhīśastanubhṛtāṃṛṣīṇāmārtvijyaṃśaraṇada sadasyāḥ sura-gaṇāḥ |kratubhraṃśastvattaḥkratuphala-vidhāna-vyasaninaḥdhruvaṃ kartuṃ śraddhāvidhuramabhicārāya hi makhāḥ ‖21‖
Dakṣa, skilled in rites, lord of embodied beings, was the sacrificer; the sages were the priests; the hosts of gods sat in the assembly; yet, O giver of refuge, the sacrifice was ruined by You, who delight in granting the fruits of sacrifice. Surely, sacrifices done without faith turn into harm for the one who performs them.
Dakṣa's great sacrifice had everything: sages as priests, gods as guests. But without faith and without honouring Śiva, it was destroyed. Ritual without faith harms the doer.

VERSE 21 · GOING DEEPER

DAKṢA'S BROKEN SACRIFICE

Dakṣa's yajña. Dakṣa, father of Satī, held a great sacrifice and pointedly left out his son-in-law Śiva. Satī went anyway, was insulted, and gave up her life in the sacrificial fire. Śiva's fury, as Vīrabhadra, destroyed the sacrifice.

Skilled but faithless. The verse plays on the name: kriyā-dakṣo dakṣaḥ, "Dakṣa, dakṣa (skilled) in rites". Skill was not the problem; pride and lack of faith were.

Śakti Pīṭhas. In many tellings, Śiva carried Satī's body across the world, and where its parts fell, the Śakti Pīṭhas arose.

💡 Satī

Dakṣa's daughter and Śiva's first wife, later reborn as Pārvatī.

💡 Vīrabhadra

The fierce form born of Śiva's anger to destroy the sacrifice.

Perfect technique without humility is empty. What is offered without love does not reach.

ZAPPY: Dakṣa did everything right, ritually.

PROF. BOLT: Every detail, says the hymn: sages, gods, rules. Only the heart was missing. And that turned his sacrifice against him.

All the right rituals, no faith: the sacrifice fell.

VERSE 22

THE HUNTER IN THE NIGHT SKY

When Brahmā, as a stag, chased his own daughter with wrong desire, Śiva shot him with an arrow. Brahmā fled to the sky, and there, as stars, the hunter still chases the deer.

VERSE 22 · GOING DEEPER

THE HUNTER IN THE NIGHT SKY

An old Vedic story. The Brāhmaṇas tell that Prajāpati desired his own daughter Uṣas, the dawn, and Rudra punished him with an arrow. The hymn retells it: Śiva upholds what is right even against the creator.

Written in the stars. Tradition reads the story in the sky: the deer's head is the star group Mṛgaśiras (part of Orion); the hunter, Mṛgavyādha, is the bright star we call Sirius; the arrow lies between. "Still today", says the poet: look up on a winter night.

No one is above dharma. Not even the creator escapes Rudra's justice.

💡 Mṛgaśiras

"The deer's head": a nakṣatra (lunar mansion) in the constellation Orion.

💡 Mṛgavyādha

"The deer-hunter": the traditional Indian name for Sirius.

Dharma applies to everyone, even the most powerful. And its memory lasts as long as the stars.

ZAPPY: A story about stars?

PROF. BOLT: A story put in the stars. Ancient watchers saw a deer, an arrow and a hunter in the winter sky, and remembered this tale.

Even the creator is not above Rudra's justice.
