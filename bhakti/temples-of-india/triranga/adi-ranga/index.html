ĀDI RAṄGA

ŚRĪ RAṄGANĀTHASVĀMI · ŚRĪRAṄGAPAṬṬAṆA · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The first Raṅga on the Kāverī

FOREWORD

The Kāverī has barely left the hills when she parts around an island and holds, in its heart, a giant at rest: Raṅganātha, Viṣṇu reclining on the seven-hooded serpent. This is Ādi Raṅga, the first of the three Raṅgas.

The island took its name from him: Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa, the town of Śrī Raṅga. Kings of four dynasties built here; the Mysore Dasara began here; a famous siege ended here. Through all of it, the Lord kept resting.

This guide tells the temple’s legends, history, festivals, and the island around it.

ZAPPY: If he's resting, who's running the world?

PROF. BOLT: He is, Zappy. That's the point of yoganidrā: the Lord at rest still holds everything. Calm is not the same as idle.

ZAPPY: A whole town named after a temple?

PROF. BOLT: Śrī-Raṅga-paṭṭaṇa: 'the town of Śrī Raṅga'. The Lord came first; the kings, the forts and the capitals came later.

WHERE IT IS

An island in the Kāverī

Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa is a river island: the Kāverī divides and flows around the whole town. The main stream runs on its eastern side; the branch on the west flows westward, and is called Paścimavāhinī, “the west-flowing”.

It is the first of the three islands of the Triraṅga, upstream of Śivanasamudra and Śrīraṅgam, in the old Mysore country of southern Karnataka.

THE LORD OF ĀDI RAṄGA

A giant at rest

🐍 On seven hoods

The Lord lies on the coils of Ādiśeṣa, whose seven hoods spread over his head like a canopy. The image is described as about 4.5 m long.

👑 The pose

He rests his head on his right hand, with his left arm along his body; a tall crown, rich ornaments, and eyes half open in yogic sleep.

Who is at his feet? Sources differ: Wikipedia names Lakṣmī at his feet, with Śrīdevī, Bhūdevī and Brahmā beside him; temple tradition identifies the figure as the river goddess Kāverī, with the sage Gautama. Both readings fit the legends on the next pages.

THE LEGENDS · I

Gautama Kṣetra
The sage on the bank. Temple tradition says the sage Gautama lived and did penance here on the Kāverī, longing to see Viṣṇu in his reclining form.
The vision. Pleased, the Lord appeared as Raṅganātha, resting on Ādiśeṣa, in the yogic sleep the sage had prayed to see.
A name for the place. So the island is also called Gautama Kṣetra, the sacred field of Gautama, and the sage is shown at the Lord’s feet.
Who built it? One telling says that at Gautama’s request Brahmā himself built the first temple. History, on the next pages, has its own answer.

ZAPPY: Gautama? The one from the Rāmāyaṇa?

PROF. BOLT: The same famous name: Gautama, husband of Ahalyā. Many Kāverī shrines are linked to him. Whether it's one sage or a family of sages, tradition doesn't fuss.

THE LEGENDS · II

The river’s three boons
The river’s penance. Another temple tradition tells of the river goddess Kāverī herself doing penance here to win the Lord’s grace.
Three boons. Pleased, Raṅganātha granted her three boons, one telling says: that her waters would be counted holier even than the Gaṅgā; that this place would become a great pilgrimage; and that he would stay here.
At his feet. So devotees see the goddess at the Lord’s feet as Kāverī, the river who holds him on her island.
One river, three Raṅgas. The same longing is told of all three islands: the Kāverī wraps her arms around the Lord three times on her way to the sea.

ZAPPY: Holier than the Gaṅgā?

PROF. BOLT: That's the river's boon, as Kāverī devotees tell it! Every great river has its own pride; the Gaṅgā's devotees have their own stories too.

HISTORY · I

From the Gaṅgas to Vijayanagara
894 CE An inscription records the temple built by Tirumalaiah, a local chief under the Western Gaṅga kings, whose capital was at Talakāḍu. An earlier shrine may have stood here. (Some sources print the date as 984.)
1108–52 The Hoysaḷa king Viṣṇuvardhana grants the village to Rāmānuja as an agrahāra, a seat of learning.
1210 An inscription of Vīra Ballāḷa II records additions and renovations; the temple’s lathe-turned pillars are Hoysaḷa work.
1300s–1500s Under Vijayanagara, Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa becomes the seat of a great viceroyalty, overseeing vassals such as Mysore and Talakāḍu. The entrance gopuram is in the Vijayanagara style.

Three styles in one temple. Gaṅga foundations, Hoysaḷa pillars and a Vijayanagara gateway: the temple grew with each dynasty that ruled the island, and was never replaced by any of them.

ZAPPY: Rāmānuja got a whole village?

PROF. BOLT: As a seat of learning, yes. The Hoysaḷa king who gave it, Viṣṇuvardhana, is remembered in tradition as Rāmānuja's great royal follower.

HISTORY · II

A royal capital, a famous siege
1610 Rāja Woḍeyar I of Mysore takes the island from the Vijayanagara viceroy, makes it his capital, and celebrates Navarātri there that year: the beginning of the royal Mysore Dasara.
1610–1799 Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa is the capital of the Mysore kingdom; the Woḍeyars add to the temple.
1761–1799 Real power passes to Hyder Ali and his son Tipu Sultan, who rule from the island fortress. Karnataka Tourism notes that both held the temple in regard.
4 May 1799 British forces storm the fort; Tipu Sultan is killed. The Woḍeyars are restored, and the capital returns to Mysuru.
Today The temple is protected by the Archaeological Survey of India as a monument of national importance.

Through it all. Capitals moved and armies came and went across this island. The temple at its heart kept its worship through every change of ruler.

ZAPPY: Dasara started here, not in Mysuru?

PROF. BOLT: Yes! The Woḍeyar Dasara began on this island in 1610 and moved to Mysuru after 1799. The Lord of Ādi Raṅga watched the first one.

THE TEMPLE TODAY

From gateway to sanctum

🏯 The approach

A Vijayanagara-style gopuram leads through two concentric enclosures (prākāras) and a line of halls: the mukhamaṇḍapa, the navaraṅga and the sukhanāsi, to the sanctum.

🪨 Hoysaḷa touch

In the inner hall stand lathe-turned pillars, a Hoysaḷa signature; the hall roof carries small stucco shrines with images of Viṣṇu.

Shrines within. Around the main sanctum are shrines to Narasiṃha, Gopālakṛṣṇa, Śrīnivāsa, Hanumān, Garuḍa and the Āḻvārs, the Tamil poet-saints of Viṣṇu.

FESTIVALS

The Lord’s calendar

☀️ Saṅkrānti Brahmotsava

The temple’s great festival falls in the month of Puṣya, around Makara Saṅkrānti. On its sixth day, as the sun turns north (Uttarāyaṇa), the Vaikuṇṭha door is opened for devotees.

🪔 Lakṣa Dīpotsava

With the Brahmotsava comes the festival of a hundred thousand lamps, when the temple glows with oil lamps.

🚪 Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī

As at every Raṅganātha temple, the Ekādaśī of Dhanurmāsa is a high day, and the season of the Triraṅga vow.

🦅 Garuḍa Utsava

Temple tradition keeps a Garuḍa festival in memory of the Lord’s appearance to Gautama.

A different door day. At Śrīraṅgam the gate of Vaikuṇṭha opens on Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī; here, sources describe it opening during the Saṅkrānti Brahmotsava. Dates follow the temple’s own calendar each year.

ZAPPY: Why a door to heaven in a temple?

PROF. BOLT: It's a picture of the soul's last journey: through the door, to the Lord. Walking through it on the holy day is a way of saying 'take me there'.

RĀMĀNUJA AND ĀDI RAṄGA

One teacher, two Raṅgas

The teacher. Rāmānuja (traditionally 1017–1137) is the great teacher of Viśiṣṭādvaita and of the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition, the worship of Viṣṇu with Lakṣmī.

In Karnataka. Tradition holds that Rāmānuja spent many years in the Hoysaḷa country, centred on Melukote, where a great Śrīvaiṣṇava centre grew around him.

The grant. Inscriptions show King Viṣṇuvardhana granting the village of Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa to Rāmānuja as a seat of learning.

Two Raṅgas. Rāmānuja’s life ended at Śrīraṅgam, the Antya Raṅga, where his preserved body is still enshrined. So the great teacher links the first and the last of the three Raṅgas.

ZAPPY: So Ādi Raṅga and Antya Raṅga share a teacher?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. The Triraṅga isn't only a river path; it's also a teacher's path, from the Hoysaḷa country to Śrīraṅgam.

AROUND THE ISLAND

A sacred island, and a historic one
PaścimavāhinīThe west-flowing branch of the Kāverī, where families traditionally perform rites for their ancestors
SaṅgamaThe point where the two branches of the Kāverī meet again at the island’s eastern end
NimiṣāmbāA temple of the Goddess (Pārvatī) on the bank of the Lokapāvanī, who devotees believe clears troubles “in a nimiṣa”, an instant
GaṅgādhareśvaraA Śiva temple among the town’s old shrines
Lakṣmī-NarasiṃhaA temple of Narasiṃha with Lakṣmī, among the town’s old shrines
KarighaṭṭaA Śrīnivāsa temple on a black hill nearby, reached by about 450 stone steps
The fort eraThe fort walls, Tipu Sultan’s summer palace (Dariyā Daulat) and the Gumbaz, the tomb of Hyder Ali and Tipu, recall the island’s years as a capital

ZAPPY: A Śiva temple, a Devī temple and Viṣṇu all on one island?

PROF. BOLT: That's typical of the old Mysore country: Śaiva, Śākta and Vaiṣṇava shrines side by side, and the river shared by all.

THE FIRST OF THREE

Ādi Raṅga in the Triraṅga

The beginning. In the Triraṅga, the vow to see all three Raṅganāthas in one day, Ādi Raṅga comes first: the first island the Kāverī makes on her way east.

Next. Downstream lies Madhya Raṅga at Śivanasamudra, the youthful Lord between the waterfalls; and at the end, Antya Raṅga at Śrīraṅgam, the greatest of the three.
