ANTYA RAṄGA

ŚRĪ RAṄGANĀTHASVĀMI · ŚRĪRAṄGAM · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The last Raṅga, and the first of the 108

FOREWORD

At the end of the Triraṅga, the Kāverī parts one last time, and between her and the Koḷḷiḍam lies Śrīraṅgam: seven walls, twenty-one towers, a whole town, and at the centre, facing south, the reclining Lord.

This is the temple the twelve Āḻvārs all sang, where Āṇḍāḷ came to her Lord, where Rāmānuja taught, from which the Lord’s image went into exile, and to which it came home. Devotees simply call it Kōyil, “the temple”.

This guide tells its legends, its saints and its long history.

Hari Oṃ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: They just call it 'the temple'?

PROF. BOLT: For Śrīvaiṣṇavas, 'Kōyil' with no other name means Śrīraṅgam. When you're first of 108, you don't need a surname.

ZAPPY: First of 108 AND last of three?

PROF. BOLT: Both! First among the Divya Deśams, last on the river. Antya means 'final', and for many devotees this is where the journey ends.

WHERE IT IS

An island between two rivers

Near the end of her journey, the Kāverī divides once more. The northern branch, the Koḷḷiḍam, and the Kāverī herself enclose the long island of Śrīraṅgam, beside the city of Tiruchirappalli.

The great temple fills the heart of the island; the town lives inside its outer walls. Just to the east stands Tiruvānaikkā, the Śiva temple of the water element; across the river rises the Rockfort.

THE LORD OF ŚRĪRAṄGAM

Periya Perumāḷ and Namperumāḷ

🛏 Periya Perumāḷ

“The Great Lord”: the reclining mūlavar, facing south. Unusually, the image is not of carved stone but of sudhai, a mix of lime, mortar and śālagrāma stones, coated with fragrant oil (thailam), so by tradition it is not bathed with water.

✨ Namperumāḷ

“Our Lord”: the festival image of five metals plated in gold, also called Aḻagiya Maṇavāḷan, “the beautiful bridegroom”. He goes out in procession, and he is the one who went into exile.

The Goddess. Raṅganāyakī has her own shrine in the second enclosure, with two fixed images and one festival image. By tradition she does not leave her shrine.

THE LEGENDS · I

The vimāna that would not move
From Brahmā. The sacred vimāna, the shrine with the reclining Lord inside, arose from Brahmā’s austerities and stayed for ages in his world, Satyaloka.
To Ayodhyā. King Ikṣvāku brought it to Ayodhyā, where it was worshipped by his descendants, the kings of the Sun dynasty, down to Rāma.
A gift to Vibhīṣaṇa. After the war, Rāma gave it to Vibhīṣaṇa, the righteous brother of Rāvaṇa, to take to Laṅkā.
It would not move. On the way, it was set down on this island and would not rise again. Vibhīṣaṇa gave it to the local king, Dharmavarma, on one condition: that the Lord should face south, towards Laṅkā.
So he faces south. To this day Raṅganātha lies facing south, watching over Vibhīṣaṇa’s island.

THE LEGENDS · II

Gaṇeśa, the river and the king
A boy on the bank. A popular local telling says that when Vibhīṣaṇa stopped to bathe in the Kāverī, he handed the image to a cowherd boy, asking him not to set it down. The boy set it down at once.
The chase. Angry, Vibhīṣaṇa chased the boy up the great rock across the river and struck him on the head. The boy was Gaṇeśa, now worshipped at the summit as Ucci Piḷḷaiyār.
The river’s wish. Another telling says the river goddess Kāverī asked Raṅganātha to stay with her on the island.
A flood and a king. Tradition says a Kāverī flood later buried the shrine, and the early Cōḻa king Kiḷḷivaḷavan rebuilt the temple.

ZAPPY: Gaṇeśa tricked Vibhīṣaṇa?

PROF. BOLT: Lovingly! The gods wanted Raṅganātha to stay in the Kāverī country. And Raṅganātha still faces Laṅkā, so Vibhīṣaṇa is not forgotten.

THE ĀḺVĀRS

Twelve poet-saints, one Lord
ĀḻvārHymns on ŚrīraṅgamConnection

Toṇḍaraḍippoḍi55Sang only of Raṅganātha; his dawn hymn wakes the Lord
Kulaśekhara31A king of Kerala who longed to serve at Śrīraṅgam
Āṇḍāḷ10Poet of the Tiruppāvai; tradition says she merged into Raṅganātha here
Tiruppāṇ10Sang only of Raṅganātha, describing the Lord from his feet upward

The Tamil Veda. The twelve Āḻvārs, Tamil poet-saints of the 6th to 9th centuries, sang the hymns later gathered as the Nālāyira Divya Prabandham, the 4,000 sacred verses. Temples they sang of are the Divya Deśams.

Āṇḍāḷ’s Lord. The girl who wore the Lord’s garland sang her Tiruppāvai to this Raṅganātha, and tradition says she was united with him at Śrīraṅgam.

ZAPPY: Is that why Tiruppāvai is sung here in Mārgaḻi?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. Āṇḍāḷ's songs come home to her Lord every Dhanurmāsa.

RĀMĀNUJA

The teacher of Śrīraṅgam

The great teacher. Rāmānuja (traditionally 1017–1137), the teacher of Viśiṣṭādvaita, made Śrīraṅgam the centre of the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition.

His works. The Śrī Bhāṣya, his commentary on the Brahma Sūtras, and the Gadya Traya, three prose hymns of surrender, are linked with his years here.

His order. Tradition credits him with organising the temple’s worship and service, which shaped how it is run to this day.

His presence. Rāmānuja’s preserved body is enshrined within the temple, in a shrine of its own, revered as his tirumeni.

Two Raṅgas. The same teacher received Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa from the Hoysaḷa king: see Ādi Raṅga. The first and last Raṅgas share him.

ZAPPY: His body is still here, after 900 years?

PROF. BOLT: That's the tradition and the temple's practice: he is honoured here, seated, as if still present among his people.

HISTORY · I

Dynasty after dynasty
Early Tradition names King Dharmavarma as the first builder and the early Cōḻa Kiḷḷivaḷavan as the one who rebuilt it after a flood.
6th–9th c. The twelve Āḻvārs sing of Raṅganātha in 247 hymns.
Cōḻas The Cōḻas build and endow the temple; Cōḻa work survives, for example in the Cakrattāḻvār shrine’s front hall.
11th–12th c. Rāmānuja leads the Śrīvaiṣṇava community from Śrīraṅgam.
Pāṇḍyas, Hoysaḷas Further patronage and building; inscriptions also name Marāṭhās and Gajapatis among donors.
1336–1565 Under Vijayanagara, the thousand-pillared hall is built.
Nāyakas The Madurai Nāyakas add the Śeṣarāya and Garuḍa maṇḍapas; Chokkanātha Nāyaka rebuilds the Veṇugopāla shrine (1674).

Never finished. Each dynasty added a wall, a hall or a tower around the last. The temple grew outward from the Lord like rings in a tree.

HISTORY · II

The Lord in exile, and his return
1310–11 The army of Malik Kāfūr raids the temple.
1323–27 Forces of Muhammad bin Tughluq invade. Tradition remembers some 13,000 devotees dying in the temple’s defence.
The exile Piḷḷai Lokācārya leads a band carrying Namperumāḷ away for safety, towards Tirunelveli; Raṅganāyakī is taken elsewhere. The Lord’s image wanders for decades.
1371 Under Kumāra Kampaṇa of Vijayanagara the temple is restored and the image returns home.

Tulukka Nācciyār. Temple tradition remembers a Sultan’s daughter who loved the Lord’s image so deeply that she is honoured within the temple, shown in a niche on horseback: a story of devotion that crossed every border.

Vedānta Deśika. The great teacher and poet of the 13th–14th centuries is closely associated with the temple and has a shrine here.

ZAPPY: The Lord himself went into exile?

PROF. BOLT: His festival image did, carried by devotees for decades. Many say the homecoming is part of why Namperumāḷ is so loved: 'our Lord', who came back.

SEVEN WALLS

The plan of the temple

🧱 Seven prākāras

Seven concentric enclosures, their walls together about 9.9 km long, surround the shrine. The outer ones hold streets, houses and shops: a town inside the temple.

🏯 21 gopurams

Twenty-one gateway towers, 17 of them major. The southern Rājagopuram, begun around 1500, was completed by the Ahobila Maṭha and consecrated on 25 March 1987, about 73 m high.

The golden vimāna. Over the sanctum rises a gold-plated vimāna shaped like the Tamil letter for Oṃ, the praṇava, with an image of Paravāsudeva on its front.

ZAPPY: A shrine to a discus?

PROF. BOLT: Cakrattāḻvār: the Lord's own weapon, honoured as a devotee. In Śrīraṅgam, even the discus is a saint.

VAIKUṆṬHA EKĀDAŚĪ

The gate of heaven opens

🌞 Pagal Pattu

“Ten days”: the first ten days of the Mārgaḻi festival.

🌙 Rā Pattu

“Ten nights”: the next ten. The first day of Rā Pattu is Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī.

The gate. The Paramapada Vāsal, the gate of the supreme abode, opens only during these ten days. Namperumāḷ passes through it at dawn on Ekādaśī, and vast crowds follow him through.

The Tamil Veda sung. The twenty days are an adhyayana utsava, a festival of recitation, when the Āḻvārs’ 4,000 verses are sung before the Lord.

In 2026, Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī falls on 20 December.

Festivals of Śrīraṅgam

Thai (Jan–Feb)Rathotsava, the temple car festival

ZAPPY: The Lord and the Goddess together just once a year?

PROF. BOLT: She never leaves her shrine; he goes to her. Paṅguni Uttiram is that one meeting, and devotees wait all year to see them side by side.

Hari and Hara. As at the other two Raṅgas, Viṣṇu’s island shares its waters with Śiva: Raṅganātha and Jambukeśvara, a short walk apart.

THE LAST OF THREE

Antya Raṅga in the Triraṅga

The end of the vow. In the Triraṅga, Antya Raṅga comes last, after Ādi Raṅga and Madhya Raṅga. The vow ends here, at dusk, before the greatest of the three.

First, middle, last. If the three are a life, this is its end: and for the Śrīvaiṣṇava, the end is the Lord’s own abode, the gate of Vaikuṇṭha that opens here every Mārgaḻi.

Everything in five lines
