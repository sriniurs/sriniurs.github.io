THREE RAṄGANĀTHAS ON THE KĀVERĪ · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Ādi Raṅga · Madhya Raṅga · Antya Raṅga

FOREWORD

The Kāverī rises in the hills of Kodagu and runs east to the sea. Three times on the way she parts and joins again, holding an island in her arms, and on each island Viṣṇu lies at rest as Raṅganātha.

To see all three in a single day, from the first to the last, is the Triraṅga Darśanam, a vow especially loved in the month of Dhanurmāsa. Two of the temples are in Karnataka; the third, Śrīraṅgam, is among the greatest temples in the world.

This guide introduces all three. A full guide to each follows.

ZAPPY: Why does Viṣṇu keep lying down by rivers?

PROF. BOLT: Resting on Ādiśeṣa is how he holds the world: still, watchful, at peace. Where the river makes an island, devotees see the cosmic ocean in small.

ZAPPY: Three temples, all called Raṅganātha?

PROF. BOLT: Yes! One Lord, resting on the same river at three places. 'Ādi', 'Madhya' and 'Antya' just mean first, middle and last, counting downstream.

WHERE THEY ARE

Three islands on the Kāverī

The Kāverī rises at Talakāveri in the hills of Kodagu and flows east across Karnataka and Tamil Nadu to the Bay of Bengal.

At three places on its way, the river splits and joins again, wrapping an island in its arms. On each island stands a temple of Raṅganātha. Counting downstream, they are the first (Ādi), middle (Madhya) and last (Antya) Raṅga.

Two of the three are in Karnataka; the last, and largest, is in Tamil Nadu, where Śrīraṅgam island lies between the Kāverī and its branch, the Koḷḷiḍam.

WHO IS RAṄGANĀTHA?

Viṣṇu, at rest on the cosmic serpent

🛏 The posture

Viṣṇu reclines on the coils of the serpent Ādiśeṣa, whose hoods shade his head. This form is called anantaśayana, “resting on Ananta”.

🏛 The name

Raṅga means a stage or hall; Raṅganātha is “Lord of the Raṅga”. His island homes are each named Śrīraṅga: Śrīraṅgam, Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa.

Asleep, yet awake. The reclining Lord is in yoganidrā, the sleep of yoga, holding the world in his rest. It is the same scene that opens the Devī Māhātmya’s first battle: Viṣṇu asleep on Śeṣa on the cosmic ocean.

THE STORIES

Why the Lord rests on these islands
Many tellings. Each temple has its own legends, and tellings differ from place to place. These are the best-known ones, given as tradition.
The river’s wish. One popular telling links all three to the Kāverī herself: born to serve humanity, she longed to keep Viṣṇu close, and so at three places she wraps her arms around an island where he rests. At Śrīraṅgam, tradition also says the river goddess asked Raṅganātha to stay.
Vibhīṣaṇa’s burden. Śrīraṅgam’s famous legend: the image was given by Rāma to Vibhīṣaṇa to carry to Laṅkā; it would not move past this island, so it stayed, and the Lord faces south, towards Laṅkā, to console him.
Indra’s penance. At Madhya Raṅga, temple tradition tells of Indra, burdened by the sin of slaying the demon Vṛtra, finding release through Raṅganātha’s grace.
Ādi Raṅga. At Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa, the island town itself is named after the temple, the first Raṅga on the river.

ZAPPY: Rāmānuja lived here?

PROF. BOLT: The Hoysaḷa king gave him this village as a seat of learning. The great Śrīvaiṣṇava teacher links Ādi Raṅga and Antya Raṅga, where he spent his last years.

MADHYA RAṄGA · ŚIVANASAMUDRA

The middle Raṅga, between the waterfalls
DeityRaṅganātha as Jaganmohana or Mohana Raṅga, “enchanter of the world”: of the three, the Lord in his youthful form
SettingAn island of the Kāverī at Śivanasamudra, between the twin waterfalls Gaganacukki and Bharacukki
TempleBuilt in the Drāviḍa style; described as ancient, with no founding date on record; renovation began in 2021
LegendTemple tradition links the shrine to Indra’s release from the sin of slaying Vṛtra
NearbyThe Someśvara temple, where tradition says Ādi Śaṅkara installed a Śrī Cakra, and a Vanadurgā temple

ZAPPY: Why is this one the 'young' Raṅganātha?

PROF. BOLT: That's how devotees see him here: Jaganmohana, the charming youth. The first Raṅga, the young Raṅga, the great Raṅga: one Lord, three moods.

Some local traditions place Madhya Raṅga at Sathyagāla, a village nearby on the Kāverī; Śivanasamudra is the usual identification.

ANTYA RAṄGA · ŚRĪRAṄGAM

The last Raṅga, and the greatest
DeityRaṅganātha, Periya Perumāḷ, reclining on Ādiśeṣa and facing south; the Goddess as Raṅganāyakī
SizeAbout 63 hectares (155 acres) inside seven concentric enclosures, with 21 gopurams; often called one of the largest functioning Hindu temples
RājagopuramAbout 73 m high, 13 tiers; begun around 1500, completed and consecrated in 1987
Divya DeśamForemost of the 108; the only one praised by all twelve Āḻvārs, in 247 hymns. Āṇḍāḷ, poet of the Tiruppāvai, is said to have merged into this Lord
HistoryCōḻa, Pāṇḍya, Hoysaḷa, Vijayanagara and Nāyaka patronage; raided in 1310–11 and 1323–27, the image was carried away for safety and reinstated in 1371
RāmānujaThe teacher (1017–1137) lived and taught here; his preserved body is enshrined in the temple

ZAPPY: Seven walls?

PROF. BOLT: Seven, one inside the other, like the layers of a mandala, with a whole town living within them. And at the centre, the Lord resting.

ONE DAY, IN DHANURMĀSA

The tradition behind the journey

🌅 Dawn to dusk

Triraṅga Darśanam means seeing all three Raṅganāthas on a single day, between dawn and dusk, following the river from the first to the last.

🪔 In Dhanurmāsa

The practice is especially linked with Dhanurmāsa (mid-December to mid-January), the holiest month of Viṣṇu worship, when temples open in the dark before dawn.

A vow, not a race. The one-day journey is understood as a saṅkalpa, a personal vow. Teachers stress the intention behind it rather than speed; the same three temples seen with devotion on different days are no less blessed.

First, middle, last. Some explain the order as a picture of a life: beginning, middle and end, or birth, life and liberation, all held in the one Lord.

The month of Raṅganātha. Dhanurmāsa is also when Āṇḍāḷ’s Tiruppāvai is sung, a song to the Lord of Śrīraṅgam; and its Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī is Śrīraṅgam’s greatest festival, when the Paramapada vāsal, the gate of heaven, is opened.

ZAPPY: Do I fail if I miss one?

PROF. BOLT: No! The tradition stresses the vow and the devotion, not a stopwatch. See them with love, in a day or in a lifetime.

THE WIDER FAMILY

The Pañcaraṅga Kṣetras: five Raṅgas on the Kāverī

SārṅgapāṇiSārṅgapāṇi temple, Kumbakōṇam, Tamil NaduDivya Deśam
Parimala RaṅgaParimaḷa Raṅganātha, Indaḷūr (Mayilāḍuturai), Tamil NaduDivya Deśam

Three and five. Triraṅga follows the three island temples. A second tradition counts five Raṅganātha temples on the Kāverī, the Pañcaraṅga Kṣetras. Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa and Śrīraṅgam belong to both; Madhya Raṅga is usually counted only in the three.

Lists vary. Some lists name other temples for the fifth place, such as the Vaṭaraṅga at Sīrkāḻi or the Raṅganātha temple at Nellore.

Other reclining Viṣṇus. The same resting form is worshipped across the South, most famously as Padmanābhasvāmi in Thiruvananthapuram.
