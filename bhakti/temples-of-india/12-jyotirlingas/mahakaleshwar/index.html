MAHĀKĀLA

THE 12 JYOTIRLIṄGAS · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Legends, history, rituals, festivals and the sacred city of Ujjayinī · explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: A one-page summary first, then maps, the stotram line, the legends, a full history, the three-storeyed temple, the Bhasma Ārati and the day's rituals, festivals, the shrines of Ujjayinī, the Pañcakrośī walk, the city of time, and its poets and kings. Keep it as a reference.

FOREWORD

Before dawn in Ujjayinī, while the city sleeps, the Lord of Time is bathed, adorned and covered in sacred ash. The Bhasma Ārati of Mahākāla is one of the most striking rituals in all of India, and one of the oldest continuous ones.

Mahākāla is the only jyotirliṅga that faces south, the direction of Yama and of death. Here Śiva is worshipped as Time itself, the power that ends all things, and so as the one who frees his devotees from the fear of untimely death.

Ujjayinī around him is one of India's seven sacred cities: Avantikā of the Purāṇas, Kālidāsa's city of lamps, the meridian of the old astronomers, a city of the Kumbh. This fourth guide in our series follows the third name in the stotram through its legends, its long history and its living rituals.

May every reader one day hear the drums of the Bhasma Ārati in the dark, and see the Lord of Time awake.

ZAPPY: Why does facing south matter?

PROF. BOLT: South is the direction of Yama, the god of death. Mahākāla faces him down. That's why devotees pray here for freedom from the fear of death.

WHERE IT IS

Ujjayinī on the Kṣiprā, in the heart of Mālwā

Ujjayinī, "the victorious", stands on the Mālwā plateau of western Madhya Pradesh, on the east bank of the Kṣiprā, a river that flows northward to join the Chambal.

The Purāṇas call the city Avantikā and count it among the seven mokṣapurīs, cities that grant liberation, with Ayodhyā, Mathurā, Māyā (Haridvār), Kāśī, Kāñcī and Dwārakā.

The Tropic of Cancer passes very close to the city, and the old astronomers made Ujjayinī the zero meridian of their maps of the sky.

IN THE JYOTIRLIṄGA STOTRAM

The third name in the twelve
saurāṣṭre somanāthaṃ caśrīśaile mallikārjunam |ujjayinyāṃ mahākālamoṅkāram amaleśvaram ‖
What it says

"Somanātha in Saurāṣṭra, Mallikārjuna on Śrīśaila, Mahākāla in Ujjayinī, and at Oṅkāra, Amaleśvara."

WordMeans
ujjayinyāmin Ujjayinī, "the victorious city"
mahāgreat
kālatime; also death, and the colour black. Mahākāla is the Time that swallows all time

The longer hymn. Verse three of the twelve-verse stotram traditionally ascribed to Ādi Śaṅkara: avantikāyāṃ vihitāvatāraṃ muktipradānāya ca sajjanānām | akāla-mṛtyoḥ parirakṣaṇārthaṃ vande mahākāla-mahāsureśam: "I bow to Mahākāla, great Lord of the gods, who took form in Avantikā to give liberation to the good, and to protect from untimely death."

Time and death. In Sanskrit, kāla is both. To worship Mahākāla is to bow to the one power no one escapes, and so to lose the fear of it. The Mahāmṛtyuñjaya mantra (see our article) is chanted here constantly.

Third in the stotram: Mahākāla in Ujjayinī, the Lord of Time who protects from untimely death.

THE LEGENDS · I

Śiva bursts from the earth

The Brahmin of Avanti. The Koṭirudra Saṃhitā of the Śiva Purāṇa tells of a learned Brahmin, Vedapriya, who lived in Avanti with his four devout sons, worshipping a liṅga of earth every day.

The demon Dūṣaṇa. On the Ratnamāla mountain nearby lived the demon Dūṣaṇa, made almost invincible by a boon from Brahmā. He set out to stop all Vedic worship, and attacked Avanti with his army.

The roar. The Brahmins did not run. They sat and went on worshipping. As the demons closed in, the earth beside the liṅga split open and Śiva burst out in a terrible form, with a roar (huṃkāra) that turned Dūṣaṇa and his army to ash.

The Lord stays. The people begged Śiva to remain and protect the city. He did, as Mahākāla, rising from that very pit as the jyotirliṅga, facing south, guardian of Avanti forever.

🔥 Ash

The demon became ash; ash became Mahākāla's ornament. The Bhasma Ārati recalls that Śiva wears the end of all things.

⏳ Kāla

Time devours everything. Mahākāla is time's master, the one who remains when even time ends.

The Brahmins did not fight; they kept worshipping. In the story, steadiness in the face of fear is what calls the Lord out of the ground.

ZAPPY: So the liṅga came up from below the earth?

PROF. BOLT: That's why the sanctum is below ground level even today. You go down to meet Mahākāla.

THE LEGENDS · II

The king, the cowherd boy and the guardians

King Candrasena. Ujjayinī's king Candrasena was a great devotee of Mahākāla. Maṇibhadra, a follower of Śiva, gave him the wish-granting jewel cintāmaṇi. Other kings, greedy for it, laid siege to the city. Candrasena simply took refuge in Mahākāla.

Śrīkara. A cowherd widow's little son, Śrīkara, watched the king's worship and copied it at home with a pebble for a liṅga, forgetting even to eat. His mother, angry, threw the stone away. The boy wept and fainted; when he woke, a temple of gold and jewels stood where his pebble had been.

The blessing. The besieging kings, hearing of the miracle, laid down their arms and came to worship. Hanumān appeared and blessed the boy, foretelling that Nanda, Kṛṣṇa's foster-father, would be born in his line. The Śiva Purāṇa tells this as part of the Mahākāla story.

King of Ujjayinī. Tradition says Mahākāla is the true king of the city. That is why, on Mondays of Śrāvaṇa and Bhādrapada, he rides out in a palanquin to see his subjects; and why, by a long-held belief, no other king should spend the night in Ujjayinī.

🔴 Harasiddhi

The Goddess beside Mahākāla: a Śakti Pīṭha where tradition says Satī's elbow fell.

🐕 Kāla Bhairava

The city's guardian, offered liquor as prasāda.

🏺 The Kumbh

A drop of amṛta fell at Ujjayinī: hence the Siṃhastha.

HISTORY · I

From Avanti to Iltutmish
6th c. BCE Ujjayinī is the capital of Avanti, one of the great kingdoms of the Buddha's time, ruled by King Pradyota. Under the Mauryas, Aśoka serves here as viceroy before becoming emperor.
Coins Ancient coins found at Ujjain carry Śaiva symbols, a sign of how old the city's Śiva worship is.
c. 400 Kālidāsa, in the Meghadūta, sends his cloud-messenger over Ujjayinī and describes the evening worship at Mahākāla's shrine, with music and dancers.
7th c. Bāṇabhaṭṭa, court poet of King Harṣa, praises Mahākāla in the Kādambarī.
11th–12th c. The Paramāra kings of Mālwā rebuild the temple; an inscription credits Udayāditya and Naravarman, in the Bhūmija style the Paramāras loved.
1234–35 Sultan Shams-ud-dīn Iltutmish of Delhi raids Ujjain and destroys the temple complex. Tradition says the jyotirliṅga was hidden in, or thrown into, the nearby Koṭitīrtha kuṇḍa.
After For five centuries the shrine survives in reduced form; worship continues.
Early dates are approximate; accounts differ on what happened to the liṅga in 1234–35.

HISTORY · II

The Marathas to Mahākāl Lok
1734 Peshwā Bājīrāv I gives charge of Mālwā to his general Ranoji Shinde (Scindia). Ranoji's diwān, Rāmcandra Bābā Shenvi (Sukhtankar), a wealthy and pious man, builds the present five-storeyed temple.
18th–19th c. The Shinde family keeps up the temple: Mahādjī Shinde, and later Daulat Rao's queen Baizā Bāī, are remembered as patrons. Under the Gwalior state, major royal ceremonies are held here.
After 1947 The temple comes under a management committee of the state government, with the Ujjain collector at its head.
2016 The last Siṃhastha Kumbh draws crores of pilgrims to the Kṣiprā.
2022 On 11 October, the first phase of Shri Mahākāl Lok opens: a corridor of about 900 m around the Rudrasāgar lake, lined with statues and murals of Śiva Purāṇa stories.
2025 Footfall at the temple, about 1.5 crore a year before the corridor, has nearly quadrupled, according to press reports.
2028 The next Siṃhastha is due from about 9 April to 8 May 2028; large works are under way across the city.

Why "Siṃhastha". The Kumbh at Ujjayinī is held when Jupiter (Bṛhaspati) is in Siṃha, Leo: hence its name, and its twelve-year cycle.

THE TEMPLE TODAY

Three shrines, one above the other

⬇️ Mahākāleśvara

The jyotirliṅga is in a sanctum below ground level, reached by steps, and faces south. Images of Gaṇeśa, Pārvatī and Kārttikeya are set around it, with Nandi before it.

🕉️ Oṃkāreśvara

On the level above, a shrine of Śiva as Oṃkāreśvara: a reminder of the next jyotirliṅga, on the Narmadā.

🐍 Nāgacandreśvara

At the top, an 11th-century image of Śiva and Pārvatī seated on a ten-hooded serpent. The shrine is opened to the public for only about 24 hours a year, on Nāga Pañcamī.

💧 Koṭitīrtha

The sacred tank in the precinct, linked by tradition with the hiding of the liṅga in 1234–35.

The temple mixes Bhūmija, Chalukya and Maratha styles. Around it, Mahākāl Lok frames the Rudrasāgar lake with a long mural wall, a "Nandī Dwār" gateway and large statues of scenes from the Śiva Purāṇa.

Bhasma Ārati~4–6 AMThe doors open in the dark. The liṅga is bathed with water, milk, curd and honey, adorned, then covered with sacred ash to the sound of drums, bells and chanting.

Sandhyā pūjā~5 PMEvening worship and adornment (śṛṅgāra).
Sandhyā ārati~7 PMThe main evening ārati: the Lord as a king, crowned and decorated.
Śayana ārati~10:30 PMThe last ārati; the Lord is put to rest.

The ash. Tradition speaks of ash from the cremation ground; today the temple's ash is widely reported to be made from burnt cow-dung cakes and sacred woods. Those inside the hall wear traditional dress (dhotī for men, sārī for women), and attendance is limited by passes from the temple administration. By custom, women cover their heads or look away at the moment the ash is applied.

AROUND THE TEMPLE

The shrines of Avantikā

🔴 Harasiddhi

A Śakti Pīṭha a short walk from Mahākāla, with two tall lamp towers (dīpa-stambhas) lit with hundreds of lamps at festivals. Tradition links her with King Vikramāditya.

🐕 Kāla Bhairava

North of the old city: the fierce guardian, famous for being offered liquor, which the image is said to drink.

🌊 Rām Ghāṭ

The main bathing ghat on the Kṣiprā, scene of the Siṃhastha bathing and the evening Kṣiprā ārati.

🪈 Gopāl Mandir

A 19th-century Kṛṣṇa temple of the Scindias in the market, with silver doors: Mahākāla's destination at Harihara Milan.

🐘 Cintāmaṇi Gaṇeśa

An old Gaṇeśa shrine outside the city, believed to be self-manifested; also Baḍe Gaṇeśa by Mahākāla.

⚫ Gaḍhkālikā

The Goddess tradition links with Kālidāsa, who is said to have received his genius from her.

🔴 Maṅgalnāth

On a rise above the Kṣiprā: held to be the birthplace of Maṅgala, the planet Mars.

📚 Sāndīpani Āśram

Where Kṛṣṇa, Balarāma and Sudāmā studied with the sage Sāndīpani; the Gomatī Kuṇḍa is here.

Also: Siddhavaṭ, a sacred banyan on the river; the Bhartṛhari caves; and Juna Mahākāla inside the temple precinct.

THE PAÑCAKROŚĪ YĀTRĀ

Walking around Mahākāla's forest

The Avantī Khaṇḍa of the Skanda Purāṇa describes four gates around Mahākālavana, the sacred forest of Mahākāla, each guarded by a form of Śiva. The Pañcakrośī Yātrā walks around them all.

Pilgrims bathe in the Kṣiprā and set out from the Nāgacandreśvara shrine, then walk about 118 km over five days in the month of Vaiśākha (traditionally from kṛṣṇa daśamī to amāvāsyā), with night halts at the guardian shrines: Piṅgaleśvara (east), Kāyāvarohaṇeśvara (south), Bilkeśvara (west) and Durdureśvara (north), before returning to the river. Lakhs walk it each year, many from Mālwā's villages, carrying their food on their heads.

The 84 Mahādevas. The same tradition lists 84 liṅgas within the city, the Caurāsī Mahādeva, each with its story; devotees visit them in a separate yātrā, especially in Śrāvaṇa.

ZAPPY: Kāśī has a Pañcakrośī too!

PROF. BOLT: And the Skanda Purāṇa, proud of Avanti, says this one gives even more merit. Each holy city praises its own walk.

UJJAYINĪ, CITY OF TIME

Where the Lord of Time met the astronomers

🌐 The first meridian

Siddhānta texts such as the Sūrya Siddhānta reckon longitude from the meridian of Ujjayinī, much as Greenwich is used today.

🔭 Varāhamihira

The great 6th-century astronomer-astrologer, author of the Bṛhat Saṃhitā, lived and worked in the Ujjayinī region.

🧭 Vedh Śālā

An observatory built by Mahārājā Sawai Jai Singh II of Jaipur in the 18th century, one of his five "Jantar Mantars"; it is still used to prepare almanacs.

📅 Vikrama Saṃvat

The calendar still used across North and West India is linked by tradition with King Vikramāditya of Ujjayinī; it counts from 57 BCE.

The city of the Lord of Time became the city of the time-keepers. Measuring time and bowing to Time are not so far apart.

UJJAYINĪ IN CULTURE

Poets, kings and teachers

📜 Kālidāsa

India's greatest Sanskrit poet loved this city. In the Meghadūta, the cloud is told not to miss Ujjayinī, its palaces, its river and the evening worship of Mahākāla.

👑 Vikramāditya

The legendary king of Ujjayinī, of the "nine gems" (navaratna) of his court and the tales of Vetāla and the throne of thirty-two statues.

🧘 Bhartṛhari

Poet of the Śataka-traya, said to have been a king of Ujjayinī who renounced his throne; the caves on the Kṣiprā bear his name.

🎓 Sāndīpani

The sage who taught Kṛṣṇa and Balarāma the sixty-four arts in a few weeks, by tradition; their friendship with Sudāmā began here.

🎭 Māc theatre

Mālwā's folk musical theatre, performed on a raised stage (māc), has deep roots in Ujjain.

🔱 The Nāth yogīs

Ujjain is linked with Bhartṛhari's Nāth tradition and the shrine of Matsyendranāth, the guru of Gorakhnāth.

ZAPPY: A king, a poet and a god all in one city?

PROF. BOLT: And in Ujjayinī, the god is the king. Every Savārī reminds the city who really rules it.
