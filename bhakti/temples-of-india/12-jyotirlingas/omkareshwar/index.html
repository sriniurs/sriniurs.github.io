OṂKĀREŚVARA

THE 12 JYOTIRLIṄGAS · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Legends, history, rituals, festivals and the island of Oṃ in the Narmadā · explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: A one-page summary first, then maps, the stotram line and its two shrines, the legends, a full history, the five-storeyed temple, the day's rituals, festivals, the island walk, the Narmadā, the valley around it, and Ādi Śaṅkara's Oṃkāreśvara. Keep it as a reference.

FOREWORD

In the middle of the Narmadā lies a small rocky island, Māndhātā. Seen from above, the river curving around it is said to trace the sacred syllable Oṃ. On it stands Oṃkāreśvara, the Lord of Oṃ; across the water stands his twin, Amaleśvara. Together they are the fourth jyotirliṅga.

Here, tradition says, a boy from Kerala walked across India to find his teacher in a cave by the river, and became Ādi Śaṅkara. Here Śiva is said to rest each night and play dice with Pārvatī. And here the Narmadā, the river whose mere sight is said to purify, flows past ghats where pilgrims begin their months-long walk around her.

This fifth guide in our series follows the fourth name in the stotram: its legends, its Paramāra and Holkar history, its rituals, and the island and river that make it unique.

May every reader one day cross the Narmadā to the island of Oṃ, and hear the river chant it.

ZAPPY: Two temples for one jyotirliṅga?

PROF. BOLT: The stotram itself names both: Oṅkāra and Amaleśvara. Pilgrims say the visit isn't complete until you've seen the two.

WHERE IT IS

An island of Oṃ in the Narmadā

The Narmadā rises at Amarkaṇṭak and flows west across central India to the Arabian Sea, between the Vindhya and Sātpuṛā ranges. In the Nimāṛ region of Madhya Pradesh it divides around a hilly island: Māndhātā, also called Śivapurī.

The Oṃkāreśvara temple stands on the island's southern side, above the river. Across the water, on the south bank, is Amaleśvara (Mamaleśvara).

A channel called the Kāverī runs along the island's north, so the island lies at a meeting of waters; pilgrims bathe at the saṅgam.

IN THE STOTRAM

Oṅkāra and Amaleśvara: one line, two shrines
saurāṣṭre somanāthaṃ caśrīśaile mallikārjunam |ujjayinyāṃ mahākālamoṅkāram amaleśvaram ‖
What it says

"Somanātha in Saurāṣṭra, Mallikārjuna on Śrīśaila, Mahākāla in Ujjayinī, and at Oṅkāra, Amaleśvara."

WordMeans
oṅkāramOṅkāra: the syllable Oṃ, and the place named after it
amaleśvaramthe pure (amala) Lord; also read as Amareśvara, "Lord of the immortals"

Two readings. The line can mean "Amaleśvara at Oṅkāra", naming the south-bank shrine as the jyotirliṅga, or "Oṅkāra and Amaleśvara", naming both. Tradition settles it gently: the Śiva Purāṇa says the one liṅga became two, and both are honoured as the fourth jyotirliṅga. Some say the jyoti (light) is at Oṃkāreśvara and the earthly (pārthiva) liṅga at Amaleśvara.

The longer hymn. Verse four of the stotram traditionally ascribed to Ādi Śaṅkara praises the Lord "on the pure island where the Kāverī and Narmadā meet", who lives "on the Māndhātṛ hill to help the good cross over": kāverikā-narmadayoḥ pavitre samāgame sajjana-tāraṇāya … oṃkāram īśaṃ śivam ekam īḍe, "I praise the one Śiva, Lord Oṃkāra."

Fourth in the stotram: Oṅkāra and Amaleśvara, the Lord of Oṃ on the island where two rivers meet.

THE LEGENDS · I

The mountain's pride and the shape of Oṃ

Vindhya's envy. The Śiva Purāṇa tells that the sage Nārada visited the Vindhya mountain and praised Mount Meru as the greatest of all. Stung, Vindhya resolved to win Śiva's grace. At Oṃkāra he made an image of Śiva on an Oṃkāra yantra and worshipped it with great austerity for six months.

The boon. Śiva appeared and granted Vindhya's wish. At the request of the gods and sages, he agreed to stay. The single liṅga became two: Oṃkāreśvara on the island, and Parameśvara, known as Amaleśvara, on the bank.

The gods' war. Another account says the gods, beaten by the dānavas, prayed here, and Śiva appeared as Oṃkāreśvara and defeated the demons.

The shape. The island and the river curving around it are said to trace the syllable Oṃ. To walk around the island, as pilgrims do, is to walk around Oṃ itself.

🕉️ Oṃ

The first sound, from which all others come: the Upaniṣads call it the whole of existence in one syllable.

⛰️ Vindhya

The same proud mountain that, in another story, bowed to the sage Agastya and has stayed low ever since.

Vindhya came out of envy and went home with grace. Even a mixed motive, offered sincerely, can open the door.

ZAPPY: A mountain that gets jealous?

PROF. BOLT: In the Purāṇas, even mountains have feelings. The point is what Vindhya did with his: he worshipped.

THE LEGENDS · II

A king, a bedtime and a teacher in a cave

King Māndhātā. The island takes its name from Māndhātā, a great king of the Ikṣvāku line (the line of Rāma), who is said to have done long austerities here until Śiva appeared as the jyotirliṅga. His sons, Ambarīṣa and Mucukunda, are also linked with the place.

Śiva's bedroom. A much-loved belief says that after roaming the three worlds each day, Śiva comes to Oṃkāreśvara to sleep. So every night, at the Śayana Ārati, a bed is laid out and a dice board (caupaṛ) set before it, for Śiva and Pārvatī to play. Priests say the dice are found scattered in the morning, as if someone had played in the night.

The boy from Kālaḍi. Tradition says the young Śaṅkara, having left Kerala as a boy-monk, walked to the Narmadā and found his guru, Govinda Bhagavatpāda, meditating in a cave at Oṃkāreśvara. Govinda asked who he was; the boy answered in verse that he was neither earth nor water nor fire, but the pure Self: words remembered in the Daśaśloki. Govinda accepted him as his disciple.

The flood. Another story says the Narmadā rose in flood while Govinda was in samādhi, and Śaṅkara held the waters back with his water-pot, singing the Narmadāṣṭakam.

HISTORY · I

The Paramāra island
Purāṇas The Skanda, Śiva and Vāyu Purāṇas praise Oṃkāra-Māndhātā; the Narmadā Khaṇḍa (Revā Khaṇḍa) of the Skanda Purāṇa lists its many tīrthas.
1055 The earliest Paramāra record at Māndhātā dates from the first regnal year of King Jayasiṃha I (1055–70). The Paramāras of Dhārā hold the island for about two centuries.
11th c. The Paramāras build or enlarge the temples here; Amaleśvara on the south bank is dated to this period.
1225 An inscription of King Devapāla.
1274 The last Paramāra record at Māndhātā, of King Jayavarman II.
13th c. The Dharmarāja Dvāra, the western gate of the old Māndhātā fort on the island, is attributed to the Paramāras.
After 1300 Paramāra power ends with the Khaljī conquest of Mālwā (1305); the region later passes under the sultans of Mālwā. Local Chauhān (Bhilālā) chiefs, the Rāo family of Māndhātā, keep the temple through these centuries.
Dates come from published inscriptions; claims of specific medieval destructions here are not well documented and are left out.

HISTORY · II

From the Holkars to Ekātma Dhām
18th c. The Holkars of Indore rebuild the Oṃkāreśvara temple: the work is begun by Queen Gautamābāī Holkar and completed by her daughter-in-law Ahilyābāī Holkar (ruled 1767–95), who also restores Amaleśvara and the ghats.
1824 The Scindias of Gwalior, by then overlords of the area, hand Māndhātā to the British.
1890s The archaeologist Henry Cousens photographs the Siddhanātha temple's carved pillars; the island's old temples are recorded and later protected.
After 1947 Amaleśvara is a protected monument of the Archaeological Survey of India; Oṃkāreśvara is run by a temple trust.
2017 The Madhya Pradesh government announces the Ekātma Dhām project in honour of Ādi Śaṅkara.
2023 On 21 September, the 108-ft Statue of Oneness (Ekātmatā kī Pratimā), showing Śaṅkara as a boy of twelve, is unveiled on the Māndhātā hill, on a 75-ft base, weighing about 100 tonnes. A museum (Advaita Lok) and an institute of Advaita Vedānta are planned around it.

Why a boy of twelve? Tradition says Śaṅkara was about twelve when he finished his studies with Govinda here and left Oṃkāreśvara to teach. The statue faces south toward the Narmadā.

THE TEMPLE TODAY

Five floors on the island, and a twin across the water

🪨 The liṅga

A natural, uncarved rock, not under the centre of the spire but to one side, always surrounded by water; a silver image of Pārvatī stands behind it.

🏛️ The halls

A large assembly hall on about 60 carved stone pillars; Nāgara-style spires above the five storeys.

🛕 Amaleśvara

On the south bank: an 11th-century stone temple with fine sculpture, protected by the ASI. Its walls carry long Sanskrit inscriptions, including the Mahimna-stotra.

📜 Halāyudha's hymn

One inscription at Amaleśvara, the Halāyudha-stotra, has been called "a little known masterpiece" by the epigraphist Richard Salomon.

Below the main temple is the Govindeśvara shrine and the cave associated with Govinda Bhagavatpāda and Śaṅkara.

A DAY AT THE TEMPLE

From Maṅgala darśan to Śayana
RitualApprox. timeWhat happens
Maṅgala~5 AMThe Lord is woken; the day's first abhiṣeka with Narmadā water, milk and curd.
Madhyāhna bhoga~12:20 PMThe midday meal offering.
Sāyaṃkālīna śṛṅgāra~4 PMThe evening adornment, with flowers, sandal and ornaments.
Śṛṅgāra ārati~8:30 PMThe main evening ārati.
Śayana~9 PMThe bedtime ritual: the bed, the dice board, a lullaby of bells. The temple then closes for the night.

Abhiṣeka three times a day. The liṅga is bathed with milk, curd and Narmadā water three times daily. On the ghats, priests also offer a nightly ārati to Mother Narmadā at Koṭitīrtha.

The palanquin. On set days the festival image of Oṃkāreśvara is carried in a pālakī (palanquin) procession to the river and around the island; on Śrāvaṇa Mondays the processions draw large crowds.

AROUND THE TEMPLE

Shrines on the island and the banks

🛕 Amaleśvara

The twin jyotirliṅga on the south bank, older in fabric, rich in sculpture and inscriptions.

🐘 Siddhanātha

A medieval temple on the island, famous for its frieze of carved elephants around the base and its sculpted pillars.

⚫ Gaurī Somanātha

A three-storeyed temple with a very large black liṅga, on the island's parikramā path.

🕉️ Govindeśvara cave

Below the main temple: the cave of Govinda Bhagavatpāda, with an image of Śaṅkara.

🗿 Ekātma Dhām

The 108-ft Statue of Oneness on the hilltop, looking south over the river.

🌊 Koṭitīrtha ghāṭ

The main ghat below the temple, where pilgrims bathe and the parikramā starts.

🐘 Pañcamukhī Gaṇeśa

A five-faced Gaṇeśa near Amaleśvara, with images of Viṣṇu's 24 avatāras nearby.

🏰 Māndhātā palace

The old residence of the Rāo family, hereditary custodians of the island, near the temple.

Also on the island: Kedāreśvara, Ṛṇamukteśvara, the old fort gates, and a gurdwara marking a visit by Guru Nānak.

THE ISLAND PARIKRAMĀ

Walking around Oṃ

Pilgrims walk around mountains they hold sacred: Kailāsa, Govardhana, Girnār. Here the mountain is an island, and the island is Oṃ. The parikramā of Māndhātā (Oṃkāra Parvata) is a path of about 7 km, starting and ending by the river, past old gateways of the Paramāra fort, ruined and living temples, āśramas and, at one point, the meeting of the Kāverī and the Narmadā.

The walk is a journey through the island's history: the Dharmarāja Dvāra, the 13th-century western gate of the Māndhātā fort; the Cānd–Sūraj Dvāra at the highest point; the carved Siddhanātha; and verses of the Gītā on stones along the way.

To circle the island is to circle Oṃ, and so, the tradition says, to circle everything. The walk ends where it began: at the river.

MOTHER NARMADĀ

The river whose sight purifies

Born of Śiva. Tradition says the Narmadā was born from Śiva's body, from his sweat as he danced or meditated on the Maikal hills; she is called Revā ("the leaping one") and Śāṅkarī, Śiva's daughter. She rises at Amarkaṇṭak and flows west for about 1,300 km to the Gulf of Khambhāt.

"Gaṅgā by bathing, Narmadā by sight." An old saying holds that the Gaṅgā purifies those who bathe in her, but the Narmadā purifies by her mere darśan. She is also called a virgin river, flowing alone and proud.

🚶 Narmadā Parikramā

One of India's great walking pilgrimages: about 2,600–3,500 km (routes vary), down one bank to the sea and back up the other, never crossing the river. It can take months or, traditionally, three years, three months and thirteen days. Oṃkāreśvara is a major stop.

🪨 Bāṇa-liṅgas

The river polishes stones in her bed into natural egg-shaped liṅgas, the Narmadeśvara or bāṇa-liṅgas, prized for home worship all over India. "Every pebble of the Narmadā is Śaṅkara," goes a saying.

🌙 Narmadāṣṭakam

The hymn traditionally ascribed to Śaṅkara, with its refrain tvadīya pāda-paṅkajaṃ namāmi devi narmade: "I bow to your lotus feet, goddess Narmadā."

🪔 The evening ārati

Each night at the ghats, lamps are waved and floated for the river, as at the Gaṅgā in Kāśī.

ZAPPY: Walking around a whole river? Without crossing it?

PROF. BOLT: That's the vow. Villages along the way feed the walkers. It's one of the last great foot pilgrimages in the world.

Pilgrims often pair Mahākāla and Oṃkāreśvara. Inside Oṃkāreśvara's temple, a Mahākāleśvara shrine sits on the floor above; at Ujjain, an Oṃkāreśvara shrine sits above Mahākāla. The two temples seem to answer each other.

The Narmadā valley: a river that is a goddess, and a dynasty that rebuilt India's temples from her banks.

OṂKĀREŚVARA IN CULTURE

A teacher, a queen and a valley of weavers

🕉️ Ādi Śaṅkara

Tradition places his discipleship here, under Govinda Bhagavatpāda, before he went on to Kāśī and across India, teaching Advaita: the oneness of the Self and Brahman.

👑 Ahilyābāī Holkar

The philosopher-queen of Mālwā, who rebuilt Oṃkāreśvara, Kāśī Viśvanātha (1780), Juna Somnāth (1783) and hundreds of ghats and shrines.

☬ Guru Nānak

Sikh tradition records a visit by Guru Nānak to Oṃkāreśvara on his travels; a gurdwara stands near the temple.

📜 The inscriptions

Amaleśvara's walls hold 11th-century Sanskrit poetry in stone: the Halāyudha-stotra, the Mahimna-stava and a hymn to the Narmadā.

🧵 Maheśvarī weaving

The light cotton-silk sārīs of nearby Maheśvara, a craft revived under Ahilyābāī and still woven in the valley.

🎶 Nimāṛ

The Nimāṛ region's language, Nimāṛī, its folk songs of the Narmadā and its Gaṇagaur festival colour the life of the town.
