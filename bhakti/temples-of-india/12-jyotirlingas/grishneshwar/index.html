THE 12 JYOTIRLIṄGAS · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Legends, history, rituals, festivals and the last of the twelve, beside Ellora · explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: A one-page summary first, then maps, the stotram line, the two places that claim it, the legend of Ghuśmā, a full history, the red-stone temple, the day's rituals, festivals, Ellora next door, the sacred places around, and the twelve together. Keep it as a reference.

FOREWORD

In the village of Verul, a short walk from the great rock-cut caves of Ellora, stands Ghṛṣṇeśvara, the twelfth and last of the jyotirliṅgas: the smallest of their temples, built of warm red stone.

Its story is the gentlest of the twelve. A devoted woman named Ghuśmā made earthen liṅgas every day and let them go in a lake. When her own son was killed out of jealousy, she did not stop her worship, and when Śiva restored him and offered to punish the killer, she asked him instead to forgive. Śiva stayed here in her name. Ghṛṣṇeśvara is often explained as "the Lord of compassion".

Here the Bhosale family, ancestors of Chhatrapati Śivājī, first restored the temple, and the Holkar queens of Indore gave it the form it has today.

This twelfth guide closes our journey through the stotram with its last line, "ghuśmeśaṃ ca śivālaye". May every reader one day complete the twelve.

WHERE IT IS

At Verul, beside the caves of Ellora

Verul is the village of Ellora, in the Khuldabad taluka of Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar (Aurangabad) district, Maharashtra, on the Deccan plateau.

The temple stands close to the Ellora Caves; the old fort of Devagiri (Daulatabad) and the shrines of Khuldabad lie on the same road.

A small stream, the Elagaṅgā, runs past the village and the caves.

IN THE JYOTIRLIṄGA STOTRAM

"And Ghuśmeśa at Śivālaya"
vārāṇasyāṃ tu viśveśaṃtryambakaṃ gautamītaṭe |himālaye tu kedāraṃghuśmeśaṃ ca śivālaye ‖
What it says

"Viśveśa in Vārāṇasī, Tryambaka on the bank of the Gautamī, Kedāra in the Himālaya, and Ghuśmeśa at Śivālaya."

WordMeans
ghuśmeśa"Lord of Ghuśmā", the devotee of the legend; later Ghṛṣṇeśa / Ghṛṣṇeśvara
ca"and": the last item of the list
śivālaye"at Śivālaya": a place name, read as the lake or village of the legend; literally "abode of Śiva"

The longer hymn. Verse twelve of the stotram traditionally ascribed to Ādi Śaṅkara places the Lord at Ilāpura, an old name of Ellora: ilāpure ramya-viśālake 'smin samullasantaṃ ca jagad-vareṇyam | vande maheśaṃ paramodāra-bhāvaṃ ghṛṣṇeśvarākhyaṃ śaraṇaṃ prapadye: "In lovely, spacious Ilāpura shines the one the world honours; I bow to the great Lord of supremely generous nature, called Ghṛṣṇeśvara, and take refuge in him."

The closing verse. The short stotram ends by promising that one who recites these twelve names morning and evening is freed from the sins of seven lives.

Twelfth and last in the stotram: Ghuśmeśa, Lord of a devotee's forgiveness.

TWO PLACES, ONE VERSE

Verul in Maharashtra and Shiwar in Rajasthan

Because the stotram names "Śivālaya" rather than a district, more than one temple can read itself in the verse. Most pilgrims and most lists identify the jyotirliṅga with Verul. A temple at Shiwar, in Sawai Madhopur district of Rajasthan, is held by its devotees to be the true twelfth.

THE LEGENDS · I

Ghuśmā, the lake and forgiveness

Two sisters. The Śiva Purāṇa tells of a learned Brahmin, Sudharmā, and his wife Sudehā, who had no child. Sudehā persuaded her husband to marry her younger sister, Ghuśmā, a devoted worshipper of Śiva.

The earthen liṅgas. Every day Ghuśmā made 101 liṅgas of clay, worshipped them, and let them go in a nearby lake. In time she had a son, and the family was happy.

Jealousy. But Sudehā grew jealous. One night she killed the young man and threw his body into the same lake.

Unbroken worship. When the household woke in grief, Ghuśmā did not stop. She made her 101 liṅgas and went to the lake as always. As she let the last one go, her son rose alive from the water.

The boon. Śiva appeared and wanted to punish Sudehā. Ghuśmā asked him to forgive her sister, and to stay there forever for the good of the world. Śiva agreed, and remained as Ghuśmeśvara, "the Lord of Ghuśmā".

🙏 Steadfastness

She kept her worship even on the day of her greatest loss.

🕊️ Forgiveness

Her first wish was mercy for the one who wronged her.

Of all twelve stories, this one ends not with a demon defeated but with a sister forgiven. That is why Ghṛṣṇeśvara is called the Lord of compassion.

THE LEGENDS · II

The names of the Lord

Ghuśmeśvara. The oldest form of the name, from the devotee Ghuśmā: "the Lord of Ghuśmā". Some tellings call her Kusumā, which gives Kusumeśvara.

Ghṛṣṇeśvara. The common modern name. It is often explained as "Lord of compassion" (from ghṛṇā, pity, compassion), fitting Ghuśmā's mercy.

Kuṅkumeśvara. A local legend says Pārvatī was rubbing (ghṛṣ) vermilion (kuṅkuma) in her palm with water from the stream, when a liṅga of light appeared in her hand. Śiva stayed there, and the liṅga was first called Kuṅkumeśvara; some say the sanctum was once kuṅkuma-red within.

Ilāpura and the Elagaṅgā. Ellora's old name, Elāpura or Ilāpura, is tied in local tradition to the Elagaṅgā stream and to tales of a king named Ela who was healed by its waters.

👩 Ghuśmeśa

From the devotee.

💗 Ghṛṣṇeśa

"Lord of compassion".

🔴 Kuṅkumeśa

From Pārvatī's vermilion.

The etymologies are traditional; scholars differ on how "Ghuśmeśa" became "Ghṛṣṇeśa". The King Ela tale is local folklore.

HISTORY · I

Elāpura to the Bhosales
Purāṇas The Śiva Purāṇa's Koṭirudra Saṃhitā tells Ghuśmā's story; the shrine is praised in other Purāṇas too.
6th–10th c. The Ellora caves are carved: Buddhist, Hindu and Jain. Elāpura is a great sacred centre of the Deccan.
8th c. The Rāṣṭrakūṭa king Kṛṣṇa I is credited with the Kailāsa temple (Cave 16), cut from a single rock: Śiva's mountain home, in stone.
12–13th c. The Yādavas of nearby Devagiri rule the region.
13–14th c. The temple is destroyed in the campaigns of the Delhi Sultanate, which took Devagiri and renamed it Daulatabad.
16–17th c. Mālojī Bhosale, grandfather of Chhatrapati Śivājī, restores the temple. Ellora was the Bhosales' jāgīr, and their ancestral house stood a few steps away. A stone inscription in the temple names Mālojī and Ekojī Bhosale.
Sources differ between the 16th and 17th century for the Bhosale restoration. The story that Mālojī found treasure in a snake's mound and gave it to the temple is traditional.

HISTORY · II

The Holkars to today
Mughal era The temple suffers again in the Mughal–Maratha wars.
c. 1730 Gautamābāī Holkar, wife of Malhārrāv Holkar, founder of the Holkar house of Indore, rebuilds the temple in its present form, according to several accounts.
18th c. Her daughter-in-law Ahilyābāī Holkar, who restored temples from Kāśī to Rāmeśvaram, renovates it; the Śivālaya tīrtha tank is credited to her (one account says 1791).
1983 The Ellora Caves are inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site; the temple lies close to the protected area.
Today The Bhosale vāḍā ruins nearby are protected by the state archaeology department.
2026 The Maharashtra government approves a revised development plan for the temple area of about ₹210 crore, with works planned around the temple in view of the Nashik–Trimbak Kumbh.

Two Maratha houses. The Bhosales, Śivājī's family, and the Holkars, the Indore line, both left their mark here. For the Bhosales it was home ground; for the Holkar queens, one of many great shrines they restored across India.

ZAPPY: Ahilyābāī again! Somanātha, Kāśī, Tryambaka…

THE TEMPLE TODAY

Red stone and a five-tiered spire

🪨 The liṅga

The jyotirliṅga sits in a small sanctum below the hall's level, facing east, within reach of the worshipper.

🔺 The spire

A spire of five tiers in red basalt; halfway up, the Daśāvatāra, Viṣṇu's ten incarnations, are carved.

🏛️ The hall

A court hall on carved stone pillars, with a Nandi facing the sanctum, and friezes of deities, dancers and scenes from the epics.

💧 Śivālaya

The stepped tank near the temple, identified with the lake of Ghuśmā's legend.

Descriptions of the style vary: some call it Hemāḍpanthī, others a Maratha temple with southern features.

ELLORA NEXT DOOR

Three faiths in one hillside

Along the hill above Verul, 34 caves were carved between about the 6th and 10th centuries: Buddhist monasteries and halls, Hindu temples, and Jain shrines, side by side. UNESCO inscribed them as a World Heritage Site in 1983.

The greatest is Cave 16, the Kailāsa: a complete Śiva temple, with hall, Nandi shrine, pillars and elephants, cut downward and inward from a single mass of rock. It is credited to the Rāṣṭrakūṭa king Kṛṣṇa I in the 8th century, and represents Mount Kailāsa, Śiva's home.

☸️ Buddhist

Caves 1–12, including the "Viśvakarma" chaitya hall.

🔱 Hindu

Caves 13–29, with Kailāsa, Rāvaṇa shaking the mountain, and Śiva's dance.

🕉️ Jain

Caves 30–34, including the Indra Sabhā.

ZAPPY: A whole temple carved out of one rock?

PROF. BOLT: Carved from the top down, with no second chances. And a few steps away, the small red temple of Ghuśmā's Lord, still in daily worship.

At Verul, the living jyotirliṅga and the rock-cut Kailāsa stand together.

AROUND THE TEMPLE

Sacred places near Verul

💧 Śivālaya tīrtha

The stepped tank of Ghuśmā's legend, near the temple.

🐘 Lakṣa Vināyaka

A large Gaṇeśa shrine in Verul, counted in local tradition among the 21 Gaṇeśa pīṭhas.

🏠 Bhosale vāḍā

Ruins of the ancestral home of the Bhosales, Śivājī's family, protected by the state.

🐒 Bhadra Māruti, Khuldabad

A temple where Hanumān is worshipped reclining, a rare form.

🕌 Khuldabad dargāhs

An old centre of Chishtī Sufi saints, revered by many for centuries.

🏰 Devagiri–Daulatabad

The hill fort of the Yādava kings, later Daulatabad, which shaped the region's history.

🪷 Paiṭhaṇ

On the Godāvarī, home of Sant Eknāth, in the same district.

🎨 Ajanta

The painted Buddhist caves, in the same district, a World Heritage Site.

Khuldabad also holds the graves of several Deccan rulers, including the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb.

GHṚṢṆEŚVARA IN CULTURE

Marathas, caves and a mother's faith

⚔️ The Bhosales

Ellora was their home ground: the temple ties the family of Chhatrapati Śivājī to the twelfth jyotirliṅga.

👑 The Holkar queens

Gautamābāī and Ahilyābāī: two women of one house who rebuilt and renewed it.

🪔 Ghuśmā's vow

Her clay liṅgas are a model of the humble pārthiva worship that anyone can do at home.

🗿 Ellora

Where the jyotirliṅga's simple faith meets the grandest rock-cut art of India.

🕌 Many faiths

Buddhist, Jain, Hindu and Sufi sites within a few miles: the Deccan's layered history.

🔁 The last stop

Many pilgrims make Ghṛṣṇeśvara the end of their round of the twelve, completing the vow.
