FOREWORD

Of all India's sacred places, few hold as much as Kāśī. It is one of the oldest living cities on earth, the city Śiva is said never to leave, the place where dying is believed to bring liberation, and home of Viśvanātha, the Lord of the universe, one of the twelve jyotirliṅgas.

This guide begins our series on the twelve jyotirliṅgas. It is meant to be kept and returned to: the legends, the long and often painful history of the temple, its rituals from Maṅgalā Ārati before dawn to Śayana Ārati at night, its festivals, the shrines and ghats around it, and practical help for anyone planning a visit.

May every reader one day stand on the steps of the Gaṅgā at Kāśī, and feel what millions have felt there before.

PROF. BOLT: The jyotirliṅga of the city Śiva never leaves. Every other Viśvanātha temple in India is named after this one.

WHERE IT IS

Kāśī between two rivers, on the curve of the Gaṅgā

Vārāṇasī takes its name from two streams: the Varuṇā to the north and the Asi to the south. The sacred city lies between them on the west bank of the Gaṅgā.

Kāśī, "the luminous", is its oldest name; Banaras is the everyday one; the Purāṇas also call it Avimukta, "never forsaken", and Ānandavana, "the forest of bliss".

Here the Gaṅgā bends and flows northward, back toward the Himālaya, an unusual turn that tradition counts as especially sacred.

IN THE JYOTIRLIṄGA STOTRAM

The line that names Viśveśvara
vārāṇasyāṃ tu viśveśaṃtryambakaṃ gautamī-taṭehimālaye tu kedāraṃghuśmeśaṃ ca śivālaye ‖
What it says

"…Viśveśa in Vārāṇasī, Tryambaka on the bank of the Gautamī (Godāvarī), Kedāra in the Himālaya, and Ghuśmeśa at Śivālaya."

WordMeans
vārāṇasyāmin Vārāṇasī
viśveśaLord of the universe (viśva + īśa), the same as Viśvanātha and Viśveśvara
jyotirliṅga"liṅga of light": a shrine where Śiva is believed to have revealed himself as a column of light

Why "jyoti". The idea goes back to the Liṅgodbhava story (see our Liṅgāṣṭakam article): Śiva appeared as an endless pillar of fire whose top and bottom neither Brahmā nor Viṣṇu could find. Each jyotirliṅga is honoured as a place where that light touched the earth.

Kāśī's special claim. The Kāśī Khaṇḍa of the Skanda Purāṇa describes Kāśī as the very spot where that light first pierced the earth, which is why the city is called "Kāśī", the shining one.

Ninth in the stotram, first in many hearts: Viśveśa, Lord of the universe, in Vārāṇasī.

THE LEGENDS · I

Why Śiva lives in Kāśī

🔱 On the trident

Tradition says Kāśī rests on the tip of Śiva's trident, above the earth. When the world dissolves at the end of an age, Kāśī alone is not destroyed.

🏠 Avimukta

"Never forsaken": Śiva and Pārvatī are said never to leave Kāśī. Even when King Divodāsa made the gods leave, Śiva longed to return, and did (see our Viśvanāthāṣṭakam article).

The city as Śiva's body. The Kāśī Khaṇḍa treats the whole city, not just the temple, as sacred: every lane has its liṅga, every ghat its story. Pilgrims speak of the "Kāśī kṣetra", the holy field, bounded by the route of the Pañcakrośī pilgrimage.

The saving word. The best-known belief about Kāśī: whoever dies here is liberated, because Śiva himself whispers the tāraka mantra, the "mantra that carries across", into the ear of the dying. For centuries, the elderly have come to Kāśī to spend their last days; some guesthouses ("mukti bhavans") exist for exactly this.

Maṇikarṇikā. One story tells that while Śiva and Pārvatī bathed in a pool Viṣṇu had dug with his discus, a jewelled earring (maṇi-karṇikā) fell into it. The pool and the ghat beside it took the name, and it became the city's most sacred cremation ground, where fires are said never to go out.

ZAPPY: Why do people want to die in Kāśī?

PROF. BOLT: Because of the promise: Śiva gives liberation here. For a believer, it turns the end of life into a homecoming.

THE LEGENDS · II

Annapūrṇā, the Sapta Ṛṣis, and the guardians

Annapūrṇā feeds Śiva. A well-loved story: Śiva once told Pārvatī that the world, food included, was illusion. To teach him otherwise, she disappeared, and with her all food vanished. Even Śiva grew hungry. She reappeared in Kāśī as Annapūrṇā, "full of food", and Śiva came to her with a begging bowl. Her temple stands just steps from Viśvanātha; tradition says no one in Kāśī goes hungry while she lives there. (See our Annapūrṇā Stotram article.)

The Saptarṣi Ārati. Each evening, the Saptarṣi Ārati is performed by priests representing the seven great sages, who are believed to come to Kāśī every evening to worship Viśvanātha.

The guardians. Kāla Bhairava is Kāśī's kotwāl, its police chief: tradition says pilgrims should visit him to seek leave to stay (see our Kāla Bhairavāṣṭakam article). Ḍhuṇḍhirāja Gaṇeśa, near the temple's main gate, is "the one who is searched for": Gaṇeśa stayed in Kāśī during Divodāsa's reign and was found here by Śiva.

Viśālākṣī. Kāśī is also a Śakti Pīṭha: Viśālākṣī, "the wide-eyed Goddess", is worshipped where tradition says Satī's earring fell.

HISTORY · I

Destroyed and rebuilt, again and again
Ancient Kāśī is named in Vedic and Buddhist texts; it was the capital of the Kāśī kingdom, one of the great states of the Buddha's time. The Buddha gave his first sermon at Sārnāth, just outside the city.
Purāṇas The Kāśī Khaṇḍa of the Skanda Purāṇa describes the city's liṅgas, ghats and pilgrim routes in great detail; Viśveśvara is its centre.
1194 The temple is destroyed when the army of Muḥammad of Ghor takes Vārāṇasī.
13th–16th c. The temple is rebuilt and destroyed again more than once in the following centuries.
1585 Rāja Ṭoḍar Mal, Akbar's minister, rebuilds the temple; orthodox Brahmins at first boycott the earlier rebuilding by Rāja Mān Singh.
1669 Emperor Aurangzeb orders the temple demolished; the Jñānavāpī (Gyanvapi) mosque is built on the site. Pilgrims continue to come to the area.
Historians differ on some dates and details of the earlier phases; this page follows the most widely cited accounts.

HISTORY · II

From Ahilyābāī to the Dhām
1780 Ahilyābāī Holkar, Maratha queen of Indore, builds the present temple on a site next to the mosque. (Some accounts give 1777 for the start.)
1785 A naubat-khānā (drum house) is built in front of the temple, on the instructions of Governor-General Warren Hastings.
1830s Mahārājā Ranjit Singh of the Sikh Empire gives about a tonne of gold to plate the temple's spires: hence "the Golden Temple of Vārāṇasī".
1841 Raghuji Bhonsle III of Nagpur gives silver to the temple.
1983 The Government of Uttar Pradesh takes over management of the temple.
2021 On 13 December, the Shri Kashi Vishwanath Dham corridor is opened: about 5 lakh sq ft, linking the temple to the Gaṅgā. Old houses were acquired and more than 40 ancient temples hidden inside them rediscovered.
2022 The inner sanctum is plated with gold given by an anonymous donor.

The Jñānavāpī question. The site of the Jñānavāpī mosque, next to the temple, is the subject of court cases that are still being heard. In July 2026, both the Hindu and the Muslim parties declined a Supreme Court mediation offer and asked for a court verdict instead. This article does not take a side; check current news for the latest.

THE TEMPLE TODAY

What you'll see inside

🛕 The sanctum

The jyotirliṅga sits in a silver altar, in a small sanctum (garbhagṛha). Devotees pour water and offer bilva leaves and flowers.

✨ Gold

Three spires/domes; the main śikhara and dome are plated in gold, gleaming above the old city.

💧 Jñānavāpī

"The well of wisdom", a sacred well; tradition says Śiva dug it to cool the liṅga with its water.

🌊 The Dhām

A broad path and plazas run from the temple down to the Gaṅgā at Lalitā and Jalāsen ghats, with pilgrim facilities, a museum and restored shrines.

The architecture is in the North Indian nāgara style, with a curving śikhara. The temple itself is modest in size; its power is in its age and meaning.

Between the āratis. The sanctum is open for darśan; pilgrims offer Gaṅgā water, milk, bilva and flowers, and at set times may touch the liṅga (sparśa darśan).

Festival days. On Mahāśivarātri the temple stays open through the night, and the day's pattern of āratis changes.

Steps from Viśvanātha; the Mother who feeds Kāśī. Free meals (prasād) are served.

The city's guardian. Traditionally visited at the start of a Kāśī pilgrimage.

🐘 Ḍhuṇḍhirāja Gaṇeśa

Śakti Pīṭha of the wide-eyed Goddess, near Mīr Ghāṭ.

🐒 Saṅkaṭa Mocana

Hanumān temple traditionally linked with Tulasīdāsa, in the south of the city.

The famous red Durgā temple beside a large tank.

☸️ Sārnāth

Just outside the city: the Buddha's first sermon, the Dhamek Stūpa and the Aśokan lion capital, now in the museum.

Banaras Hindu University (1916) has its own large Viśvanātha temple on campus.

ZAPPY: Which ghat should I see first?

PROF. BOLT: Dawn boat from Assi to Maṇikarṇikā, then evening ārati at Daśāśvamedha. That's Kāśī in one day.

THE PAÑCAKROŚĪ YĀTRĀ

Walking the boundary of the holy city

The Pañcakrośī Yātrā is a walking pilgrimage around the sacred boundary of Kāśī, said to be one of the oldest pilgrim routes in the world. It starts and ends at Maṇikarṇikā and covers about 25 kos, roughly 88 km, traditionally over five days on foot, with a night at each halt.

The five halts: Kardameśvara · Bhīmacaṇḍī · Rāmeśvara (on the Varuṇā) · Śivapur (Pāñco Pāṇḍava) · Kapiladhārā. Along the way, pilgrims visit over a hundred shrines.

Why walk it? Tradition holds that walking the boundary is like walking around Śiva himself, and that the whole kṣetra inside is his body.

KĀŚĪ IN CULTURE

Saints, poets, musicians

🕉️ Ādi Śaṅkara

Taught in Kāśī. Tradition says an outcaste (caṇḍāla) met him in a lane and asked: what do you want to move away from, the body or the Self? Śaṅkara bowed and wrote the Manīṣā Pañcakam.

📖 Tulasīdāsa

Wrote much of the Rāmacaritamānas at Tulsī Ghāṭ, including the Rudrāṣṭakam (see our article).

🧵 Kabīr & Ravidās

The weaver-poet Kabīr and the saint Ravidās both lived in Kāśī; their verses are still sung across India.

🎵 Music

Home of the Banaras gharānā and of shehnāī maestro Bismillah Khan, who played at Viśvanātha temple and at the ghats.

🧣 Banarasi silk

Famous brocade sārīs, woven in the city's lanes for centuries.

🎓 Learning

A centre of Sanskrit learning for millennia; Banaras Hindu University was founded in 1916.
