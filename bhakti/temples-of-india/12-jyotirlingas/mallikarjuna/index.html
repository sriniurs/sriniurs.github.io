FOREWORD

Deep in the Nallamala forest, on a hill above the gorge of the Kṛṣṇā, stands a temple where Śiva and Śakti are worshipped side by side: Mallikārjuna, one of the twelve jyotirliṅgas, and Bhramarāmbā, one of the great Śakti Pīṭhas.

Śrīśaila, "the auspicious mountain", is a place parents come to, because the story says that is why Śiva and Pārvatī came here: to be near a son who had left home. It is also a place of walkers. Every Ugādi, lakhs of pilgrims from Karnataka and Maharashtra walk through tiger forest to visit, as they put it, their daughter and son-in-law.

This third guide in our series follows the second name in the stotram: the legends of Kārttikeya and the bee goddess, kings from the Sātavāhanas to Śivājī, the darśan where you may touch the liṅga, and the four gateways through the forest.

May every reader one day stand on Śikhareśvaram and see the gopura of Śrīśaila far below.

WHERE IT IS

A hill in the forest, a river in the gorge

Śrīśaila, "the auspicious mountain", rises in the Nallamala hills of the Eastern Ghats, in Nandyal district of Andhra Pradesh.

Below the temple town, the Kṛṣṇā runs through a deep gorge; here it is called Pātālagaṅgā, "the Gaṅgā of the underworld". Upstream, the river is held back by the great Śrīśailam dam.

All around is the Nāgārjunasāgar–Śrīśailam Tiger Reserve; the last stretch of road is a forest ghat road with gates that close at night.

IN THE JYOTIRLIṄGA STOTRAM

The second name in the twelve
saurāṣṭre somanāthaṃ caśrīśaile mallikārjunam |ujjayinyāṃ mahākālamoṅkāram amaleśvaram ‖
What it says

"Somanātha in Saurāṣṭra, Mallikārjuna on Śrīśaila, Mahākāla in Ujjayinī, and at Oṅkāra, Amaleśvara."

WordMeans
śrīśaileon Śrīśaila, "the auspicious (or glorious) mountain"
mallikājasmine; in one reading, a name for Pārvatī
arjuna"bright, white"; here a name of Śiva (also the arjuna tree)

The longer hymn. Verse two of the twelve-verse stotram traditionally ascribed to Ādi Śaṅkara: śrīśaila-śṛṅge vibudhāti-saṅge tulādi-tuṅge 'pi mudā vasantam | tam arjunaṃ mallika-pūrvam ekaṃ namāmi saṃsāra-samudra-setum: "I bow to the One, Mallikārjuna, who lives joyfully on the lofty peak of Śrīśaila, thronged by the gods: the bridge across the ocean of worldly life."

Two ways to read the name. Mallikā + Arjuna = Pārvatī and Śiva together. Or: Arjuna (Śiva) worshipped with mallikā, jasmine. Both readings are old, and both are loved.

Second in the stotram: Mallikārjuna on Śrīśaila, the jasmine-white Lord of the auspicious mountain.

THE LEGENDS · I

The son who left home

The race. Śiva and Pārvatī's sons, Gaṇeśa and Kārttikeya (Skanda, Kumāra), each wanted to marry first. The parents set a contest: whoever went round the world first would win. Kārttikeya set off on his peacock. Gaṇeśa simply walked around his parents, saying they were his world, and won.

The hurt. When Kārttikeya returned and found his brother married, he was deeply hurt. He left Kailāsa and went south to the Krauñca mountain, to live alone.

The parents follow. Śiva and Pārvatī could not bear the separation. They came south to be near him, and settled on Śrīśaila as Mallikārjuna and Bhramarāmbā. The Śiva Purāṇa (Koṭirudra Saṃhitā) says they still visit their son: Śiva on new-moon days, Pārvatī on full-moon days.

The jasmine princess. Another story tells of a princess, Candrāvatī, who left her palace for this forest and worshipped a liṅga she found here with garlands of mallikā (jasmine). Pleased, Śiva took the name Mallikārjuna.

🦚 Kārttikeya

The son who walked away; in the south he is beloved as Murugan, Subrahmaṇya, Kumāra.

🌼 Mallikā

Jasmine, the flower of this temple: white, fragrant, offered to Śiva here above all.

Gaṇeśa's answer is the one every child learns: your parents are your world. Kārttikeya's story adds the other half: when a child leaves, love follows.

ZAPPY: So Śiva moved house for his son?

PROF. BOLT: That's the story. Ask any parent whose child lives far away: they understand Śrīśaila at once.

THE LEGENDS · II

The bee goddess, the forest people, the summit

Bhramarāmbā. The demon Aruṇāsura won a boon that no two-legged or four-legged creature could kill him. The Goddess took a form he had not thought of: a vast swarm of bees (bhramara), six-legged, that destroyed him. She stayed on Śrīśaila as Bhramarāmbā, "the Mother of bees". Tradition says a faint humming can be heard behind her shrine.

A Śakti Pīṭha. In the story of Satī's body falling across India, Śrīśaila is where a part fell: usually named as the neck, in some lists the upper lip. Ādi Śaṅkara's list of eighteen great pīṭhas includes "śrīśaile bhramarāmbikā".

Sākṣi Gaṇapati. On the road up, Gaṇeśa sits with a pen and a book. He is the "witness": he notes the name of every pilgrim, so that Śiva knows who came. Pilgrims stop to tell him their name.

Śikhareśvaram. From this high point the temple's tower can be seen far below. An old saying: śikharaṃ dṛṣṭvā punarjanma na vidyate, "whoever sees the śikhara is not born again".

The forest people. The Cheñcu, the forest-dwelling community of the Nallamala, have their own close tie to the god; a well-loved local tradition calls him "Cheñcu Mallayya", one of their own.

HISTORY · I

Śrīparvata of the ancients
Prehistory Stone tools in the Nallamala show people lived in these hills tens of thousands of years ago.
Epics As Śrīparvata, the hill is praised as a tīrtha in the Mahābhārata and the Purāṇas; Buddhist and other traditions also knew a "Śrīparvata" in this region.
c. 1st c. The temple's own history traces the first mention of the hill to a Sātavāhana inscription. The Ikṣvākus of the Kṛṣṇā valley followed.
375–612 The Viṣṇukuṇḍin kings worship "Śrīparvatasvāmī", the same Lord, naming him in their inscriptions.
7th–8th c. The Chalukyas leave their mark in inscriptions and architecture; the four "gateway" shrines of Śrīśaila are traceable from the 8th–9th centuries.
Kākatīyas Under the Kākatīyas of Warangal, Mailamadevī, sister of King Gaṇapatideva, is credited with the vimāna over the sanctum; in 1313 Pratāparudra confirms gifts of 70 villages to the temple.
Tevāram The Tamil Nāyanmār saints sing of Śrīśaila ("Tiruppaṛuppatam"), making it one of the 276 Pāḍal Peṭra Sthalams.
Early dates come mostly from the temple's published history and later inscriptions; the origin of the shrine is not documented.

HISTORY · II

Reḍḍis, Vijayanagara, Śivājī and the dam
1325–1448 The Reḍḍi kings of Kondavīḍu: called the temple's golden age. Prolaya Vema Reḍḍi builds a stepped path up to Śrīśaila and down to Pātālagaṅgā.
1377 Anavema Reḍḍi builds the Vīraśiro Maṇḍapa in the temple, recorded in an inscription dated Śaka 1299.
Vijayanagara Harihara II builds the mukha maṇḍapa and a southern gopura.
1511–16 Kṛṣṇadevarāya's reign: his inscriptions mention several maṭhas here; the eastern rājagopura and the pillared sālu maṇḍapas are credited to him.
1677 Chhatrapati Śivājī visits during his southern campaign (some accounts say 1674) and builds the northern gopura, still called the Śivājī Gopuram. A memorial meditation hall in the town recalls the visit.
20th c. The temple comes under the state's Endowments administration.
1981 The Śrīśailam dam on the Kṛṣṇā is completed; its reservoir submerges some old shrines, including Saṅgameśvaram at the Kṛṣṇā–Tuṅgabhadrā confluence (visible again when the water falls).
2022 Śrīśailam moves from Kurnool district to the new Nandyal district.

Śivājī and the Goddess. Maratha tradition holds that at Śrīśaila Śivājī, in a moment of deep feeling, was ready to give up his life before the Goddess, and that Bhramarāmbā gave him a sword and her blessing instead. It is a story, told with love in Maharashtra; the gopura is the record.

THE TEMPLE TODAY

What you'll see inside the walls

🛕 Mallikārjuna

The jyotirliṅga sits in a small, old sanctum facing east, with a Nandi in front and the mukha maṇḍapa between.

🐝 Bhramarāmbā

The Goddess's shrine stands on a higher level at the back of the complex: pilgrims visit both.

🧱 The walls

High prākāra walls are covered with carvings: forms of Śiva, scenes from the epics, elephants, hunters and daily life.

🌳 Vṛddha Mallikārjuna

"The old Mallikārjuna": a small shrine with a weathered liṅga that tradition counts among the oldest in the complex.

The complex is Dravidian in style, built in layers from the Kākatīya to the Vijayanagara period, with four gopuras, one on each side. Exact positions of minor shrines in the diagram are simplified.

Four ways in. Free general (sarva) darśan; paid quick darśan in two price bands (one gives only alaṅkāra darśan, seeing the decorated liṅga from a little distance); and sparśa darśan, where you go right up to the liṅga and touch it. Sparśa is offered in paid slots, and also free, by token, on some weekday afternoons; it is suspended on big festival days.

Check before you go. Slots and prices have changed several times since 2024; book and confirm on srisailadevasthanam.org.

AROUND THE TEMPLE

Shrines and sights on the hill

✍️ Sākṣi Gaṇapati

On the road up to the temple: pilgrims tell Gaṇeśa their name, so the visit is "recorded".

🌊 Pātālagaṅgā

The bathing ghat on the Kṛṣṇā, far below the temple: hundreds of steps down, or the ropeway.

⛰️ Śikhareśvaram

About 8 km south, the highest point: a Śiva shrine and the famous view of the temple tower.

🔱 Hāṭakeśvaram

A Śiva shrine in the forest south of the temple, linked by tradition with the gold (hāṭaka) liṅga.

💧 Phāladhāra Pañcadhāra

Springs in a quiet glen where, tradition says, Ādi Śaṅkara meditated; a small shrine marks the spot.

🪔 Akka Mahādevī caves

Natural caves on the river, reached by boat, where the 12th-century poet-saint is said to have lived.

🌿 Iṣṭakāmeśvarī

A small forest shrine of the Goddess deep in the Nallamala; by custom, pilgrims visit her after Mallikārjuna and Bhramarāmbā.

🏞️ Dam and museum

The Śrīśailam dam (dramatic when the gates open after the monsoon) and the Cheñcu Lakṣmī tribal museum.

THE FOUR GATEWAYS

How pilgrims entered the holy forest

Tradition and inscriptions describe Śrīśaila as reached through four main gateways (dvāra) on the plains, one in each direction, a pattern traceable to the 8th–9th centuries. Each grew into a pilgrim centre of its own, and pilgrims would start their forest walk from one of them.

Four secondary gateways lie at the corners; two of them, Saṅgameśvaram and Eleśvaram, are now under reservoirs.

Bhramarāmbā at Śrīśaila and Jogulāmbā at Ālampur are both among the eighteen Mahā Śakti Pīṭhas; pilgrims often pair them.

ZAPPY: Which one would you add first?

PROF. BOLT: Mahānandi, for its water. Ahobilam if your knees are strong: it's a walk in the forest, like Śrīśaila once was.

ŚRĪŚAILA IN CULTURE

Saints, poets and pilgrims on foot

🌸 Akka Mahādevī

The 12th-century Kannada poet-saint of the Vīraśaiva movement, devoted to Śiva as "Chennamallikārjuna", her beautiful Lord of jasmine. Tradition says her final years were spent in the caves of Śrīśaila.

🧘 Allama Prabhu

The great Vīraśaiva mystic is said to have ended his days at Śrīśaila too; his vacanas are still sung in Karnataka.

🕉️ Ādi Śaṅkara

Tradition says Śaṅkara stayed here and composed the Śivānandalaharī; the Bhramarāmbāṣṭakam is also ascribed to him.

🎶 Tevāram

Tamil Nāyanmār saints sang of Śrīśaila as "Tiruppaṛuppatam", making it a revered shrine for Tamil Śaivas too.

🚶 The Ugādi walkers

From Bidar, Kalaburagi, Belagavi and Maharashtra, lakhs walk for days with kāvaḍis and drums: a living tie between Karnataka and this Andhra hill.

🌿 The Cheñcus

The forest people of the Nallamala, whose life, crafts and bond with the god are shown in the town's tribal museum.

ZAPPY: Why do Kannada pilgrims call him son-in-law?

PROF. BOLT: They see the Goddess as a daughter of their land. So every Ugādi, the family goes to visit the newly-weds.
