BHĪMAŚAṄKARA

THE 12 JYOTIRLIṄGAS · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Legends, history, rituals, festivals and the forest where the Bhīmā is born · explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: A one-page summary first, then maps, the stotram line and the question of Ḍākinī, the legends, a full history, the temple today, rituals and festivals, the shrines around, the forest and its giant squirrels, the river Bhīmā, and the culture of the Sahyādri. Keep it as a reference.

FOREWORD

High in the Sahyādri, where monsoon clouds sit on the ridges for months and the forest drips with rain, a small black-stone temple stands at the head of a valley. A spring rises nearby; it becomes the Bhīmā, one of the great rivers of the Deccan. This is Bhīmaśaṅkara, the sixth jyotirliṅga.

Its story is of Śiva the protector, who burst from a humble clay liṅga to destroy a demon and save a captive king. Its history is Maratha: Śivājī's gift, a Peshwa minister's hall, and a Portuguese church bell brought home as a prize of war. And around it, a protected forest shelters the shy giant squirrel, Maharashtra's state animal.

This seventh guide in our series follows the sixth name in the stotram, including the question of where "Ḍākinī" lies, which has been answered both in the Sahyādri and in Assam.

May every reader one day walk up through the mist to Bhīmaśaṅkara, and hear its great bell ring out over the valley.

WHERE IT IS

At the head of a valley in the Sahyādri

Bhīmāśaṅkar stands at about 1,000 m on the crest of the Sahyādri, the Western Ghats, in the north-west of Pune district. To the west the hills drop steeply to the Konkan; to the east the land slopes gently down to the Deccan plateau.

The Bhīmā rises here from a spring and flows east across Maharashtra and Karnataka to join the Kṛṣṇā. On the way it passes Pandharpur, home of Viṭṭhala.

The area receives heavy monsoon rain; the temple is often wrapped in cloud from June to September.

IN THE JYOTIRLIṄGA STOTRAM

The sixth name: Bhīmaśaṅkara in Ḍākinī
paralyāṃ vaidyanāthaṃ caḍākinyāṃ bhīmaśaṅkaram |setubandhe tu rāmeśaṃnāgeśaṃ dārukāvane ‖
What it says

"Vaidyanātha at Paralī, Bhīmaśaṅkara in Ḍākinī, Rāmeśa at Setubandha, and Nāgeśa in the Dāruka forest."

WordMeans
ḍākinyāmin Ḍākinī: a place name; Ḍākinī is also the name of a class of goddess-attendants
bhīma"the terrible": the demon of the story; also the river
śaṅkara"the giver of good": a name of Śiva

The longer hymn. Verse six of the stotram traditionally ascribed to Ādi Śaṅkara places the Lord "in the Ḍākinī-Śākinī region, in the beautiful Sahya mountains, who gives the river Bhīmā": yaṃ ḍākinī-śākinikā-samāje niṣevyamāṇaṃ piśitāśanaiś ca … sadaiva bhīmādi-pada-prasiddhaṃ taṃ śaṅkaraṃ bhakta-hitaṃ namāmi, "I bow to that Śaṅkara, always known as Bhīma, kind to his devotees."

Ḍākinī and Śākinī. Local tradition says the goddess-attendants Ḍākinī and Śākinī accompanied Śiva here, and the forest took the name. Maharashtra's devotees read the "Sahya" and "Bhīmā" of the longer hymn as fixing the site in the Sahyādri.

Sixth in the stotram: Bhīmaśaṅkara, the protector who rose from a clay liṅga, where the Bhīmā is born.

WHERE IS ḌĀKINĪ?

The Sahyādri and Assam, side by side
Bhīmāśaṅkar, Sahyādri (Maharashtra)Bhīmeśvar Dhām, Ḍākinī hill (Assam)
TextThe longer stotram's "Sahya" mountains and the river "Bhīmā"The Śiva Purāṇa story is set in Kāmarūpa; a hill near Guwahati is called Ḍākinī (Ḍāinī) pahāṛ
SettingSource of the Bhīmā river; Ḍākinī-Śākinī forest traditionA forest stream at Pamohi, beside the Dīpar Bīl lake
TempleStone temple from about the 13th century, with Maratha additionsA Śiva shrine at a stream, worshipped locally as Bhīmaśaṅkara
Widely listed asThe sixth jyotirliṅga in most listsPromoted as the sixth jyotirliṅga by its devotees and, in 2023, by the Assam government

2023. In February 2023 a Mahāśivarātri advertisement by Assam's tourism department described Ḍākinī hill in Kamrup as "the sixth Jyotirlinga site". Leaders in Maharashtra objected strongly. Assam's Chief Minister replied that the state was not shifting any shrine, but expressing its own belief based on the Śiva Purāṇa. No official body has ruled on the question.

Other names. Some devotees also connect the jyotirliṅga with other shrines elsewhere in India; these claims are less widely known.

This article takes no side. Most traditional lists, and most pilgrims, name the Sahyādri temple; Assam's devotees hold their own belief.

ZAPPY: How can the same story point to two places?

PROF. BOLT: The Purāṇa says Kāmarūpa; the hymn says Sahya and Bhīmā. Read one closely and you go east; read the other and you go west.

THE LEGENDS · I

Bhīma and the clay liṅga

Kumbhakarṇa's son. The Koṭirudra Saṃhitā of the Śiva Purāṇa tells of Bhīma, son of Rāvaṇa's brother Kumbhakarṇa and the demoness Karkaṭī, raised in the forest by his mother. When he learned that Rāma, Viṣṇu on earth, had killed his father, he swore revenge on the gods.

The boon. After long austerities, Bhīma won great strength from Brahmā. He defeated Indra and the gods, and attacked the righteous king Sudakṣiṇa of Kāmarūpa, a devotee of Śiva, throwing him in chains.

The prisoner's liṅga. In prison, the king made a liṅga of clay and kept up his worship with his queen. When guards reported it, Bhīma stormed in and raised his sword to smash the liṅga.

The Lord rises. As the blade fell, Śiva burst out of the clay liṅga and blocked it. With a roar he burned Bhīma and his army to ash. The gods and sages begged him to remain; he did, as Bhīmaśaṅkara.

🪨 A clay liṅga

Not a grand temple: a prisoner's handful of earth. The story says sincere worship is enough to call Śiva.

🛡️ The protector

Bhīmaśaṅkara is prayed to for protection from enemies and from fear.

The demon attacked the liṅga, but it was the devotee's faith he was really trying to break. The Lord answered from inside the very thing being struck.

ZAPPY: This sounds like Mahākāla's story!

PROF. BOLT: Well spotted. Several jyotirliṅgas share the shape: a devotee in danger, a demon, and Śiva rising from the earth.

THE LEGENDS · II

Tripura, Kamalajā and the birth of a river

The three cities. Maharashtra's local tradition links Bhīmaśaṅkara with the great battle against Tripurāsura, lord of three flying cities. After destroying them with a single arrow, Śiva, as Tripurāri, rested here on the Sahyādri.

The sweat of battle. The sweat that poured from Śiva's body after the battle flowed down the mountain and became the river Bhīmarathī, the Bhīmā. Pilgrims see the river itself as Śiva's gift.

Kamalajā. Pārvatī is said to have helped Śiva in the war against Tripura; she is worshipped near the temple as Kamalajā Devī.

Ḍākinī and Śākinī. The goddess-attendants who came with Śiva and gave the forest its old name are also remembered in local worship.

Two in one. Tradition says the liṅga here is half Śiva and half Pārvatī, an Ardhanārīśvara form, with a faint line dividing it.

🏹 Tripurāri

Destroyer of the three cities.

💧 Bhīmarathī

A river from Śiva's sweat.

🌸 Kamalajā

Pārvatī, the helper.

This Tripura story and the Bhīma story are separate traditions; both are told at Bhīmaśaṅkara, and versions differ.

HISTORY · I

From stone temple to Maratha shrine
Antiquity The source of the Bhīmā has long been a holy place; the forest groves around it were protected by local custom as sacred (devrāī).
13th c. The core of the present temple, built of black basalt in the Nāgara manner with Deccan (Hemāḍpantī-style) features, is dated by inscriptions to about this century.
13th c. Tradition says the poet-saint Jñāneśvar visited Bhīmaśaṅkara, as he visited Tryambakeśvara.
17th c. Chhatrapati Śivājī grants the village of Kharośī to the temple to pay for its worship; the Marathas keep up the shrine as their power grows.
Feb–May 1739 The Marathas under Cimājī Appā, brother of Peshwā Bājīrāv I, take Vasai from the Portuguese. Cimājī collects five large church bells as trophies and gives them to temples; one hangs at Bhīmaśaṅkara.
18th c. Nānā Phaḍṇavīs, the Peshwās' great minister, builds the sabhāmaṇḍapa (assembly hall) and the śikhara.
The temple's early date rests on inscriptions as reported by the temple and secondary sources; Jñāneśvar's visit is traditional.

HISTORY · II

Sanctuary, controversy and renewal
1985 The forest around the temple becomes the Bhīmāśaṅkar Wildlife Sanctuary, chiefly to protect the Indian giant squirrel.
20th c. The temple is managed by a trust; the hill becomes a favourite of trekkers, who climb up from the Konkan side by old footpaths.
Feb 2023 Assam's tourism advertisement names Ḍākinī hill near Guwahati as the sixth jyotirliṅga, sparking a public debate (see page 4).
2025 Maharashtra approves a development plan of about ₹288 crore for Bhīmaśaṅkar, to manage the crowds expected around the Nashik–Tryambakeśvar Siṃhastha Kumbh of 2027.
2026 The temple is closed to devotees from 9 January (including Mahāśivarātri) for redevelopment of the hall and surroundings; daily rituals continue inside. It reopens in June 2026.
Now Plans include a ropeway from Khāṇḍas; the sanctuary's giant squirrel count has risen, from about 1,800 in 2021 to about 2,340, according to officials.

Why the Kumbh matters here. The Kumbh at Nashik draws pilgrims to Tryambakeśvar, another jyotirliṅga; many then travel on to Bhīmaśaṅkara. The plan is meant to prepare the narrow hilltop for far larger numbers.

Reports give slightly different reopening dates in June 2026.

THE TEMPLE TODAY

Black stone in a green valley

⬇️ The sanctum

The garbhagṛha lies lower than the hall, so pilgrims step down to the liṅga, which is set low in the floor and often covered with flowers and a silver mask.

🗿 The carvings

Walls and pillars of dark basalt carry images of deities, scenes from the epics and the Daśāvatāra, worn by centuries of monsoon rain.

🔔 The Vasai bell

Between two pillars before the Śani shrine hangs the great bell: a Portuguese church bell cast with Mary and the infant Jesus, given by Cimājī Appā in 1739.

🏛️ The hall

Nānā Phaḍṇavīs's 18th-century sabhāmaṇḍapa, renovated in the 2026 works, with the temple's spire above.

The temple is modest in size, set into the slope below the village, so one walks down to it: the reverse of most hill shrines.

A DAY AT THE TEMPLE

The rhythm of worship
RitualWhenWhat happens
Kākaḍ āratiBefore dawnThe waking ārati, sung in the Maharashtrian manner as the doors open.
AbhiṣekaMorningThe liṅga is bathed with water, milk, curd, honey and ghee; devotees offer bilva leaves.
NaivedyaMiddayThe food offering, followed by madhyāhna ārati.
Śṛṅgāra darśanAfternoonThe liṅga is adorned with a silver mask (mukhavaṭā), flowers and ornaments; abhiṣeka pauses.
Sāyam āratiEveningThe evening ārati with bells, and the Vasai bell rung in the courtyard.
ŚejāratiNightThe bedtime ārati; the temple closes.

Mukhavaṭā. As at many Maharashtra Śiva temples, the bare liṅga is covered for part of the day with a silver face of Śiva, turning the abstract into the personal. Pilgrims often see both forms in a single visit.

Monsoon worship. In Śrāvaṇa the rain and cloud are at their height; worship goes on in the mist, with the sound of streams all around.

Exact hours change with season and festivals; they are not given here.

ZAPPY: Why is Śrāvaṇa so busy if it rains so much?

PROF. BOLT: Because it's Śiva's month, and because the hill is never more beautiful. Clouds below, waterfalls everywhere. Devotion and wonder together.

Dates follow the Hindu lunar calendar and change each year; check a pañcāṅga.

AROUND THE TEMPLE

Shrines and sacred spots on the hill

💧 Gupta Bhīmaśaṅkara

A short forest walk away: where the Bhīmā is said to rise, with a small liṅga in the stream bed.

🌸 Kamalajā Devī

The shrine of Pārvatī as Kamalajā, Śiva's helper in the war against Tripura.

✍️ Sākṣi Vināyaka

Gaṇeśa the witness, who records the pilgrim's visit, as at Śrīśaila.

🐒 Hanumān lake

A small lake in the forest with a Hanumān shrine.

🐍 Nāgphaṇī

A rock outcrop shaped like a cobra's hood, at the edge of the escarpment, looking down to the Konkan.

🙏 Mokṣa kuṇḍa

A sacred tank associated by tradition with the sage Kauśika.

🪐 Śani shrine

In the temple precinct, beside the Vasai bell.

🌳 Devrāīs

Sacred groves, forest patches protected for generations by village custom.

Some of these lie inside the wildlife sanctuary, where forest rules apply.

THE FOREST AND THE ŚEKHARŪ

A jyotirliṅga in a wildlife sanctuary

The Bhīmāśaṅkar Wildlife Sanctuary, set up in 1985, protects a stretch of evergreen and semi-evergreen forest on the crest of the Western Ghats, one of the world's great biodiversity regions.

Its star is the Indian giant squirrel (Ratufa indica), locally śekharū: a squirrel as long as a cat, with a deep maroon-brown back and a long bushy tail, that leaps between tall trees and builds big leafy nests. It is Maharashtra's state animal. Officials report its numbers here rising, to about 2,340.

🌧️ Monsoon forest

Very heavy rain, mist and moss: a cloud forest for half the year.

🦋 Small wonders

Birds, butterflies, and fireflies that light up the trees just before the monsoon.

🌳 Sacred groves

Devrāīs kept by villagers out of reverence: ecology by devotion.

The Lord who protects the captive king now presides over a protected forest. Here, guarding the temple and guarding the trees are the same act.

THE RIVER BHĪMĀ

From Śiva's sweat to Viṭṭhala's feet

The Bhīmā rises at Bhīmaśaṅkara and runs east across the Deccan for several hundred kilometres before joining the Kṛṣṇā in Karnataka. The Purāṇas call it Bhīmarathī; in tradition it carries the sweat of Śiva's battle with Tripura.

Pandharpur. Halfway along, at Pandharpur, the river bends in a crescent and is called Candrabhāgā. On its bank stands the temple of Viṭṭhala (Viṭhobā), heart of the Vārkarī tradition, whose pilgrims walk there each Āṣāḍhī Ekādaśī singing the songs of Jñāneśvar and Tukārām.

🔱 Hari and Hara

Śiva at the source, Viṣṇu as Viṭṭhala midstream: one river joins two great traditions of Maharashtra.

🪷 The Vārī

The Vārkarī walk to Pandharpur, like the kāṃvaṛ to Deoghar, is a pilgrimage made on foot, in song, by lakhs.

ZAPPY: So Viṭṭhala's river starts at a Śiva temple?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. The saints of Maharashtra never saw a wall between the two. Neither does the river.

BHĪMAŚAṄKARA IN CULTURE

Saints, Marathas and trekkers

🪶 Jñāneśvar

The 13th-century poet-saint, who wrote the Jñāneśvarī on the Gītā in Marathi, is said by tradition to have visited Bhīmaśaṅkara.

🗡️ Śivājī

The founder of the Maratha state endowed the temple; his birthplace, Śivneri fort, lies in the same district, at Junnar.

⚔️ Cimājī Appā

The general who won Vasai from the Portuguese in 1739. His bells hang in several Maharashtra temples: a lasting souvenir of that campaign.

🏛️ Nānā Phaḍṇavīs

The shrewd minister who held the Maratha state together in the late 18th century, and gave Bhīmaśaṅkara its hall and spire.

🥾 The trek

Old footpaths, the Gaṇeś ghāṭ and the Śiḍī ghāṭ, climb steeply from Khāṇḍas in the Konkan. For generations, devotees walked up; today trekkers do too, especially in the rains.

🏞️ The Sahyādri circuit

Nearby: the Aṣṭavināyaka Gaṇeśas of Ojhar and Leṇyādri, Khaṇḍobā of Jejurī, and Pune itself.

ZAPPY: Śivājī, the Peshwās, Jñāneśvar... this temple is all Maharashtra!

PROF. BOLT: Which is why the 2023 debate touched such a nerve. For Maharashtrians, Bhīmaśaṅkara is part of who they are.
