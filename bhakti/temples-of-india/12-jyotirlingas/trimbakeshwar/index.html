FOREWORD

At the foot of Brahmagiri, a flat-topped hill in the Sahyādri near Nashik, the Godāvarī begins her long journey across India to the Bay of Bengal. Beside her source stands Tryambakeśvara, the tenth jyotirliṅga, a black-stone temple of the Peshwās.

It is unlike any other jyotirliṅga. In the sanctum there is no tall liṅga, only a hollow in the floor holding three tiny stone forms, which tradition honours as Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva: the whole Trinity in one place. Here the sage Gautama is said to have brought the Gaṅgā down to earth as the Godāvarī; here the Nāth saint Nivṛttināth lies in samādhi; and here, every twelve years, the Śaiva ascetics of India bathe in the Siṃhastha Kumbh. The Kumbh of 2026–28 opened here on 31 October 2026.

This tenth guide in our series follows the line "tryambakaṃ gautamītaṭe": its legends, its history, its rituals for the living and the dead, and the river it gives to the south.

May every reader one day stand at Kuśāvarta and see the Godāvarī begin.

ZAPPY: Brahmā and Viṣṇu inside a jyotirliṅga?

PROF. BOLT: Only here. Every other jyotirliṅga is Śiva alone; at Tryambakeśvara, tradition sees all three gods of the Trinity together.

WHERE IT IS

Under Brahmagiri, where the Godāvarī begins

Trimbak is a small temple town in the Sahyādri, west of the city of Nashik in Maharashtra, ringed by three hills: Brahmagiri, Nīlagiri and Kālagiri.

The Godāvarī rises on Brahmagiri. Its physical source is up on the hill; its ritual origin is the stepped tank of Kuśāvarta in the town. From here it flows east across Maharashtra, Telangana and Andhra Pradesh to the Bay of Bengal.

Downstream lies Nashik, with Pañcavaṭī and Rāmkuṇḍ, the other half of the Siṃhastha Kumbh.

IN THE JYOTIRLIṄGA STOTRAM

"Tryambaka on the bank of the Gautamī"
vārāṇasyāṃ tu viśveśaṃtryambakaṃ gautamītaṭe |himālaye tu kedāraṃghuśmeśaṃ ca śivālaye ‖
What it says

"Viśveśa in Vārāṇasī, Tryambaka on the bank of the Gautamī, Kedāra in the Himālaya, and Ghuśmeśa at Śivālaya."

WordMeans
tryambaka"three-eyed" (tri + ambaka): Śiva; the same word opens the Mahāmṛtyuñjaya mantra, tryambakaṃ yajāmahe
gautamīthe river of Gautama: the Godāvarī
taṭe"on the bank"

The longer hymn. Verse ten of the stotram traditionally ascribed to Ādi Śaṅkara: sahyādri-śīrṣe vimale vasantaṃ godāvarī-tīra-pavitra-deśe | yad-darśanāt pātakam āśu nāśaṃ prayāti taṃ tryambakam īśam īḍe: "I praise Tryambaka, who dwells on the pure summit of the Sahyādri, in the holy land on the bank of the Godāvarī, by whose sight sin is quickly destroyed."

The Mṛtyuñjaya link. The great Vedic mantra for health and freedom from death begins "tryambakaṃ yajāmahe": "we worship the three-eyed one". Devotees feel a special bond between that mantra and this temple.

Tenth in the stotram: Tryambaka, the three-eyed Lord, where the Godāvarī is born.

THE LEGENDS · I

Gautama, the cow and the Gaṅgā

The sage's granary. The Śiva Purāṇa tells that during a long drought the sage Gautama and his wife Ahalyā lived on Brahmagiri. By the grace of Varuṇa, Gautama's hermitage always had water and grain, and he fed everyone who came.

The trick. Other sages grew jealous. They sent a weak, sickly cow into his field. When Gautama gently drove it away with a blade of darbha grass, it fell dead. The sages accused him of the great sin of killing a cow, and drove him out.

The penance. Grieving, Gautama did fierce penance to Śiva and asked for the Gaṅgā to come down and purify him and the land. Śiva granted it. The Gaṅgā descended on Brahmagiri, but kept disappearing into the hill.

Kuśāvarta. Gautama ringed the stream with kuśa grass to hold it, and the place became Kuśāvarta ("the kuśa circle"). From there the river flows to the sea as the Godāvarī, also called Gautamī. At Gautama's and the Gaṅgā's request, Śiva stayed here as Tryambakeśvara.

🐄 The false charge

Gautama was innocent, yet he sought purification anyway: the story honours humility over self-defence.

🌊 Dakṣiṇa Gaṅgā

The Godāvarī is the Gaṅgā brought south: "the Gaṅgā of the South", as holy as her northern sister.

A trick meant to ruin a good man became the reason a great river flows across the Deccan. In the story, grace turns even injustice into a gift for many.

ZAPPY: So the Godāvarī is really the Gaṅgā?

PROF. BOLT: In the story, yes. Bhagīratha brought the Gaṅgā to the north; Gautama brought her to the south.

THE LEGENDS · II

Three in one, and a hill that is Śiva

The pillar of light. As with all jyotirliṅgas, the deeper story is of Brahmā and Viṣṇu arguing over who was greater, and Śiva appearing as an endless pillar of light. Viṣṇu honestly admitted he could not find its end; Brahmā falsely claimed he had. At Tryambakeśvara, the story goes on: all three were reconciled, and all three are present in the liṅga.

Three forms. In the sanctum, there is no raised liṅga. Instead a small hollow in the stone pedestal holds three tiny, thumb-sized forms, honoured as Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva. Constant bathing with water has worn them over the centuries.

Brahmagiri is Śiva. Local tradition holds that the hill itself is a form of Śiva, so that, in the past, climbing it was frowned upon; pilgrims walked around it instead.

Gaṇeśa's part. Some tellings say Gaṇeśa took the form of the cow, at Pārvatī's wish, so that the Gaṅgā would come down to the south. Trimbak is also called a birthplace of Gaṇeśa in local tradition.

HISTORY · I

From ancient tīrtha to the Peshwās
Purāṇas Brahmagiri and the Godāvarī's source are praised as tīrthas; the Brahma Purāṇa's Gautamī Māhātmya is devoted to this river. Tradition says Rāma made a pilgrimage here.
13th c. The Nāth tradition flourishes in the caves of Brahmagiri. Nivṛttināth, initiated by the Nāth yogī Gahinīnāth, becomes the guru of his younger brother Jñāneśvar.
1297 Nivṛttināth takes samādhi at Trimbak; his shrine becomes a great Vārkarī pilgrimage.
17th c. The older temple is destroyed in the time of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb.
1755 Peshwā Bālājī Bājīrāv (Nānāsāheb), the third Peshwā, begins a new temple of black basalt on the old site. A popular story says it began with a wager over whether the liṅga's stone was hollow.
1786 The temple is completed after 31 years, at a cost recorded as about 16 lakh rupees.
Details of the earlier temple are not documented; the construction story of the wager is traditional.

HISTORY · II

Kuśāvarta to the Kumbh of 2027
1779 The temple is renovated under Ahilyābāī Holkar of Indore, according to the ASI.
18th c. The stone tank of Kuśāvarta is built by Rāosāheb Pārnerkar, Phaḍṇavīs (chief accountant) of the Indore state; busts of him and his wife stand at the tank's edge.
Kumbh order By long arrangement, the Śaiva akhāḍās (orders of ascetics) bathe at Trimbak during the Siṃhastha, and the Vaiṣṇava akhāḍās at Rāmkuṇḍ in Nashik.
Modern The temple is protected by the Archaeological Survey of India and run by the Shree Trimbakeshwar Devasthan Trust.
2015–16 The previous Nashik–Trimbak Siṃhastha Kumbh.
31 Oct 2026 The new Siṃhastha Kumbh begins with flag-hoisting (dhvajārohaṇa) at Rāmkuṇḍ in Nashik, performed by President Droupadi Murmu, and at Kuśāvarta in Trimbak, by the Chief Minister.
2027–28 Main bathing days (Amṛt Snān) fall in August–September 2027, with Trimbak's last great bath on 12 September 2027; the Kumbh closes on 24 July 2028.

Two towns, one Kumbh. The Nashik–Trimbak Siṃhastha is unique among the four Kumbhs in being held in two places at once, and in dividing the ascetics by tradition: Śiva's devotees at the river's source, Viṣṇu's in the city where Rāma lived in exile.

Some 2027 bathing dates were reported differently in early announcements; the dates here follow the June 2025 official announcement.

THE TEMPLE TODAY

A Peshwā temple of black stone

🕳️ The sanctum

The three small forms sit in a hollow at floor level, usually covered by a silver mask; a constant thin stream of water keeps them wet.

👑 The crown

A jewelled crown with diamonds, emeralds and other stones, said by tradition to date from the Pāṇḍavas' age, is placed on the liṅga and shown to devotees on Mondays and festivals.

🗿 The stone

Black basalt, carved with deities, animals and human figures; the spire rises in the Nāgara manner above the sanctum; the temple faces east.

💧 The tanks

Inside the precinct is the Amṛtavarṣiṇī tank (about 28 m × 30 m), with other small tīrthas named Bilva, Viśvanātha and Mukunda.

Statues of the Gaṅgā, Jaleśvara, Rāmeśvara, Gautameśvara and other deities stand in the precinct.

The Monday procession. Every Monday the Lord's image goes out to bathe in his own river at Kuśāvarta, and, as tradition puts it, to see how his town is doing. On Mahāśivarātri, Kārtika Pūrṇimā and Dasarā, the procession carries the golden crown.

Water always. Because the liṅga sits in a hollow, water is always present in the sanctum: the Godāvarī, devotees say, is born at Śiva's feet.

Hours change with festivals and the Kumbh; exact times are not given here.

RITES FOR THE ANCESTORS

Why families come to Trimbak

Trimbak is one of India's best-known places for rites performed for ancestors and for family afflictions, conducted by the town's hereditary tīrtha purohits (pilgrim priests). The Siṃhastha Māhātmya counts a śrāddha on the Godāvarī here among the most meritorious.

RiteWhat it is, in tradition
Nārāyaṇa NāgabaliA three-day rite in two parts: Nārāyaṇa bali, for an ancestor whose last rites were incomplete or whose death was untimely; and Nāga bali, a funeral rite for a figure of a serpent made of dough, done to atone for harming a cobra. Trimbak's tradition holds that this rite is properly performed only here, on specific auspicious dates.
Kālasarpa śāntiA rite of pacification for those whose horoscopes show the planets placed between Rāhu and Ketu (the "kālasarpa" pattern).
Tripiṇḍī śrāddhaOfferings of three rice balls (piṇḍa) for ancestors of three past generations who are thought not to have received due rites.
Gaṅgā pūjana, tarpaṇaWorship of the river, water offerings for ancestors, and other śrāddha rites at Kuśāvarta.
These rites are matters of faith and family tradition; this page describes them and makes no claims about their results.

THE SIṂHASTHA KUMBH

Trimbak and Nashik, 2026–28

Why "Siṃhastha". The Kumbh at Nashik and Trimbak, like Ujjain's, is held when Jupiter (Bṛhaspati) is in Siṃha (Leo), roughly every twelve years. Tradition says a drop of the nectar of immortality fell here when the gods and demons fought over the pot (kumbha).

2026–28. The current Siṃhastha opened on 31 October 2026 with the hoisting of the religious flag at Kuśāvarta in Trimbak and Rāmkuṇḍ in Nashik. The great royal baths (Amṛt Snān) fall in August–September 2027, with processions of the akhāḍās and the Nāga sādhus; Trimbak's final great bath is on 12 September 2027. The flag comes down on 24 July 2028.

The ascetics' bath. At Trimbak, the akhāḍās go down to Kuśāvarta in a fixed order, the Nāga sādhus first, before pilgrims bathe. For many, seeing that procession is the heart of the Kumbh.

The Kumbh at Trimbak is Śiva's half of the Siṃhastha: the ascetics bathe where the river is born.

AROUND THE TEMPLE

Sacred places of Trimbak and Nashik

🌊 Kuśāvarta

The square stepped tank in the town, the Godāvarī's ritual origin and the Kumbh bathing place.

⛰️ Brahmagiri and Gaṅgādvāra

Steps lead up the hill to Gaṅgādvāra, where the Gaṅgā (Godāvarī) shrine stands, and on to the summit and the river's source.

🪷 Nivṛttināth samādhi

The shrine of the Nāth saint, Jñāneśvar's guru, at the edge of town.

🌸 Nīlāmbikā

A Goddess temple on Nīlagiri hill, one of the three hills around the town.

🐒 Añjanerī

A hill near Trimbak held by local tradition to be the birthplace of Hanumān, son of Añjanā; other places in India also hold this belief.

✍️ Sākṣi Vināyaka

Gaṇeśa the witness, who records each pilgrim's yātrā.

🏹 Pañcavaṭī, Nashik

Where Rāma, Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa lived in exile: Rāmkuṇḍ, the Kālārāma temple and Sītā Guphā.

🕉️ Kapāleśvara, Nashik

A Śiva temple by Rāmkuṇḍ notable for having no Nandi before it, explained by its own legend.

Nashik also has the Buddhist Pāṇḍavleṇī caves, and the Jain shrine at Gajpanth.

THE GODĀVARĪ

The Gaṅgā of the South

From Trimbak the Godāvarī flows east for about 1,465 km, India's second-longest river and the longest in the peninsula, across Maharashtra, Telangana and Andhra Pradesh to the Bay of Bengal.

Along it lie holy places of many traditions: Nashik and Pañcavaṭī of the Rāmāyaṇa; Paiṭhaṇ, home of Sant Eknāth; Nānded, where Guru Gobind Singh spent his last days (Takht Sachkhand Hazūr Sāhib); Bhadrācalam, Rāma's temple in Telangana; and Rājamahendravaram, where the Godāvarī Puṣkaram is celebrated every twelve years.

🌊 Gautamī

The river's name for Gautama, whose penance brought her down.

🪔 Puṣkaram

A twelve-yearly river festival when Jupiter enters Siṃha: the same timing as the Siṃhastha.

ZAPPY: A Sikh Takht on the same river?

PROF. BOLT: At Nānded. The Godāvarī carries many faiths to the sea.

TRYAMBAKEŚVARA IN CULTURE

Nāth yogīs, Vārkarīs and Peshwās

🧘 The Nāth yogīs

The caves of Brahmagiri were a home of the Nāth tradition; Gahinīnāth initiated Nivṛttināth here, linking the Nāths to Maharashtra's devotional movement.

🪶 Nivṛttināth

Eldest of four saintly siblings (with Jñāneśvar, Sopāndev and Muktābāī), guru to Jñāneśvar, poet of abhaṅgas; his samādhi draws Vārkarīs from across Maharashtra.

🏛️ The Peshwās

Bālājī Bājīrāv's temple is one of the finest monuments of Peshwā-era Maharashtra, in black stone in the old Deccan manner.

📜 The purohits

Trimbak's tīrtha purohit families have served pilgrims for generations, keeping family registers much like the paṇḍās of Deoghar and Kāśī.

🏹 Rāma's land

Nashik's Pañcavaṭī ties the Godāvarī to the Rāmāyaṇa: Rāma, Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa lived on its bank in exile.

🔱 The sādhus

Every twelve years the akhāḍās of Śaiva ascetics descend on Trimbak: a living link to India's oldest monastic orders.

ZAPPY: Jñāneśvar again! He visited Bhīmaśaṅkara too.

PROF. BOLT: And here his own guru, his brother, rests. Trimbak is where Maharashtra's great saint was taught.
