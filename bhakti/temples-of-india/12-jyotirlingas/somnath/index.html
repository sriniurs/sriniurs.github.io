FOREWORD

The Dvādaśa Jyotirliṅga Stotram opens with one name: Somanātha, the Lord of the Moon, on the shore of Saurāṣṭra where the land ends and the ocean runs unbroken to the South Pole.

No temple in India carries a longer story of loss and return. Raided in 1026, rebuilt by kings and a queen, sacked again and again, and raised once more after Independence, Somanātha marked a thousand years since that first raid in January 2026, and seventy-five years since its reconsecration in May 2026.

This second guide in our series is meant to be kept and returned to: the legend of the waning moon, Kṛṣṇa's last days at Prabhāsa, the long history, the āratis and the evening show by the sea, the shrines of Prabhāsa, and the wider land of Saurāṣṭra.

May every reader one day watch the sun set behind the śikhara of Somanātha, with the waves of the Arabian Sea below.

WHERE IT IS

Where Saurāṣṭra meets the sea

Prabhāsa, "the radiant", is the old name of this stretch of coast; the town is Prabhāsa Pāṭaṇ, next to the fishing port of Veraval, at the southern edge of the Saurāṣṭra (Kāṭhiyāvāḍ) peninsula.

The temple stands right on the shore. Just east of it, the rivers Hiraṇ and Kapilā reach the sea at the Triveṇī Saṅgam, where tradition adds a third, unseen river: the Sarasvatī.

Look south from the temple's sea wall and, the old pillar says, there is no land until the South Pole.

IN THE JYOTIRLIṄGA STOTRAM

The line that begins the twelve
saurāṣṭre somanāthaṃ caśrīśaile mallikārjunam |ujjayinyāṃ mahākālamoṅkāram amaleśvaram ‖
What it says

"Somanātha in Saurāṣṭra, Mallikārjuna on Śrīśaila, Mahākāla in Ujjayinī, and at Oṅkāra, Amaleśvara."

WordMeans
saurāṣṭrein Saurāṣṭra, the peninsula of western Gujarat
somanāthamLord of Soma (soma + nātha): Soma is the Moon
ca"and": the list of twelve begins here

The longer hymn. The twelve-verse Dvādaśa Jyotirliṅga Stotram traditionally ascribed to Ādi Śaṅkara also opens here: saurāṣṭra-deśe viśade 'tiramye jyotirmayaṃ candrakalāvataṃsam, "in the bright and lovely land of Saurāṣṭra, the radiant one who wears the crescent moon as his crest-jewel"; its refrain is taṃ somanāthaṃ śaraṇaṃ prapadye, "in that Somanātha I take refuge".

Why "jyoti". Each jyotirliṅga is honoured as a place where Śiva's endless pillar of light, the one Brahmā and Viṣṇu could not measure, touched the earth (see our Liṅgāṣṭakam article).

First in the stotram: Somanātha, Lord of the Moon, in Saurāṣṭra.

THE LEGENDS · I

The Moon's curse, and the Moon's cure

Twenty-seven wives. Soma, the Moon, married the twenty-seven daughters of Dakṣa Prajāpati: the twenty-seven nakṣatras, the lunar mansions. But he loved only one, Rohiṇī, and neglected the rest.

The curse. The sisters complained to their father. Dakṣa warned Soma; Soma did not change. Angry, Dakṣa cursed him to waste away. The Moon began to shrink, and with him the plants and medicines he nourishes.

Prabhāsa. On Brahmā's advice, Soma came to this shore and worshipped Śiva with deep austerity and the Mṛtyuñjaya mantra. Śiva could not undo Dakṣa's word, but he softened it: Soma would wane for one fortnight and wax for the next. The Moon's phases were born.

The name. Soma installed a liṅga and begged Śiva to stay; Śiva did, as Somanātha, and took the crescent onto his own head. The place where Soma got back his lustre (prabhā) became Prabhāsa. The story is told in the Śiva Purāṇa (Koṭirudra Saṃhitā) and the Prabhāsa Khaṇḍa of the Skanda Purāṇa.

🌙 Four temples, four metals

A well-known tradition: Soma built the first temple in gold, Rāvaṇa in silver, Kṛṣṇa in wood, and King Bhīmadeva in stone. A story, not a record.

☾ Candraśekhara

The crescent on Śiva's head, "Candraśekhara", is often linked to this story: the Lord who sheltered the dying Moon.

Waxing and waning go on forever, but the light is never lost. No condition is permanent; grace works inside the law, not against it.

ZAPPY: So the Moon's phases come from a curse?

PROF. BOLT: In the story, from a curse softened by grace. Astronomy has its own explanation; the legend is about favouritism, and forgiveness.

THE LEGENDS · II

Kṛṣṇa's last days at Prabhāsa

The end of the Yādavas. The Mausala Parva of the Mahābhārata tells how, years after the great war, the Yādavas of Dwārakā came to Prabhāsa. Drunk, they quarrelled and destroyed one another with clubs of reed (eraka) grass, fulfilling an old curse.

Balarāma. Kṛṣṇa's elder brother sat in meditation; tradition says a great serpent, Śeṣa, left his body and returned to the sea. A cave near the Triveṇī, the Baldev Guphā, marks the spot.

The hunter's arrow. Kṛṣṇa lay resting under a tree. A hunter named Jarā mistook his foot for a deer and shot an arrow. Seeing what he had done, Jarā begged forgiveness, and Kṛṣṇa comforted him. The place is Bhālkā Tīrtha (from bhalla, arrowhead).

Dehotsarga. Kṛṣṇa then walked to the bank of the Hiraṇ and left his body near the Triveṇī Saṅgam: the Dehotsarga or Nijadhāma tīrtha, where the Gītā Mandir now stands. Tradition counts the start of the Kali Yuga from his departure.

A tīrtha of the epics. Prabhāsa appears in the Mahābhārata as a great pilgrim place on the sea, where the Sarasvatī meets the ocean; Arjuna visits it on his pilgrimage.

HISTORY · I

The raid and the rebuilders
Ancient Prabhāsa is a famous tīrtha in the epics and Purāṇas. When the first stone temple was built is not firmly known; some accounts credit the Maitraka kings of Valabhī (7th–8th c.), but this is debated.
Jan 1026 Maḥmūd of Ghaznī raids Somanātha, plunders its wealth and breaks the liṅga. The scholar al-Bīrūnī, who knew India well, wrote about the temple and its fame.
11th c. The Chaulukya (Solaṅkī) king Bhīma I rebuilds; tradition also links King Bhoja of Mālava with the restoration.
1169 Kumārapāla replaces a decaying temple, rebuilding it "in excellent stone and studded with jewels", according to an inscription of that year.
1299 Alauddin Khaljī's army under Ulugh Khān defeats the Vāghelā king Karṇa and sacks the temple. Local tradition honours Vīr Hamīrjī Gohil, who died defending it.
14th c. The Chūḍāsamā king Mahīpāla of Junāgaḍh rebuilds (around 1308).
1395–1706 More attacks follow, under Gujarat's sultans and finally on Aurangzeb's orders; worship at the old site is stopped.
The number of destructions (often given as "seven rebuildings") and many of the dates vary between sources; this page follows the most widely cited accounts.

HISTORY · II

From Ahilyābāī to the Swābhimān Parv
1783 Ahilyābāī Holkar of Indore builds a new temple next to the ruins so that worship can continue: today's Juna (old) Somnāth, as she did at Kāśī three years earlier.
1842 The "Proclamation of the Gates": Governor-General Lord Ellenborough claims to bring Somnāth's gates back from Ghaznī. The gates turn out not to be from Somnāth.
1947 After Independence, Sardār Vallabhbhāī Paṭel visits the ruins and resolves to rebuild; K. M. Munshi drives the project through a public trust.
1950 The old ruins are cleared in October and work begins.
1951 On 11 May, President Rajendra Prasad performs the consecration (prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā).
1995 The whole complex is completed; President Shankar Dayal Sharma dedicates it on 1 December.
2021 Narendra Modi becomes chairman of the Shree Somnath Trust (January). In August, the 1.4 km Samudra Darśan Path, an exhibition centre and the renovated Juna Somnāth open, and the foundation of a Pārvatī temple is laid.
2026 The Somnāth Swābhimān Parv (8–11 January) marks 1,000 years since the 1026 raid; 11 May marks 75 years since the 1951 reopening.

Reading the record. Somanātha has long been a symbol, and people read it differently. In 1951, Prime Minister Nehru had reservations about the President attending the consecration; Rajendra Prasad went. Historians also debate how the 1026 raid was remembered over the centuries, for example in Romila Thapar's Somanatha: The Many Voices of a History (2004). This article reports these views and takes no side.

THE TEMPLE TODAY

What you'll see inside

🛕 The sanctum

The jyotirliṅga sits in the garbhagṛha; the halls in front, the sabhāmaṇḍapa and nṛtyamaṇḍapa, hold the crowds at ārati time. The temple faces east, toward the rising sun.

🗼 The śikhara

About 155 ft (47 m) high, topped by a kalaśa said to weigh about 10 tonnes and a saffron flag. Pilgrims sponsor the changing of the flag (dhvajā) as an offering.

🏹 Bāṇa Stambha

The "arrow pillar" on the sea side. Its Sanskrit line, āsamudrānta dakṣiṇa dhruva paryanta abādhita jyotirmārga, says the path due south is unbroken to the South Pole.

🚪 Digvijay Dwār

The main gateway, with a statue of Sardār Paṭel in front, honouring his role in the rebuilding.

The style is Kailāśa Mahāmeru Prāsāda, in the Māru-Gurjara tradition of the Chaulukyas, built by the hereditary Sompurā temple-builders; the chief architect is usually named as Prabhashankar Sompura. The pillar's age is unknown and its geographic claim is a traditional one: it is not an ancient survey.

AROUND THE TEMPLE

Shrines and sights within a few kilometres

🛕 Juna Somnāth

Ahilyābāī Holkar's temple of 1783, a short walk from the main temple; renovated in 2021.

🌊 Triveṇī Saṅgam

Stepped ghats where the Hiraṇ and Kapilā (and, by tradition, the Sarasvatī) meet the sea. Pilgrims bathe here before darśan.

🏹 Bhālkā Tīrtha

Near Prabhās, toward Veraval: where Kṛṣṇa was struck by Jarā's arrow. A shrine shows Kṛṣṇa reclining under a pipal tree.

📖 Gītā Mandir · Dehotsarga

By the Hiraṇ near the Triveṇī: marks Kṛṣṇa's departure; verses of the Gītā line the walls. Nearby: Lakṣmīnārāyaṇa and Baldev Guphā.

🚶 Samudra Darśan Path

A 1.4 km seafront promenade behind the temple, opened in 2021, looking out over the Arabian Sea.

🏺 Exhibition centre

Carved stones and sculpture from the old Somnāth temples, displayed near the main temple.

☀️ Sūrya Mandir

An old Sun temple in Prabhāsa Pāṭaṇ, a reminder of how many shrines this town once held.

⛰️ Pāṇḍava Guphā

Caves linked by local tradition with the Pāṇḍavas; small temples around them.

A new Pārvatī temple near the main shrine had its foundation stone laid in 2021.

THE PRABHĀSA CIRCUIT

A walk through the holy field in one day

There is no fixed "official" route, but pilgrims to Prabhāsa have long followed a simple pattern: bathe first, then worship, then follow Kṛṣṇa's last journey. The Prabhāsa Khaṇḍa of the Skanda Purāṇa lists hundreds of tīrthas in this kṣetra; most visitors see a handful.

Triveṇī Saṅgam: a dip or a sprinkle of water at the confluence, at sunrise.
Somanātha: darśan and the 7 AM ārati.
Juna Somnāth: Ahilyābāī's temple, a few minutes' walk.
Bhālkā Tīrtha: the place of the arrow, toward Veraval.
Gītā Mandir and Dehotsarga: back by the Hiraṇ, where Kṛṣṇa left his body.
Baldev Guphā: Balarāma's cave nearby; end at the sea wall for the evening ārati.

SOMANĀTHA IN CULTURE

Builders, warriors, writers

⚒️ The Sompurās

Gujarat's hereditary temple-builders (salāṭs), heirs to the Chaulukya craft. Their family has built Somnāth and many famous temples across India.

⚔️ Vīr Hamīrjī Gohil

Remembered in Saurāṣṭra's ballads as the young warrior who fell defending the temple in 1299; a memorial stands near the temple.

📜 Al-Bīrūnī

The Persian scholar described Somanātha and its links with the Moon and the tides in his book on India, written about 1030.

📖 K. M. Munshi

Gujarati novelist and statesman. His historical novel Jay Somnāth retold the 1026 story long before he helped rebuild the temple.

🇮🇳 Sardār Paṭel

Saw the rebuilding as a matter of national self-respect; he died in December 1950 and did not see the consecration.

⛵ Veraval

One of India's biggest fishing harbours, and a centre of wooden boat (dhow) building: Prabhāsa has always lived by the sea.

ZAPPY: Why does everyone call Somnāth a symbol?

PROF. BOLT: Because it kept coming back. People read different lessons into that; the pilgrims simply keep coming.
