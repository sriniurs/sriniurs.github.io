NĀGEŚVARA

THE 12 JYOTIRLIṄGAS · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The Lord of serpents in the Dāruka forest: legends, history and worship at Dwārakā, Auṇḍhā and Jāgeśvar · explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: A one-page summary first, then maps, the stotram line and the question of where the Dāruka forest lies, the legends, the history and today of each of the three shrines, rituals, festivals, the places around them, and the serpent in Indian worship. Keep it as a reference.

FOREWORD

The stotram says only "Nāgeśa in the Dāruka forest": the Lord of serpents, in a forest named after a demoness, or after the deodar tree. Where was that forest? Three old shrines answer, each with conviction.

Near Dwārakā on the Gujarat coast, a temple with a giant seated Śiva points to the Śiva Purāṇa's "forest by the western sea". At Auṇḍhā in Maharashtra, a carved Yādava-era temple, the one that turned to face the saint Nāmdev, holds Maharashtra's faith. And at Jāgeśvar in the Kumaon Himalaya, more than a hundred stone temples stand in a deodar forest that is itself a "dāru vana".

This ninth guide in our series honours all three. It tells the story of Supriya, the merchant who would not stop chanting; the history of each shrine; and the place of the serpent in Indian worship, without deciding for the reader where the true forest lies.

May every reader one day stand in one of these three forests, and find the Lord of serpents waiting.

ZAPPY: Another jyotirliṅga with three homes?

PROF. BOLT: Like Vaidyanātha. The stotram names a forest, not a town, so each region has looked for that forest in its own landscape.

WHERE IT IS

A coast, a plateau and a mountain forest

Dwārakā: on the north-west tip of the Saurāṣṭra peninsula, Devbhūmi Dwārakā district, Gujarat. The Nāgeśvar temple stands on the road between Dwārakā town and the island of Beṭ Dwārakā.

Auṇḍhā Nāgnāth: a small town in Hingoli district, Marāṭhwāḍā, Maharashtra, on the Deccan plateau, not far from Parli Vaijnāth.

Jāgeśvar: at about 1,870 m in the Kumaon Himalaya, Almora district, Uttarakhand, in a valley of tall deodar trees beside the Jaṭā Gaṅgā stream.

IN THE JYOTIRLIṄGA STOTRAM

"Nāgeśa in the Dāruka forest"
paralyāṃ vaidyanāthaṃ caḍākinyāṃ bhīmaśaṅkaram |setubandhe tu rāmeśaṃnāgeśaṃ dārukāvane ‖
What it says

"Vaidyanātha at Paralī, Bhīmaśaṅkara in Ḍākinī, Rāmeśa at Setubandha, and Nāgeśa in the Dāruka forest."

WordMeans
nāgeśaLord of serpents (nāga + īśa)
dārukāthe demoness Dārukā of the legend; also linked with dāru, the deodar tree
vanaforest

The longer hymn. Verse eight of the stotram traditionally ascribed to Ādi Śaṅkara reads in many printings: yāmye sadaṅge nagare 'tiramye vibhūṣitāṅgaṃ vividhaiś ca bhogaiḥ | sadbhukti-mukti-pradam īśam ekaṃ śrī-nāganāthaṃ śaraṇaṃ prapadye: "In the south, in the lovely town of Sadaṅga, I take refuge in Śrī Nāganātha, his body adorned with serpents, the one Lord who gives both joy and liberation."

A clue for each. Auṇḍhā's devotees read "in the south (yāmye), in the town of Sadaṅga" as pointing to them. Dwārakā's devotees point to the Śiva Purāṇa, which places the forest by the western sea. Jāgeśvar's devotees point to the word dāru, the deodar, whose forest surrounds their temples.

Eighth in the stotram: Nāgeśa, the Lord who wears serpents, honoured in three forests.

No final word. No authority has settled which shrine is the Nāgeśvara of the stotram. Pilgrims on the Gujarat circuit visit Dwārakā; those on the Marāṭhwāḍā circuit, with Parli and Ghṛṣṇeśvara, visit Auṇḍhā; Himalayan pilgrims honour Jāgeśvar. Many visit more than one.

This article takes no side. Dwārakā is the claimant most often named in all-India lists today; Auṇḍhā and Jāgeśvar have older surviving buildings.

ZAPPY: So the "Dāruka forest" could be by the sea, on a plateau, or in the mountains?

PROF. BOLT: Each reading is honest to some text. And each of the three places is old, holy and loved. That's worth more than a final ruling.

THE LEGENDS · I

Supriya, the merchant who kept chanting

Dārukā and Dāruka. The Koṭirudra Saṃhitā of the Śiva Purāṇa tells of a demoness, Dārukā, who had won a boon from Pārvatī: her forest by the western sea could move wherever she wished. With her husband, the demon Dāruka, she terrorised the region, attacking sages and travellers.

The prisoner. Among their captives was Supriya, a merchant and a great devotee of Śiva. In the demons' prison he went on worshipping, chanting Śiva's name and teaching the other prisoners to chant with him.

The Lord appears. When Dāruka came to kill him, Supriya called on Śiva. Śiva rose from the ground in a shrine of light, gave Supriya the Pāśupata weapon, and the demons were destroyed. Pārvatī, keeping her word to Dārukā, protected a remnant of her people.

The name. Śiva stayed at that spot as Nāgeśvara, with Pārvatī as Nāgeśvarī, to protect his devotees there forever.

🗣️ The chant

Supriya had no weapon but Śiva's name. The story is told to encourage steady prayer in hard times.

🐍 Nāga

Śiva wears serpents as ornaments: Vāsuki around his neck. As their Lord, he is prayed to for protection from poison and fear.

Supriya's prison did not stop his worship; his worship opened his prison. The story's point is that devotion does not wait for good conditions.

ZAPPY: A prisoner again, like King Sudakṣiṇa at Bhīmaśaṅkara!

PROF. BOLT: The Śiva Purāṇa loves that pattern: the devotee in chains who still worships, and the Lord who answers.

THE LEGENDS · II

The deodar forest, and the temple that turned

The sages of Dāruvana. Several Purāṇas tell of sages living in Dāruvana, the deodar forest, proud of their rituals. Śiva came among them as a naked, beautiful wandering ascetic wearing only serpents; their wives followed him, and the angry sages cursed him. His liṅga fell to the ground and the worlds trembled, until the sages understood who he was and began to worship the liṅga. Jāgeśvar's tradition places this story in its own deodar valley, and the Dwārakā shrine also tells a version.

Nāmdev and the turning temple. At Auṇḍhā, the much-loved story is of the saint-poet Nāmdev (13th–14th c.). He came to sing before Nāgnāth but, the story goes, the priests pushed him out of the temple. He sat down behind it and sang his kīrtan with all his heart. Then the whole temple turned around to face him, so that the god could hear his devotee. Tradition says that is why the temple's Nandi stands at the back.

Visobā's lesson. At the same temple Nāmdev met his guru, Visobā Khecar, who lay resting with his feet on a liṅga. When Nāmdev objected, Visobā asked him to move his feet to where there was no Śiva; wherever Nāmdev put them, a liṅga appeared. Nāmdev learned that God is everywhere.

HISTORY · I

Dwārakā and Auṇḍhā

Dwārakā: Nāgeśvar. The shrine between Dwārakā and Beṭ Dwārakā was long a small temple on the pilgrim road. Its present form was built in the 1990s with funds from the Gulshan Kumar charitable trust; after his death in 1997 his family completed the work. The liṅga lies in a sanctum below ground level; the giant statue of Śiva was added in the same period. The temple is run by a local trust.

Tradition Auṇḍhā's first temple is credited by local tradition to Yudhiṣṭhira, eldest of the Pāṇḍavas, during their exile.
13th c. The Seuna (Yādava) kings of Devagiri build the present temple in the Hemāḍpantī style, of dark stone without mortar, its walls covered with carvings. Tradition says it rose seven storeys.
13th–14th c. The saints Jñāneśvar, Muktābāī and Nāmdev are linked with the temple; in the Jñāndev Gāthā, Jñāneśvar sends Nāmdev to Auṇḍhā to find his guru.
16th c. Guru Nānak is said to have visited Auṇḍhā and nearby Narsī, Nāmdev's birthplace, on his travels.
17th c. The upper storeys are destroyed in the time of Aurangzeb.
18th c. Ahilyābāī Holkar rebuilds the spire; the lower, carved Yādava temple survives beneath.

HISTORY · II

Jāgeśvar: a valley of temples
Early Śiva worship in the Jāgeśvar valley is old; the deodar groves were sacred long before the stone temples.
7th c. The earliest surviving temples are built; the Mahāmṛtyuñjaya temple is often named as the oldest.
7th–12th c. The Katyūrī kings of Kumaon build most of the temples. The valley becomes a major centre of the Lakulīśa Pāśupata Śaiva order.
Inscriptions More than 25 inscriptions in Sanskrit, mostly of the 7th–10th centuries, are carved on the walls and pillars.
15th–18th c. The Chand kings of Kumaon maintain and add to the temples and give land grants.
Modern The Archaeological Survey of India protects the temple groups and runs a site museum with sculptures from the valley.

How many temples? The main Jāgeśvar complex has about 124 temples, large and small, inside a stone wall; with the nearby Daṇḍeśvar group and roadside shrines, some 150 have been numbered. Tradition says there were once many more.

Śaṅkara. A popular belief credits Ādi Śaṅkara with reviving worship here; scholars find no inscription to confirm it.

Dates for Jāgeśvar follow architectural and inscriptional studies cited by the ASI and scholars; individual temples vary.

NĀGEŚVAR, DWĀRAKĀ TODAY

The giant Śiva of the coast

⬇️ The sanctum

The liṅga lies in a small chamber below ground; pilgrims go down a few steps. A silver serpent coils over it, and Pārvatī as Nāgeśvarī is beside it.

🗿 The statue

A seated Śiva about 25 m (some 80 ft) high, painted blue, visible from far across the flat coastal land; built in the 1990s.

🌳 Garden and pond

A broad garden with a pond surrounds the statue and temple, in quiet scrubland near the sea.

🐍 Offerings

Pilgrims offer small silver or copper serpents (nāga-nāgin), praying for protection from snakebite and from fear.

The temple is visited by most pilgrims to Dwārakā on their way to Beṭ Dwārakā, as part of a single journey with the Dwārakādhīśa temple.

AUṆḌHĀ NĀGNĀTH TODAY

The carved stone temple of Marāṭhwāḍā

🪨 Hemāḍpantī walls

Dark stone set without mortar, every surface carved: gods, dancers, elephants, scenes from the epics and geometric bands. The lower temple is the Yādava original.

⬇️ The sanctum

As at Dwārakā, the liṅga is in a small chamber below ground level, reached by a narrow passage.

🐂 Nandi behind

Nandi stands behind the temple, not in front: by tradition, because the temple turned to face Nāmdev.

🛕 Many shrines

The precinct holds many small Śiva shrines (local sources speak of 108 temples and 68 shrines), and tanks before the Śani shrine.

Auṇḍhā is also revered by Sikhs, through Nāmdev, whose verses are included in the Guru Granth Sahib.

JĀGEŚVAR TODAY

A hundred spires among the deodars

🛕 Jāgeśvar (Bāl Jāgeśvar)

The main temple, Śiva as Jāgeśvar or Yogeśvar, "Lord of yoga", guarded at the door by two tall bronze-like door guardians, Nandi and Skandi.

⏳ Mahāmṛtyuñjaya

The largest and, by many accounts, oldest temple of the group, dedicated to Śiva as the conqueror of death.

🏛️ ASI museum

A site museum holds sculptures from the valley, including Umā-Maheśvara images and figures linked with the Lakulīśa tradition.

🌲 The deodars

Huge, ancient deodar (Himalayan cedar) trees fill the valley; one is worshipped as Ardhanārīśvara, half Śiva and half Pārvatī.

Uphill is Vṛddha Jāgeśvar ("old Jāgeśvar"), and nearby the Daṇḍeśvar and Kuber temple groups.

Mṛtyuñjaya at Jāgeśvar. As the home of the Mahāmṛtyuñjaya temple, Jāgeśvar is a favourite place for the Mahāmṛtyuñjaya japa, the chant for health and freedom from the fear of death.

Underground darśan. At both Dwārakā and Auṇḍhā, pilgrims go down to the liṅga in a chamber below the earth: the Lord of serpents, like the nāgas, dwelling below the ground.

Hours and sevās differ at each shrine and change through the year; they are not given here.

AROUND THE THREE SHRINES

Neighbours on each pilgrim road

🪈 Dwārakādhīśa

Kṛṣṇa's great temple in Dwārakā town, one of the four Cār Dhāms, by the Gomatī creek.

🏝️ Beṭ Dwārakā

The island linked with Kṛṣṇa's palace and his friend Sudāmā, now joined to the mainland by the Sudarśan Setu bridge.

💛 Gopī Talāv

A pond near Nāgeśvar where, tradition says, the gopīs of Vraj met Kṛṣṇa; its yellow clay (gopīcandana) is used for tilak.

🛕 Rukmiṇī temple

The carved 12th-century temple of Kṛṣṇa's queen, outside Dwārakā town.

🎶 Narsī Nāmdev

Near Auṇḍhā: Nāmdev's birthplace, with a temple and gurdwara.

🔱 Parli Vaijnāth

A Vaidyanātha claimant, within reach of Auṇḍhā: two jyotirliṅga claimants in Marāṭhwāḍā.

🌲 Vṛddha Jāgeśvar

Above Jāgeśvar: the "old" shrine on the ridge, with Himalayan views.

🕳️ Pātāl Bhuvaneśvar

In Kumaon: a cave temple with natural rock forms worshipped as deities, including Śeṣa-nāga.

NĀGEŚVARA IN CULTURE

Serpents, saints and the meaning of "Dāruka"

🐍 The nāga in India

Serpents are guardians of water, treasure and the earth's depths. Viṣṇu rests on Śeṣa, Śiva wears Vāsuki, and village shrines to nāgas dot India from Kerala's sarpa-kāvu groves to Kumaon.

🎶 Nāmdev

The tailor-saint of Pandharpur, a founder of the Vārkarī tradition; his abhaṅgas are sung in Maharashtra and his verses are in the Guru Granth Sahib.

🧘 Lakulīśa

Founder of the Pāśupata order, shown with a club; Jāgeśvar was one of the order's great Himalayan centres.

🌲 Dāru

The deodar, devadāru, "tree of the gods": sacred in the Himalaya and the very word in "Dāruka forest", in one reading.

🪈 Kṛṣṇa's coast

At Dwārakā, Nāgeśvara is part of Kṛṣṇa's land: pilgrims to Dwārakādhīśa make Śiva part of the same journey.

🎞️ Modern patrons

As in earlier centuries kings did, 20th-century donors rebuilt shrines; Dwārakā's temple and statue are the work of a music-industry family's trust.

ZAPPY: Why do people fear and worship snakes at the same time?

PROF. BOLT: Because what we fear, we respect. Śiva wears the serpent calmly: the poison is under his control. That's the lesson of Nāgeśvara.
