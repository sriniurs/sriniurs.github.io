KEDĀRANĀTHA

THE 12 JYOTIRLIṄGAS · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Legends, history, rituals, festivals and the highest of the twelve · explained and illustrated

FOREWORD

High in the Garhwal Himālaya, at about 3,583 metres, where the Mandākinī rises below the snows, stands Kedāranātha, the eleventh jyotirliṅga and the highest of the twelve. Its grey stone walls have faced the mountains for well over a thousand years.

The liṅga here is not a smooth pillar but a rough, humped rock, which tradition calls the back of Śiva in the form of a bull, caught by the Pāṇḍavas as he tried to escape them. For six months the temple is open; for six months it lies under snow, and the Lord is worshipped far below at Ukhīmaṭh. Its chief priest comes, by a tradition centuries old, from the Vīraśaiva community of Karnataka. Behind it lies the memorial to Ādi Śaṅkara, and the great rock that stood between the temple and the flood of 2013.

This eleventh guide in our series follows the line "himālaye tu kedāraṃ": its legends, its history, its seasons and its people.

May every reader one day stand before that rock-hewn temple beneath the snows.

WHERE IT IS

In the Garhwal Himālaya, at the head of the Mandākinī

Kedarnath lies in the Rudraprayag district of Uttarakhand, in a high glacial valley of the Garhwal Himālaya, at about 3,583 m, with the Kedārnāth peaks behind.

The Mandākinī rises from the Chorābārī glacier above the temple and flows south to join the Alakanandā at Rudraprayag; the Alakanandā later joins the Bhāgīrathī at Devaprayāg to become the Gaṅgā.

The temple has always been reached on foot, along the Mandākinī valley from Gaurīkuṇḍ.

IN THE JYOTIRLIṄGA STOTRAM

"Kedāra in the Himālaya"
vārāṇasyāṃ tu viśveśaṃtryambakaṃ gautamītaṭe |himālaye tu kedāraṃghuśmeśaṃ ca śivālaye ‖
What it says

"Viśveśa in Vārāṇasī, Tryambaka on the bank of the Gautamī, Kedāra in the Himālaya, and Ghuśmeśa at Śivālaya."

WordMeans
himālaye"in the Himālaya", the abode of snow
tu"and", a small joining word
kedāramKedāra: the Lord of the Kedāra land; kedāra also means a "field" or watered meadow

The longer hymn. Verse eleven of the stotram traditionally ascribed to Ādi Śaṅkara: mahādri-pārśve ca taṭe ramantaṃ sampūjyamānaṃ satataṃ munīndraiḥ | surāsurair yakṣa-mahoragādyaiḥ kedāram īśaṃ śivam ekam īḍe: "I praise the one Śiva, Lord Kedāra, who delights on the slope of the great mountain, ever worshipped by the best of sages, by gods and demons, yakṣas and great serpents."

A field of liberation. The Kāśī-Kedāra Māhātmya explains the name poetically: Kedāra is the field where the crop of liberation grows.

Eleventh in the stotram: Kedāra, Lord of the high field, in the Himālaya.

THE LEGENDS · I

The Pāṇḍavas and the bull

The burden of war. After the Kurukṣetra war, the Pāṇḍavas carried the sin of killing their own kin and teachers. On Vyāsa's or Kṛṣṇa's advice, the popular tradition says, they set out to find Śiva and ask his forgiveness.

Śiva hides. Śiva did not wish to grant it so easily. He fled from Kāśī to the Himālaya, and hid at Guptakāśī ("hidden Kāśī"). Then he took the form of a bull and mingled with a herd grazing in the mountains.

Bhīma's watch. Bhīma spread his legs across two hills, and all the cattle passed beneath him, except one bull, which would not. Knowing it was Śiva, Bhīma lunged. The bull began to sink into the ground, and Bhīma caught only its hump.

Forgiveness. Moved by their devotion, Śiva freed them of their sin and stayed at Kedāra as the hump-shaped liṅga. The other parts of the bull appeared at four other places, the Pañca Kedāra.

The last journey. Tradition says the Pāṇḍavas later left for heaven from the Kedāra region by the Mahāpantha, "the great path" into the high mountains.

🐂 The hump

Why the liṅga here is a rough, humped rock, not a carved pillar.

🪨 Five places

Hump, arms, face, navel and hair: the Pañca Kedāra.

ZAPPY: Is this story in the Mahābhārata?

PROF. BOLT: Not in the epic itself. It's the Himālaya's own tradition, told in the Kedāra Khaṇḍa and by every Garhwali grandmother.

THE LEGENDS · II

Nara and Nārāyaṇa, and the jyotirliṅga

The two sages. The Śiva Purāṇa (Koṭirudra Saṃhitā) gives the jyotirliṅga's own story. Nara and Nārāyaṇa, two forms of Viṣṇu, did penance at Badarikāśrama in the Himālaya, worshipping a liṅga of earth each day.

The boon. Pleased, Śiva appeared and offered a boon. They asked him to stay there forever, in his own form, for the good of the world. Śiva agreed, and stayed at Kedāra as a jyotirliṅga.

Its promise. The Purāṇa says that one who worships Kedāreśvara, and even one who dies on the way to him, is freed; and that whoever drinks water at Kedāra will not be born again.

Two shrines, one valley. So the Himālaya holds a pair: Badrīnāth, where Nara-Nārāyaṇa did penance, for Viṣṇu; and Kedāranātha, where Śiva stayed at their request. Pilgrims have long visited both.

🙏 Nara-Nārāyaṇa

Viṣṇu's sages ask Śiva to stay.

🏔️ Badrīnāth

Viṣṇu's shrine, Kedāra's partner.

💧 Kedāra water

Praised as freeing from rebirth.

In this story, it is Viṣṇu's own sages who ask Śiva to remain. Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava devotion meet in one valley.

HISTORY · I

From the Tēvāram to Śaṅkara
Origins Who built the first temple, and when, is not known. Tradition credits the Pāṇḍavas; the present stone temple is often dated to around the 8th century, though firm evidence is lacking.
7th–8th c. The Tamil Śaiva saints Sambandar and Sundarar sing of Kedāram in the Tēvāram, making it one of the 275 Pāḍal Peṯṟa Sthalams, though they sang of it from far away.
8th c. Ādi Śaṅkara is credited with reviving Kedāranātha, Badrīnāth and the shrines of the Himālaya. Hagiographies following Mādhava's Śaṅkara-digvijaya say he left the world near Kedāra; others place his end at Kāñcīpuram.
12th c. Kedāranātha is already a major pilgrimage, named in the Kṛtyakalpataru of Lakṣmīdhara, minister of the Gāhaḍavāla king Govindacandra.
Centuries The Rāval line of Vīraśaiva priests from Karnataka takes charge of worship; the region's kings and pilgrims support the temple.
The temple's age is uncertain. A popular claim that it lay under snow for centuries during the "Little Ice Age" is often repeated but not established.

HISTORY · II

The flood and after
1939 / 1948 The Shri Badarinath and Shri Kedarnath Mandir Act of the United Provinces (later Uttar Pradesh) places both temples under a committee, today the BKTC.
16–17 Jun 2013 After extreme rain, a landslide and then a burst of water from Chorābārī Tāl sweep down the valley. The town of Kedarnath is devastated and thousands of lives are lost across Uttarakhand.
The rock A huge boulder lodged just behind the temple and split the torrent, which passed on both sides. The temple, on its raised platform, survived with damage around it. The boulder is now honoured as Bhīma Śilā.
2013–14 Worship is restored after purification rites; meanwhile the winter seat at Ukhīmaṭh keeps the worship going.
2017 on A large rebuilding of the town: retaining walls and ghats on the Mandākinī and Sarasvatī, a wide approach to the temple, and meditation caves.
Nov 2021 A rebuilt Ādi Śaṅkara samādhi with a new statue of the ācārya is opened behind the temple.
2026 The temple opens on 22 April and closes on Bhāī Dūj, 11 November, after more than 19 lakh pilgrims.

ZAPPY: The rock saved the temple?

PROF. BOLT: Engineers point to the rock and the thick old stone; devotees see the Lord's hand. Both honour it, and both mourn all who were lost that night.

THE TEMPLE TODAY

Grey stone beneath the snows

🪨 The liṅga

A dark, irregular, humped rock in the sanctum, worshipped directly by pilgrims, many of whom touch it and offer ghee.

🧱 The stone

Massive grey stone slabs, closely fitted, on a raised platform; a low pyramidal roof made for snow; a carved head on the triangular front gable is of unknown identity.

🐂 Nandi

A large stone Nandi sits in the open before the door, facing the sanctum.

👥 The hall

Images of the five Pāṇḍavas, Kṛṣṇa, Kuntī, Draupadī and other deities line the maṇḍapa, keeping the epic legend present.

Bare and adorned. The day has two faces: in the morning, the nirvāṇa (bare) rock that anyone may touch; in the evening, the Lord dressed as a king. The pilgrim meets both the formless and the formed Śiva.

Who performs it. The Rāval directs the worship; assistant priests perform the daily rites.

Hours vary with the season and crowds; exact times are not given here.

SIX MONTHS OPEN, SIX CLOSED

The two homes of Kedāranātha

Closing. On Bhāī Dūj (Yama Dvitīyā, after Dīpāvalī), after a samādhi pūjā, the liṅga is covered, the doors are sealed and a lamp is left burning. The Pañcamukhī Utsava Ḍolī, a palanquin bearing the five-faced festival image, is carried down by priests and devotees.

The winter seat. The ḍolī halts on the way, at Rāmpur and at the Viśvanātha temple of Guptakāśī, and is installed at the Oṅkāreśvara temple, Ukhīmaṭh, where daily worship of Kedāranātha continues all winter. Madhyamaheśvara's image winters there too.

Opening. The opening date is announced at Ukhīmaṭh on Mahāśivarātri. Before the ḍolī climbs, Bhairavanātha, the guardian, is worshipped; the doors open in late April or May, near Akṣaya Tṛtīyā.

Kedāranātha never stops being worshipped: in summer on the mountain, in winter at Ukhīmaṭh.

THE PAÑCA KEDĀRA

Five shrines, one bull
ShrinePart of the bullNotes
KedāranāthaThe humpThe jyotirliṅga; first of the five.
MadhyamaheśvaraThe navel (middle)A high meadow temple; its image winters at Ukhīmaṭh.
TuṅganāthaThe armsOften called the highest Śiva temple in the world; priests are local.
RudranāthaThe faceŚiva worshipped as Nīlakaṇṭha, with a face in rock.
KalpeśvaraThe hair (matted locks)The only one of the five open all year.

The head in Nepal. Some traditions add that the head of the bull emerged at Paśupatinātha in Kathmandu, binding the Himālayan shrines of India and Nepal into one body of Śiva.

The circuit. Visiting all five, high in the Garhwal hills, is the Pañca Kedāra yātrā, among the most demanding pilgrimages in India. Many also add Kalpeśvara in winter, when the others are closed.

ZAPPY: So one bull became five temples?

PROF. BOLT: Six, if you count Nepal. The whole mountain range is read as Śiva's body.

AROUND THE TEMPLE

Sacred places of the Kedāra valley

🕉️ Ādi Śaṅkara samādhi

Behind the temple: the memorial to the ācārya, rebuilt after 2013 with a new statue.

🛡️ Bhairavanātha

On the slope east of the temple: the kṣetrapāla, guardian of Kedāra, said to watch over the temple while it is closed.

🌊 Chorābārī Tāl

A glacial lake above the temple, also called Gāndhī Sarovar after some of Gandhi's ashes were immersed there.

🐍 Vāsuki Tāl

A high lake linked in tradition to the serpent Vāsuki.

♨️ Gaurīkuṇḍ

Where Pārvatī is said to have done penance to win Śiva; a hot spring and a Gaurī temple.

🔥 Triyugīnārāyaṇa

Where Śiva and Pārvatī are said to have married before Viṣṇu; a fire kept burning, tradition says, since that wedding.

🫥 Guptakāśī

"Hidden Kāśī", where Śiva hid from the Pāṇḍavas; a Viśvanātha temple and the Ardhanārīśvara shrine.

🏛️ Ukhīmaṭh

The Oṅkāreśvara temple, the winter seat, and the seat of the Rāval.

Gaurīkuṇḍ and Triyugīnārāyaṇa sit in the Mandākinī and Songanga valleys, below Kedāra.

THE PRIESTS FROM KARNATAKA

The Rāvals of Kedāra

The Rāval. The chief priest of Kedāranātha is the Rāval, also styled Jagadguru. By long tradition he belongs to the Vīraśaiva (Jaṅgama) community of Karnataka, the Liṅgāyata tradition of Basava's land, and his seat is the maṭh at Ukhīmaṭh.

Guide, not performer. Unlike the Rāval of Badrīnāth, Kedāra's Rāval does not himself perform the daily pūjā; he directs it, and assistant priests carry it out. With him serve a Nāib Rāval, an Ācārya (Dharmādhikārī) and a Vedapāṭhī.

The line. The present Rāval, Jagadguru Bhīmāśaṅkaraliṅga Śivācārya, is counted as the 325th. Before him came Vāgīśaliṅgācārya of Davanagere district, among many from Karnataka.

Wider ties. Vīraśaiva Jaṅgamas have also served at Madhyamaheśvara, Guptakāśī and Ukhīmaṭh, linking the Garhwal Himālaya to the Kannada Śaiva world in a bond centuries old.

🗺️ North and south

A Kannada lineage at the Himālaya's top, as a Malayāḷī Nambūdiri serves at Badrīnāth.

📿 Iṣṭaliṅga

The Vīraśaiva tradition wears the liṅga on the body; at Kedāra its priests serve Śiva in the rock.

How and when the Vīraśaiva line began at Kedāra is not documented; some accounts say mantras are still recited partly in Kannada, which we could not confirm.

KEDĀRANĀTHA IN CULTURE

Saints, sages and a whole region

🎶 The Tēvāram

Sambandar and Sundarar sang of Kedāram from the Tamil south: an early sign of the jyotirliṅgas as an all-India network.

🕉️ Ādi Śaṅkara

Credited with reviving the shrine, and remembered at the samādhi behind it; Jyotirmaṭh, his northern seat, lies near Badrīnāth.

🏔️ Char Dhām

With Yamunotrī, Gaṅgotrī and Badrīnāth, Kedāranātha forms Uttarakhand's Char Dhām, the "Dev Bhūmi" of the Himālaya.

📜 Kedāra Khaṇḍa

A section of the Skanda Purāṇa devoted to the region, the source of many of its legends and tīrthas.

🥁 Garhwali faith

The doli processions with drums and Army bands, and the songs of the hill people, give Kedāra its living voice.

🎬 Modern memory

The 2013 flood and the temple's survival have entered films, books and songs across India.
