VAIDYANĀTHA

THE 12 JYOTIRLIṄGAS · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The Lord of physicians: legends, history, rituals and festivals at Deoghar and Parli · explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: A one-page summary first, then maps, the stotram line and the question of where Vaidyanātha is, the legends, the history of each shrine, the temples today, rituals, festivals, the kāṃvaṛ walk, the shrines around, and the culture of the pilgrims. Keep it as a reference.

FOREWORD

Vaidyanātha means "the Lord of physicians". The story says Śiva healed the wounded Rāvaṇa here, and the liṅga he gave him never reached Laṅkā: it was set down on the way, and stayed.

Where exactly it stayed is a question devotees have long answered differently. In the east, Deoghar in Jharkhand, "Bābā Dhām", draws lakhs of saffron-clad pilgrims who walk over a hundred kilometres with Gaṅgā water every Śrāvaṇa. In the west, Parli Vaijnāth in Maharashtra points to the stotram's own word, paralyām. In the Himalaya, Baijnāth in Himachal has its own ancient temple and claim.

This sixth guide in our series honours all of them. It sets out the legends, the history of each shrine, and the reasons each is revered, without taking sides, because for the pilgrim at each door, the Lord inside is the same.

May every reader one day hear "Bol Bam!" on the road to Vaidyanātha, wherever they believe that road ends.

WHERE IT IS

Three shrines across India

Deoghar, "the abode of the gods", is in the Santhāl Parganās of Jharkhand. Old Sanskrit texts call the area Haritakīvana or Ketakīvana. Its Gaṅgā water comes from Sultānganj in Bihar, where the river flows north.

Parli Vaijnāth is a town in Beed district of Marāṭhwāḍā, Maharashtra; its temple stands on a low hill. Parli is also called Kāntipur or Vaijayantī in local tradition.

Baijnāth lies in the Kangra valley of Himachal Pradesh, under the Dhauladhār range.

IN THE JYOTIRLIṄGA STOTRAM

One word, two readings
paralyāṃ vaidyanāthaṃ caḍākinyāṃ bhīmaśaṅkaram |setubandhe tu rāmeśaṃnāgeśaṃ dārukāvane ‖
What it says

"Vaidyanātha at Paralī, Bhīmaśaṅkara in Ḍākinī, Rāmeśa at Setubandha, and Nāgeśa in the Dāruka forest."

WordMeans
vaidyaphysician, healer
nāthalord: Vaidyanātha is "Lord of physicians"
paralyāmat Paralī: read by Parli's devotees as their town's name
citābhūmau"at the cremation ground": a reading Deoghar's tradition cites, linking the place with Satī

The text debate. The short stotram as commonly printed says paralyām, which supports Parli. Deoghar's tradition points to other verses that place Vaidyanātha "at the cremation ground" (citābhūmau), where Satī's body was cremated and her heart fell, and to the longer stotram's fifth verse, which locates him in the north-east (pūrvottare), "dwelling always with Girijā, whose lotus feet gods and demons worship": śrī-vaidyanāthaṃ tam ahaṃ namāmi.

Why it matters little to pilgrims. Texts were copied by hand for centuries, with regional variants. Both shrines are ancient places of worship; both are visited as jyotirliṅgas.

Fifth in the stotram: Vaidyanātha, the healer, honoured as a jyotirliṅga in more than one place.

No final word. There is no single authority on which shrine is "the" jyotirliṅga, and none of the three has given up its claim. Popular lists across North and East India usually name Deoghar; Maharashtra's lists usually name Parli. Many pilgrims visit both.

This article takes no side. Similar questions arise for Nāgeśvara and Bhīmaśaṅkara; we treat each in its own guide.

ZAPPY: Doesn't the disagreement bother people?

PROF. BOLT: Less than you'd think. A jyotirliṅga is light, not a postal address. Each place has kept the flame burning for centuries.

THE LEGENDS · I

Rāvaṇa and the physician

Rāvaṇa's penance. The Śiva Purāṇa tells that Rāvaṇa, king of Laṅkā and a great devotee, did fierce austerities to bring Śiva to Laṅkā. When Śiva did not appear, Rāvaṇa began cutting off his heads, one by one, as offerings.

The healer. After nine heads, Śiva appeared and stopped him. He restored the heads and healed Rāvaṇa's wounds like a physician (vaidya): hence Vaidyanātha. Śiva gave him a liṅga to take home, with one condition: once set down on the ground, it would stay there forever.

The gods' worry. The gods feared that with Śiva in Laṅkā, Rāvaṇa would be unbeatable. As Rāvaṇa flew south, Varuṇa (or the Gaṅgā) entered his body, and he was seized by an overwhelming need to relieve himself.

The cowherd. Viṣṇu appeared as a cowherd boy, often named Baiju. Rāvaṇa handed him the liṅga for a moment. The "boy" set it down, and vanished. When Rāvaṇa returned, he could not lift it. In anger he pressed it with his thumb, leaving a dent that devotees still point to. He worshipped it and went home empty-handed.

🩺 Vaidya

Śiva the healer: devotees pray here for recovery from illness. The Matsya Purāṇa calls the place "Ārogya Vaidyanātha".

📍 Harilā Joṛī

Deoghar's tradition places the moment of the handover at Harilā Joṛī, a few kilometres north of the temple.

Even Rāvaṇa's devotion was answered with healing, not refusal. But the story also says: what is given for the whole world cannot be carried off for one.

ZAPPY: The same trick happens in other stories, doesn't it?

PROF. BOLT: Yes: at Gokarṇa in Karnataka, the ātmaliṅga, with Gaṇeśa as the boy. India loves this story enough to tell it in many places.

THE LEGENDS · II

Satī's heart, the cowherd Baiju, and Mārkaṇḍeya

Hṛdaya Pīṭha. When Śiva wandered the universe carrying the body of Satī, Viṣṇu's discus cut it apart, and her body fell in pieces across India, each place becoming a Śakti Pīṭha. Deoghar's tradition holds that Satī's heart fell here, making Bābā Dhām both a jyotirliṅga and a Śakti Pīṭha: Śiva and Śakti together. That is why the two temples are tied with red cloth.

Baiju the cowherd. A Deoghar tradition, strong among the Santhāl people, tells of a cowherd named Baiju who found the forgotten liṅga in the forest and worshipped it with stubborn devotion. The Lord took his name: "Baijnāth".

Parli's amṛta. Parli's tradition says that at the churning of the ocean, the nectar of immortality and the divine physician Dhanvantari were hidden in this liṅga. That is why devotees at Parli may touch it and why it is a place to pray for health.

Mārkaṇḍeya. Parli also claims the boy-sage Mārkaṇḍeya, fated to die at sixteen, who clung to the liṅga when Yama came for him; Śiva struck Yama down and made the boy immortal. (Other places, too, claim this story.)

HISTORY · I

Deoghar: Bābā Dhām
Antiquity The origin of the shrine is lost in time. Sanskrit texts know the area as Haritakīvana, the forest of haritakī trees.
1266 The Gidhaur princely line is founded (by tradition under Rājā Bīr Vikram Singh); its rulers keep a close tie with the temple for centuries.
1596 Pūran Mal, an ancestor of the Mahārājās of Gidhaur, builds or repairs the front part of the temple, recorded in an inscription. The original builder is not known.
Inscription The inscription says he built it at the request of the priest Raghunāth Ojhā; local tradition adds that the priest disliked Pūran Mal putting up his name.
1630–1813 The other temples of the complex are built by various patrons, giving the 22 shrines seen today.
1782 The English painter William Hodges paints the temple in oil, one of its earliest European images.
Much of the early history comes from temple tradition and a few inscriptions; dates before 1596 are not documented.

HISTORY · II

Deoghar today, Parli and Baijnāth
Deoghar The temple is now run by the Baba Baidyanath Temple Management Board under the Jharkhand government; the hereditary paṇḍās (priest-guides) continue to serve pilgrims.
2022 Deoghar airport opens, and the town is linked to India's air network; numbers at the Śrāvaṇī Melā continue to grow.
Parli: 12th–13th c. Maharashtra Tourism dates Parli's temple to the Yādava dynasty of Devagiri; tradition credits their minister Hemādri (Hemāḍpant).
Parli: 18th c. Ahilyābāī Holkar restores the temple, bringing special stone for the work. (A widely repeated date of "1706" cannot be right: Ahilyābāī lived 1725–95.)
Baijnāth: 1204 The Baijnāth temple in Kangra is built in the Nāgara style; inscriptions record its building by two merchants and praise Vaidyanātha.

The Ahilyābāī pattern. Parli joins Kāśī, Somanātha, Oṃkāreśvara and many others restored by the queen of Indore: by the late 18th century, half of the great Śiva shrines of India carried her mark.

Living tradition. At all three places, worship has continued in some form for centuries, whatever the written record shows.

Baijnāth's builders are named in published inscriptions as the merchants Āhuka and Manyuka; this and the 1204 date are as commonly reported.

BĀBĀ DHĀM TODAY

Deoghar's temple complex

🔺 The main temple

A tall, plain, pyramid-shaped stone śikhara, about 72 ft high, topped by gold kalaśas and a pañcaśūla, a five-pronged trident, instead of the usual three.

🎀 Gaṭhbandhan

Long red cloth ties run from the pañcaśūla of Śiva's temple to the spire of Pārvatī's: the "marriage knot" of Śiva and Śakti. Couples sponsor new ties for a happy marriage.

🪨 The liṅga

Small and cylindrical, its top broken or dented, which tradition links with Rāvaṇa's thumb.

🛕 The 22

Shrines to Pārvatī, Kālī, Annapūrṇā, Lakṣmī-Nārāyaṇa, Gaṇeśa, Rāma and others ring the courtyard.

Pañcaśūla: the five prongs are explained in tradition as Śiva's five faces, or the five elements; on Mahāśivarātri eve the pañcaśūlas are taken down, worshipped and replaced.

PARLI VAIJNĀTH TODAY

The hilltop temple of Marāṭhwāḍā

🏯 The temple

A stone temple on a low hill, enclosed by a walled courtyard and approached by a broad flight of steps; built in the Deccan style with a fortress-like enclosure.

🤲 Touch the liṅga

At Parli, devotees of every background may go in and touch the liṅga themselves, a custom the temple is known for.

💧 Harihara tīrtha

Water from the nearby Harihara tīrtha is brought for the daily worship of the liṅga, by tradition.

🪔 The lamp tower

A tall dīpamāḷ (lamp tower) outside the temple, with niches for lamps, typical of Maharashtra's temples.

ZAPPY: Deoghar has the crowds; what does Parli have?

PROF. BOLT: Quiet stone, an open sanctum, and the stotram's own word on its gate. Many find that just as powerful.

A DAY AT BĀBĀ DHĀM

The rituals of Deoghar
RitualWhenWhat happens
Kāñcā jal pūjāBefore dawn, as the doors openThe first worship of the day with "raw" (fresh) water, before the public enters.
Ṣoḍaśopacāra pūjāEarly morningThe full sixteen-part worship by the temple priests.
JalārpaṇaThrough the dayPilgrims pour Gaṅgā water, milk and bilva leaves on the liṅga. In the peak season, water is offered through an "arghā" channel that carries it to the liṅga.
Śṛṅgāra pūjāEveningThe adornment of the liṅga with flowers, sandal and a crown-like decoration; darśan of the decorated Lord.
Śayana āratiNightThe last ārati; the temple closes for the night.

The paṇḍās. Deoghar's hereditary paṇḍās guide pilgrims through the worship and keep family record books (bahī) going back generations: a pilgrim can often find the names of grandparents who came before.

Mahāśivarātri. On the eve of Śivarātri, the pañcaśūlas on the temples are taken down, worshipped and put back, and a grand wedding procession of Śiva, the "Śiv bārāt", winds through the town.

The temple's hours change across the year, and especially during the Śrāvaṇī Melā; times are not given here because they change. Parli's rituals follow the Maharashtra pattern, with abhiṣeka, a noon offering and evening ārati.

PROF. BOLT: They think of Pārvatī as their daughter, so Bābā is their son-in-law. Remember the Kannada pilgrims at Śrīśaila? Same love, different family.

THE KĀṂVAṚ YĀTRĀ

105 km for a pot of water

Every Śrāvaṇa, pilgrims in saffron, called kāṃvaṛiyās or simply "Bam", bathe at Sultānganj in Bihar, where the Gaṅgā turns and flows north (an especially holy sign), and fill pots with its water. Slinging them from a decorated pole on the shoulder, the kāṃvaṛ, they walk about 105 km barefoot to Deoghar, chanting "Bol Bam!", and pour the water on Bābā Baidyanāth.

Rules of the road. The kāṃvaṛ must not touch the ground until the water is offered; it is hung on special stands at rest stops. Many go on to Basukināth in Dumka district to complete the pilgrimage.

🚶 Ordinary Bam

Walk in three to four days, resting at camps on the way.

⚡ Ḍāk Bam

Run or walk without stopping, finishing within 24 hours.

🙇 Daṇḍī Bam

Measure the road with their bodies, prostrating length by length.

The water is carried, not the pilgrim. For a hundred kilometres, the whole self is in service of one small pot: that is the teaching of the kāṃvaṛ.

AROUND THE SHRINES

Deoghar's circle, and Parli's neighbours

🔱 Basukināth

In Dumka district: a much-loved Śiva temple, traditionally visited after Bābā Dhām; tradition calls it the "court" where wishes are heard.

📍 Harilā Joṛī

North of Deoghar: where, tradition says, Rāvaṇa handed over the liṅga.

🧘 Tapovan

Hills and caves where sages are said to have meditated; a Śiva temple and Hanumān shrine.

⛰️ Trikūṭa

A three-peaked hill near Deoghar with a Śiva shrine, linked in local story with Rāvaṇa.

🛕 Naulakhā Mandir

A 20th-century temple of Rādhā-Kṛṣṇa, said to have cost nine lakh rupees: hence its name.

🕊️ Satsaṅg Āśram

The centre of Śrī Śrī Ṭhākur Anukūlcandra's Satsaṅg movement, in Deoghar since 1946.

🌺 Ambājogāī

Near Parli: the temple of Yogeśvarī (Jogāī), family goddess of many Marathi families.

🐍 Auṇḍhā Nāgnāth

In Hingoli district, Maharashtra: a claimant to the Nāgeśvara jyotirliṅga (see that guide).

Baijnāth in Himachal sits in the Kangra valley, near Pālampur and the Dhauladhār range.

VAIDYANĀTHA IN CULTURE

Walkers, priests and kin

🧡 Bol Bam

The chant of the kāṃvaṛiyās has become a whole culture: songs, tea-stall camps, volunteers who wash pilgrims' feet along the road.

📚 The paṇḍā books

Deoghar's priestly families keep pilgrim registers (bahī) by village and clan, going back generations: living family histories.

💍 Mithilā's son-in-law

Maithil pilgrims, with their own kāṃvaṛs, dress and songs, treat Bābā as family: the Basant Pañcamī tilak is their privilege.

🌿 The Santhāl story

The tale of Baiju the cowherd keeps alive the place of the forest peoples of the Santhāl Parganās in the shrine's story.

🖼️ William Hodges

The painter who travelled with Warren Hastings's circle painted the temple in 1782, when it stood among palms and huts.

🩺 The healer's shrine

At all three places, people come especially to pray for the sick. The name itself means healer.

ZAPPY: The priests keep records of my great-grandparents?

PROF. BOLT: If they came, quite possibly. Tell the paṇḍā your village and family name, and he may open a very old book.
