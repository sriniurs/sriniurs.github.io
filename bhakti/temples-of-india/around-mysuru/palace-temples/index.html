THE PALACE TEMPLES

INSIDE THE MYSURU PALACE FORT · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Five centuries of a royal family’s gods

FOREWORD

Millions come to Mysuru to see the palace lit up at night. Few notice the temples around it: a dozen shrines tucked along the fort walls, some older than the palace by four hundred years.

Here a five-year-old king was crowned, a white Boar arrived from Tamil Nadu, a captive queen smuggled in a god, and the last Maharaja built two temples to the Goddess.

This guide walks you round the fort, temple by temple.

How many? Sources speak of about twelve temples in and around the fort. Most are protected monuments of the ASI. They span 450 years, from a grant of 1499 to the last temple of 1953.

ZAPPY: Temples inside a palace? Isn't the palace the main show?

PROF. BOLT: For tourists, yes. For the kings, the temples came first! The palace burned and was rebuilt; the gods stayed put.

THE FORT AND ITS TEMPLES

Where they stand

The fort. The palace stands inside a walled fort with gates on each side. The temples sit along its edges, so the royal house is ringed by its gods.

Pairs. The palace’s own description pairs them: Bhuvaneśvarī in the north faces Śvetavarāha in the south, and Gāyatrī answers Trinayaneśvara in the south-east.

WHY A PALACE FULL OF TEMPLES?

Kings who called Kṛṣṇa their ancestor
Yadu lineageThe Woḍeyars trace their descent from the Yadu race of Kṛṣṇa; one king built a Kṛṣṇa temple just for that reason
CoronationsRoyal rites were held in the palace temples: Kṛṣṇarāja Woḍeyar III was crowned with ceremonies at Lakṣmīramaṇa
ProtectionTradition says temples were placed at the fort’s gates and corners to guard the palace
DasaraMysuru Dasara has been celebrated at the palace every year since the early 19th century, ending in the Vijayadaśamī procession of the goddess Cāmuṇḍeśvarī
Fire and rebuildingThe old wooden palace burned in 1897; the present palace by Henry Irwin was finished in 1912. The temples are older than the palace you see today

ZAPPY: So the temples are older than the palace?

PROF. BOLT: Most of them! The palace is barely a hundred years old. Lakṣmīramaṇa's first record is over five hundred.

LAKṢMĪRAMAṆA

The oldest temple in Mysuru
1499 An inscription found at Banni Maṇṭapa (Cole’s Gardens) records a grant to Lakṣmīramaṇa: the earliest record of a temple in the city. One account credits Cāmarāja Woḍeyar II, another Tuḷuva Narasa Nāyaka of Vijayanagara.
c. 1599 Royal annals tell of a half-blind Brahmin cured here through Rāja Woḍeyar’s intercession.
1638–59 Narasarāja Woḍeyar I adds the maṇḍapa.
30 June 1799 The coronation rites of the five-year-old Kṛṣṇarāja Woḍeyar III are held here, after the fall of Tipu.
1851 Kṛṣṇarāja Woḍeyar III raises the gopura over the main gate.

The Lord. Viṣṇu as Nambi Nārāyaṇa, with Lakṣmī: “Lakṣmī’s beloved”. The temple stands in the western part of the fort.

TRINAYANEŚVARA

The three-eyed Lord who came inside the walls
DeityŚiva as Trinayaneśvara, “Lord of the three eyes”
AgeAn ancient temple that stood before Rāja Woḍeyar’s time (before 1578; one account says 1569)
Outside, then insideIt once stood outside the fort, on the bank of the great tank Dēvarāya Sāgara (Dōḍḍakere). When Kaṇṭhīrava Narasarāja and Doḍḍa Dēvarāja enlarged the fort, it came inside the walls
WhereThe south-east corner of the fort
StyleDravidian

ZAPPY: A temple that moved without moving?

PROF. BOLT: The temple stayed exactly where it was. The palace walls came to it!

ŚVETAVARĀHA · I

The white Boar’s journey
From Śrīmuṣṇam. King Cikka Dēvarāja Woḍeyar (r. 1673–1704) obtained a stone image of Śvetavarāha, “the white Boar”, from Śrīmuṣṇam in Tamil Nadu, one of Viṣṇu’s self-manifested shrines.
To the capital. He installed it in a new temple at Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa, then his capital. A processional image there still carries an inscription naming him as donor.
A temple lost. Later accounts, including the Mysore Gazetteer, say that temple was demolished in Tipu Sultan’s time.
To Mysuru. After 1799 the capital returned to Mysuru, and in 1809 the image was installed in the present temple by the south gate, built by Dīvān Pūrṇayya at the wish of Kṛṣṇarāja Woḍeyar III (with the guidance, one account says, of Queen Lakṣmammaṇṇi).

ŚVETAVARĀHA · II

A Hoysaḷa-style temple of 1809
StyleBuilt in the Hoysaḷa manner: moulded basement, ornate doorframe, carved pillars in the maṇḍapa
Stone from afarThe Gazetteer records that Pūrṇayya used materials from a ruined Hoysaḷa building in Shivamogga district
MuralsThe inner hall’s walls carry Mysuru-style paintings of the Rāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata
TeachersInscribed images of the Śrīvaiṣṇava ācāryas Vedānta Deśika and a Jīyar
Twin shrineBeside it stands Ambujavallī Mahālakṣmī, the Goddess’s own temple
The gateThe south gate of the fort is called the Varāha gate after it

Sources differ. The ASI dates the temple’s style to Cikka Dēvarāja’s reign; most accounts give 1809 for the present building and the installation.

KILLE VEṄKAṬARAMAṆA

The queen’s dream
A dynasty in eclipse. By 1760 Hyder Ali held real power in Mysuru, and under Tipu Sultan the Woḍeyar family lived in confinement.
The dream. Queen Lakṣmammaṇṇi, wife of Kṛṣṇarāja Woḍeyar II, dreamt that Lord Veṅkaṭaramaṇa told her to bring his image from the temple at Bāḷamuri and install it at Mysuru. If she did, the family would rule again.
Secretly. She had the image brought without the Sultan’s knowledge.
Restored. In 1799, after Tipu’s death, the British restored the Woḍeyars to the throne. The temple of the fort, Kille Veṅkaṭaramaṇa, keeps the story.

The same queen’s secret letters to the British are part of the Melukōṭe story in our Tipu Sultan special.

PRASANNA KṚṢṆA

A temple for the family’s ancestor
WhyThe Woḍeyars claim descent from the Yadu race of Kṛṣṇa. Kṛṣṇarāja Woḍeyar III was saddened that there was no Kṛṣṇa temple in the palace, and built one
WhenBegun in 1825, completed in 1829, as an inscription records
The LordA charming crawling child Kṛṣṇa, Bāla Kṛṣṇa, in chlorite schist
BronzesAbout 40 bronze images of gods and saints in the side shrines
MuralsThe pillared hall is painted with scenes from the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, in the 19th-century Mysuru style
FestivalJanmāṣṭamī, Kṛṣṇa’s birthday, is celebrated here every year

ZAPPY: A king built a temple to his great-great-great... grandfather?

PROF. BOLT: Many greats! If your family line goes back to Kṛṣṇa, you'd want a temple for him at home too.

BHUVANEŚVARĪ

A temple of 1951
Built1951, by Jayacāmarājendra Woḍeyar, the last ruling Maharaja, soon after Mysuru joined the Indian Republic
WhereThe northern side of the fort, near the Balarāma gate, facing Śvetavarāha in the south
The GoddessBhuvaneśvarī, “Queen of the worlds”, carved by the sculptor Siddaliṅga (Siddalingaswamy)
TreasureA great copper Sūrya Maṇḍala, the Sun on his chariot, moved here from the palace and used at Ratha Saptamī

A scholar-king. Jayacāmarājendra Woḍeyar was a devoted Śākta, a philosopher and a patron of music; both of the palace’s newest temples are to the Goddess.

GĀYATRĪ

The youngest temple
Built1953, by Jayacāmarājendra Woḍeyar: the youngest temple of the palace
WhereThe east of the fort, beside the Jaya Mārtāṇḍa gate
Three shrinesThe inner sanctum holds three: Sāvitrī, Gāyatrī and Sarasvatī
GāyatrīThe goddess of the Gāyatrī mantra, the most sacred verse of the Vedas

Read our article on the Gāyatrī Mantra.

ZAPPY: Why a temple to a mantra?

PROF. BOLT: Because Gāyatrī is both: the most sacred verse, and the Goddess who is that verse. A royal thank-you to the Veda itself.

THE KŌḌI SHRINES

Bhairava and Someśvara by the old tank
Kōḍi BhairavaA temple of Śiva as Bhairava, the fierce guardian; its name, the palace says, comes from where it stood
Kōḍi SomeśvaraŚiva as Someśvara, Lord of the Moon
KōḍiIn Kannada, kōḍi is the outlet where a tank overflows; the name probably recalls a site by the overflow of an old tank
GuardiansBhairava temples traditionally guard towns and forts

The older city. If the name is read this way, these small shrines recall a Mysuru of tanks and fields before the grand avenues: when Dōḍḍakere still lapped the fort, and Trinayaneśvara stood outside the walls.

TIMELINE

before 1578 Trinayaneśvara stands outside the fort.

1673–1704 Cikka Dēvarāja brings Śvetavarāha from Śrīmuṣṇam to Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa.
1760–1799 Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan; Queen Lakṣmammaṇṇi brings Veṅkaṭaramaṇa from Bāḷamuri.

1809 Śvetavarāha installed at the south gate.
1825–29 Prasanna Kṛṣṇa built.
1851 Lakṣmīramaṇa’s gopura.
1897–1912 The old palace burns; the new palace rises.

THE PALACE TEMPLES ON ONE PAGE

Everything in five lines
The oldest. Lakṣmīramaṇa (grant of 1499), where Kṛṣṇarāja III was crowned; Trinayaneśvara, older than the walls around it.
The travellers. Śvetavarāha from Śrīmuṣṇam via Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa (1809); Veṅkaṭaramaṇa from Bāḷamuri, brought in secret by a queen.
The family god. Prasanna Kṛṣṇa (1829), for the kings of the Yadu line.
The Goddesses. Bhuvaneśvarī (1951) and Gāyatrī (1953), the last temples of the last Maharaja.
The guardians. The Kōḍi shrines of Bhairava and Someśvara.
