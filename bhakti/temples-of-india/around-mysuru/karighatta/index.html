KARIGHAṬṬA

ŚRĪ ŚRĪNIVĀSA · CHIKKA TIRUPATI · A REFERENCE GUIDE
A hill from Tirumala, beside the Kāverī

FOREWORD

Just outside Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa, one dark hill rises alone from the green fields, where the little Lokapāvanī meets the Kāverī.

On its summit stands Śrī Śrīnivāsa, the Lord of the Seven Hills, and devotees call the place Chikka Tirupati. The legends say the hill itself came from Tirumala.

This guide tells the stories of the hill, its sages and its Lord.

Govinda Govinda,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: A hill that flew in from Tirupati?

PROF. BOLT: That's the legend! Monkeys, sages, an elephant and an angry rishi: this little hill has more stories than steps.

WHERE IT IS

A lone hill above a confluence

The hill. Karighaṭṭa rises alone from the paddy fields just outside the island town of Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa. Its slopes carry dry scrub, with tamarind and gooseberry trees around the temple on top.

The rivers. The small Lokapāvanī, “purifier of the world”, flows past the hill and meets the Kāverī at a saṅgama below it.

The view. From the top you can see Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa and Mysuru, the confluence, and the Nimiṣāmbā temple across the Lokapāvanī.

THE LORD OF THE HILL

Śrīnivāsa, Karigirivāsa, Bairāgi Veṅkaṭaramaṇa

🙏 Three Śrīnivāsas

The main image is Vaikuṇṭha Śrīnivāsa, about 6 ft of black stone. Beside him stand Yoga Śrīnivāsa, shown without his consort, and Bhoga Śrīnivāsa.

🌸 Bairāgi

He is called Bairāgi Veṅkaṭaramaṇa because when he is decorated with flowers, he looks like a bairāgi, a wandering ascetic.

The Goddess. Padmāvatī, consort of Śrīnivāsa, has her own shrine on the western side. A Garuḍa statue and pillar face the main shrine, and behind the temple are the Lord’s footprints, the Viṣṇu-pāda.

THE LEGENDS · I

The hill that came from Tirumala
Nīlācala. The Varāha Purāṇa calls this hill Nīlācala, “the blue mountain”. Tradition says it was once one of the Seven Hills of Tirumala.
Sugrīva’s version. When Rāma’s army was building the bridge to Laṅkā, Sugrīva uprooted Nīlācala and flew south with it. Over the Kāverī confluence, Viṣṇu’s devotees doing penance there begged him to leave it, and he set it down.
Hanumān’s version. Another telling says Hanumān, sent for the Sañjīvanī mountain to save Lakṣmaṇa, took Nīlācala by mistake. The sages at the saṅgama told him the healing herbs were in the Himālaya, so he left this hill here.
So, Chikka Tirupati. A piece of Tirumala, with Śrīnivāsa on top: chikka is Kannada for “small”.

ZAPPY: Sugrīva or Hanumān: which one is right?

PROF. BOLT: Both are told! Different tellings, same point: this hill is a piece of the Lord's own mountain.

THE LEGENDS · II

Kari the elephant, and the sage Kutsa
The elephant. A wild elephant named Kari attacked four girls bathing by the river, devotees of Śrīnivāsa, and killed them.
The sage. The sage Kutsa (Kutsamuni) performed penance and prayed to the Lord to bring them back to life and to protect his devotees.
The name. The hill is said to take its name from the elephant: Kari-ghaṭṭa, “elephant hill”. Others read kari as “black”, for the dark rock: “the black hill”.

ZAPPY: Elephant hill or black hill?

PROF. BOLT: In Kannada, kari can mean either! The legend says elephant; the rock says black. We'll let the hill keep both.

THE LEGENDS · III

Bhṛgu’s curse and Bhṛgu’s prayer
A war. In a war between gods and demons, the fleeing demons took shelter with Khyāti, wife of the sage Bhṛgu, who would not give them up. Viṣṇu struck her down with his discus.
The curse. Bhṛgu cursed Viṣṇu to be born on earth and suffer separation from his wife. Viṣṇu accepted it, and did penance under an aśvattha tree on Nīlācala.
Narasiṃha. Lakṣmī Narasiṃha appeared there, and told Bhṛgu that he too must atone, for cursing the Lord.
The prayer. Bhṛgu did penance on the banks of the Maṇikarṇikā river. Pleased, the Lord blessed him, and Bhṛgu asked him to stay on this hill for his devotees. So Śrīnivāsa came to Karighaṭṭa, and tradition says it was Bhṛgu who installed the image.

SAGES OF NĪLĀCALA

Tales from the Purāṇas and tradition
CandraThe Moon, who had wronged his teacher Bṛhaspati by loving his wife Tārā, did penance by the Paścimavāhinī and had darśan of the Lord on Nīlācala every day
BodhāyanaThe sage did penance here and became fit to compose the Bodhāyana Dharma-sūtras
VaikhānasaHeld the first chariot festival for Śrīnivāsa, in Kumbha month under the star Uttara Phalgunī: the festival still held today
The darbha grassThe sharp grass on the hill, darbhe hullu in Kannada, used in sacred rites: the Varāha Purāṇa says it grew from hairs that fell when Viṣṇu as Varāha, the boar, shook himself

ZAPPY: Even the grass has a legend?

PROF. BOLT: Of course! Priests all over use darbha in rituals. Here they say it came straight from the Lord's own boar-form.

THE TEMPLE

On the summit

🪜 The climb

A long flight of stone steps, or a winding road, leads to the flat summit. Sources count the steps differently, from about 300 to 450.

🚪 Inside

Huge wooden doors open into a large quadrangle with the main shrine of the three Śrīnivāsas, Garuḍa facing them, and Padmāvatī to the west.

Also on the summit: the Lord’s footprints behind the temple, a shrine of Hanumān, and a Kalyāṇa Maṇḍapa where weddings are held.

HISTORY

Legend, tradition and the siege
Tretā Yuga By tradition: Nīlācala set down at the saṅgama; Vaikhānasa’s first chariot festival.
Purāṇas The Varāha Purāṇa names the hill Nīlācala and tells of its grass; tradition cites the Padma Purāṇa for the tales of Candra and Bodhāyana.
Bhṛgu Tradition says the sage installed the image.
1792 Third Anglo-Mysore War: Tipu Sultan holds a post on the hill, which British records call Carigat or Karigaut Hill. Lord Cornwallis’s army attacks it during the siege of Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa, and later pulls his command back to the hill.
1799 The final siege, in which Tipu dies. Local legend says British cannons stood on Karighaṭṭa; the records we found confirm only 1792.
Today A living hill shrine with its yearly car festival and a steady stream of pilgrims.

A note on dates. Unlike the great island temples nearby, Karighaṭṭa has no dated inscriptions in the sources we used. Its story is carried by Purāṇa and local tradition, so this guide does not give it a building date.

ZAPPY: A battlefield on a temple hill?

PROF. BOLT: It's the highest point around: a perfect view of Tipu's fort. In 1792 the British wrote it into their war records as Carigat Hill.

THE CAR FESTIVAL

Vaikhānasa’s chariot

Since the Tretā Yuga. Tradition says the sage Vaikhānasa held the first chariot festival for Śrīnivāsa, in Kumbha month under the star Uttara Phalgunī, and that it has been held every year since.

When. The annual car festival and fair fall in Kumbha and Phālguna, roughly February–March, and draw pilgrims from all around Mysuru and Mandya.

Why Vaikhānasa? Vaikhānasa is the sage of the Vaikhānasa Āgama, the tradition of temple worship followed at Tirumala: fitting for a “little Tirupati”.

CHIKKA TIRUPATI

Why devotees call it little Tirupati
The hillBy legend, Nīlācala is one of Tirumala’s seven hills, carried here in the Rāmāyaṇa age
The LordThe same Śrīnivāsa (Veṅkaṭeśvara) as on Tirumala, with Padmāvatī as at Tiruchanur
The sageBhṛgu, whose quarrel with Viṣṇu is also at the heart of Tirumala’s own story
The festivalA chariot festival in the tradition of Vaikhānasa, sage of Tirumala’s mode of worship

Two Bhṛgu stories. At Tirumala the best-known tale is of Bhṛgu kicking Viṣṇu’s chest, Lakṣmī leaving, and Viṣṇu coming to earth as Śrīnivāsa. At Karighaṭṭa the story is of Bhṛgu’s curse over Khyāti. Both tie the sage to the Lord’s coming to earth.

ZAPPY: So I can pray here instead of going to Tirupati?

PROF. BOLT: Devotees around Mysuru have done just that for generations. The Lord is the same; only the queue is shorter!

AROUND KARIGHAṬṬA

The confluence and its temples
The saṅgamaThe meeting of the Lokapāvanī and the Kāverī below the hill
NimiṣāmbāAcross the Lokapāvanī: the Goddess as Pārvatī, believed to clear her devotees’ troubles in a moment (nimiṣa)
Ādi RaṅgaRaṅganātha on the island of Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa, first of the Triraṅga
From the summitŚrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa, Mysuru city and the Cāmuṇḍī hills on a clear day

Viṣṇu reclining, Viṣṇu standing. In one short stretch of the Kāverī you can see the Lord lying on Ādiśeṣa at Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa and standing on his hill at Karighaṭṭa.

More temples around Mysuru: Nañjanagūḍu, Ādi Raṅga and Madhya Raṅga.

Everything in five lines
