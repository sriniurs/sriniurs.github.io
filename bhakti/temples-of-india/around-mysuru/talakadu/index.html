The temples under the sand

FOREWORD

On a bend of the Kāverī, in the green heart of Karnataka, there is a desert. Under its dunes lies a royal city of more than thirty temples.

This is Talakāḍu: capital of the Gaṅgas, prize of the Cōḻas and Hoysaḷas, home of five liṅgas of Śiva, and the place where a queen’s curse is said to have come true.

This guide tells its legends, its temples, its history, and what science says about the sand.

WHERE IT IS

A river bend full of sand

The bend. Talakāḍu lies on the left bank of the Kāverī, south-east of Mysuru, at the point where the eastward-flowing river changes course.

The sand. Here the river spreads sand over a wide area. The old city lies under dunes nearly a mile long; once, only the tops of two gopurams showed above them.

The five liṅgas. Three stand in the dunes around the old town; Arkeśvara is at Vijayapura nearby, and Mallikārjuna on the hill of Mudukutore.

THE NAME

Tala, Kāḍu and the elephants
Two hunters. Tradition tells of twin brothers of the Kirāta (hunter) people, Tala and Kāḍu, who found an image of Śiva in a tree being worshipped by wild elephants.
The elephants were sages. The elephants were really ṛṣis in disguise. All of them, the brothers too, attained liberation, and the place took the brothers’ names: Tala-kāḍu.
Other names. In Sanskrit it became Daḷavana (Daḷavanapura); the forest was Gajāraṇya, the elephant forest. Tradition also says Rāma halted here on his way to Laṅkā.

THE FIVE LIṄGAS

Pañcaliṅga: the five faces of Śiva
LiṅgaAlso calledWhere
VaidyeśvaraVaidyanātheśvara, the healer; with the Goddess ManonmaṇīTalakāḍu, the chief temple
PātāleśvaraVāsukīśvaraTalakāḍu, in the dunes
MaraḷeśvaraSaikateśvara, “Lord of the sand”Talakāḍu, in the dunes
ArkeśvaraLord of the SunVijayapura, nearby
Mallikārjunawith the Goddess BhramarāmbāMudukutore hill, nearby

Five faces. The five are held to stand for the five faces of Śiva (Sadyojāta, Vāmadeva, Aghora, Tatpuruṣa and Īśāna). One account names Arkeśvara as the Tatpuruṣa face and Mallikārjuna as the Vāmadeva face.

Sand and serpent. Pātāleśvara is the Lord of the underworld, Vāsuki’s Lord; Maraḷeśvara, Saikateśvara, is the Lord of the sand itself.

THE PAÑCALIṄGA DARŚANA

A festival that waits for the stars
WhenOn the new-moon day of Kārtika, when it falls on a Monday and the auspicious Kuhu yoga coincides with the right star (Viśākhā in most accounts)
How oftenNot on a fixed cycle: gaps of 3, 5, 7, 9 or 12 years are described
Recent2006, 2009, 2013 and 2020 (the last held quietly, during the pandemic)
NextAfter 2020, officials named 2033 as the next
The roundPilgrims bathe in the Kāverī and at the Gokarṇa tīrtha, worship Gokarṇeśvara, then visit all five liṅgas in a single auspicious window
Sand clearedBefore each Darśana, the sand is cleared from the buried temples so they can be reached

Sources differ on the exact star (Viśākhā, Jyeṣṭhā), on whether the day is the new moon or full moon, and on the cycle. All agree it is rare, falls in Kārtika, and draws huge crowds.

ZAPPY: It only happens when the stars line up?

PROF. BOLT: And the calendar, and the weekday! Miss one, and you may wait seven years or more.

VAIDYEŚVARA

The healer, and the grandest temple
The LordVaidyeśvara, Śiva the physician; his Goddess is Manonmaṇī
DatesConsecrated in the 10th century, with additions up to the 14th
StyleThe largest and most ornate temple of the group, mixing Gaṅga, Cōḻa and Hoysaḷa features
GuardiansA pair of 10-ft dvārapālas at the entrance of the dance hall
The enclosureA large prākāra wall with sculptures from Gaṅga, Hoysaḷa and Vijayanagara times; a five-headed serpent carved with a stone chain

The sick prince. It was to this temple that the Vijayanagara viceroy came to pray for a cure, the start of Alamelamma’s story.

KĪRTINĀRĀYAṆA

A victory temple of 1117
Built1117, by the Hoysaḷa king Viṣṇuvardhana, after defeating the Cōḻas at Talakāḍu
TitleViṣṇuvardhana took the title Talakāḍugoṇḍa, “conqueror of Talakāḍu”
The imageA tall standing Nārāyaṇa, about 2–2.5 m, on a Garuḍa pedestal
PlanA star-shaped (stellate) sanctum, an open vestibule and a hall with three entrances, on a raised platform (jagati)
RescuedExcavated from the dunes, its great gateway recovered; taken apart and rebuilt by the ASI

ZAPPY: Built for a victory?

PROF. BOLT: Kīrti means fame. Kīrtinārāyaṇa: Viṣṇu of the famous victory. Kings liked to say thank you in stone!

HISTORY

Capital, prize and ruin
c. 350–999 The Western Gaṅgas; tradition says Harivarma lived at Daḷavanapura, and later Gaṅga kings were crowned here when it became their capital.
Early 1000s The Cōḻas defeat the Gaṅgas, take Talakāḍu and rename it Rājarājapura.
1116–17 The Hoysaḷa Viṣṇuvardhana drives out the Cōḻas and builds Kīrtinārāyaṇa. Talakāḍu grows to seven towns and five maṭhas.
14th c. Vijayanagara rule; the minister Mādhavamantri, a native of Talakāḍu, builds an anicut (dam) across the Kāverī.
1610 Rāja Woḍeyar of Mysuru takes Talakāḍu; Alamelamma’s curse.
1600s on The sands advance, burying temple after temple.
1800s–today Temples dug out again: Ānandeśvara and Gaurīśaṅkara in the 19th century; ASI excavations in 1992–93 and after.

ALAMELAMMA’S CURSE

A queen, her jewels and the river
A dying viceroy. Tirumala Rāya (Śrīraṅga Rāya), the Vijayanagara representative at Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa, came to Talakāḍu to pray at Vaidyeśvara for a cure. His wife Alamelamma left Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa in the care of Rāja Woḍeyar of Mysuru and joined him.
The jewels. The story says Rāja Woḍeyar wanted her jewels, and when she would not give them, sent an army. In 1610 he took Talakāḍu.
The river. Alamelamma went to the Kāverī, threw her jewels into the water, and drowned herself opposite Mālaṅgi.
The curse. As she died, the story says, she spoke three lines:
Talakāḍu maraḷāgi, Mālaṅgi maḍuvāgi, Maisūru dhorege makkaḷāgade hōgali.
“May Talakāḍu turn to sand, Mālaṅgi become a whirlpool, and the kings of Mysuru have no heirs.”

A living legend. Popular belief links the sand of Talakāḍu, the whirlpool at Mālaṅgi and the Woḍeyars’ many adoptions of heirs to these words. The tale itself comes from tradition; versions differ on the details.

WHY THE SAND?

Three scientific explanations
The damEcologist K. N. Ganeshaiah argued that the Mādhavamantri anicut of 1336, still standing, changed the river’s flow and sediment, and with deforestation set off an ecological disaster
The windGeologists S. V. Srikantia and T. R. Anantharamu (1997) traced the sand to a point bar on the river’s bend, blown north-east by the south-west monsoon winds and trapped by settlements into migrating dunes
The earthA. B. Roy and M. S. Sethumadhav linked the burial to repeated earthquakes and ground movement in the region
What digs showStratigraphy suggests most of the sand came from the 17th century on: dunes advanced up to 9–10 ft a year, and some Hoysaḷa-period ground lies far below today’s surface

ZAPPY: So the curse wasn't real?

PROF. BOLT: Science gives causes; faith gives meaning. Here the two tell the same story from different ends: in the 1600s the sand began to swallow the town.

DIGGING OUT A CITY

What the sand has given back
ĀnandeśvaraBuilt by Cidānanda Svāmī, a contemporary of Hyder Ali, who is said to have crossed the flooded Kāverī on a plantain leaf
GaurīśaṅkaraA Kannada inscription dates it to Cikka Dēvarāja Woḍeyar (1672–1704)
PātāleśvaraA 10th-century Gaṅga core; four inscriptions on its walls, one Gaṅga-period Kannada and three Tamil
MaraḷeśvaraLike Pātāleśvara, a Gaṅga-period sanctum and vestibule, its tower perhaps a Cōḻa renewal
FindsWalls, inscriptions, drainage pipes below the temple floors, and even a Roman coin mould
Holding back the sandRetaining walls and tree belts have slowed the dunes; the sand still has to be cleared for worship

An archaeologist’s delight. The ASI’s excavations of 1992–93 and later uncovered layer after layer of a town that was busy for more than a thousand years before the dunes came.
