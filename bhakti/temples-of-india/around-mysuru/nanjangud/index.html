NAÑJANAGŪḌU

ŚRĪKAṆṬHEŚVARA · NAÑJUṆḌEŚVARA · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The Lord who drank the poison

FOREWORD

When the ocean was churned, the first thing to rise was not nectar but poison. Śiva drank it, and his throat turned blue.

On the Kapilā, south of Mysuru, that Śiva is worshipped as Nañjuṇḍeśvara, the one who drank the poison, and Śrīkaṇṭheśvara, the one with the blessed throat. His town, Nañjanagūḍu, is called the Kāśī of the South.

This guide tells its legends, its history, and the morning when five chariots roll at once.

Oṃ Namaḥ Śivāya,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Kāśī is in Vārāṇasī. How is this Kāśī?

PROF. BOLT: A holy river, a great Śiva, a bath that washes away sin. Wherever those three meet, devotees see Kāśī.

ZAPPY: A god named after poison?

PROF. BOLT: Named after drinking it! He swallowed the world's poison so nobody else had to. That's the whole story of this temple.

WHERE IT IS

On the Kapilā, south of Mysuru

The river. The temple stands on the right bank of the Kapilā, better known as the Kabini, one of the Kāverī’s great tributaries. Pilgrims bathe at its ghāṭ before darśan.

The confluence. Near the town the Kapilā is joined by the Kauṇḍinya, also called the Gundlu. The meeting place is the saṅgama, known as Paraśurāma Kṣetra.

Why “Kāśī of the South”. As at Kāśī, a great Śiva shrine stands beside a sacred river and a confluence, and a bath and a darśan here are held to wash away sin.

THE LORD WHO DRANK THE POISON

Nañjuṇḍeśvara and Śrīkaṇṭheśvara
The churning. Gods and demons churned the Ocean of Milk for the nectar of immortality, using Mount Mandara as the churning rod and the serpent Vāsuki as the rope.
The poison. Before any treasure came up, a deadly poison, hālāhala, rose from the ocean and began to burn the worlds.
Śiva drinks it. At the gods’ plea, Śiva swallowed it. Pārvatī pressed his throat so it went no further, and his throat turned blue: he became Nīlakaṇṭha.
The names. In Kannada nañju is poison: Nañjuṇḍa is “the one who ate the poison”. In Sanskrit he is Śrīkaṇṭha, “the one with the blessed throat”, and the town is Nañjanagūḍu, “the abode of Nañjuṇḍa”.

ZAPPY: Why is the throat 'blessed' if it's full of poison?

PROF. BOLT: Because it saved the world! The mark of poison is the mark of his compassion. That's why devotees call it śrī, auspicious.

THE LEGENDS · I

Gautama, and the demon Keśī
Gautama’s liṅga. Local tradition holds that the sage Gautama lived here for a time and installed the liṅga. His name lives on in the temple’s greatest chariot, the Gautama Ratha.
Garalapurī. The Śiva Purāṇa calls the place Garalapurī, from garala, poison, the same idea as Nañjuṇḍa.
The demon Keśī. A demon named Keśī, proud of boons from Brahmā and Viṣṇu, tormented the sages. At their prayer Śiva appeared and destroyed him.
A promise. Śiva declared that a part of himself would remain here forever, making Garalapurī a place where sins are removed.

ZAPPY: Same name as Kṛṣṇa's horse-demon Keśī?

PROF. BOLT: Same name, different demon. Purāṇas recycle names a lot: always check which story you're in!

THE LEGENDS · II

Paraśurāma, and Pārvatī’s jewel
A heavy sin. At his father Jamadagni’s command, Paraśurāma struck down his own mother, Reṇukā (in the story she is later restored to life). The sin of it stayed with him.
To Garalapurī. On Nārada’s advice he came here. While clearing the bushes, his axe struck a hidden liṅga, and it bled.
The mud. Śiva told him to press wet mud from the spot onto the wound, and the bleeding stopped. Paraśurāma was freed of his sin, and his own shrine stands at the saṅgama.
Pārvatī’s jewel. Another legend tells that a jewel from Pārvatī’s crown fell into the Kabini. Śiva declared that she too would be present here, and the bathing place became the Dakṣiṇa Maṇikarṇikā ghāṭ, “the southern Maṇikarṇikā”, after Kāśī’s holiest ghāṭ.

HAKĪM NAÑJUṆḌA

The physician of Nañjanagūḍu
A sick elephant. Temple tradition tells that the royal elephant of Tipu Sultan developed a disease of the eyes. On the priests’ advice it was bathed in the Kapilā and its eyes washed with the Lord’s abhiṣeka water for one maṇḍala of 48 days.
Sight restored. On the 48th day the elephant could see again.
The gifts. Tipu offered a green marakata (emerald, also described as jade) liṅga with a makara-kaṇṭhi necklace, which is still brought out once a year, at the Girijākalyāṇa festival.
The name. He called the Lord Hakīm Nañjuṇḍa, “Nañjuṇḍa the physician”. The Mysore Gazetteer tells a similar story of his father, Hyder Ali.

ZAPPY: So the Lord is a doctor too?

PROF. BOLT: Devotees call him Vaidya Nañjuṇḍeśvara, the healer. People still come here praying for cures, and the old name from a Sultan's court says the same.

HISTORY

A thousand years of builders
9th–10th c. The temple’s origins are traced to the Western Gaṅgas.
c. 11th c. The garbhagṛha, the sanctum, dates to the Cōḻa period.
c. 13th c. The hall in front of the sanctum, where devotees sit, is Hoysaḷa work.
1517 A Kannada inscription linked to the father-in-law of Kṛṣṇadevarāya of Vijayanagara.
Vijayanagara A brick-and-mortar śikhara over the shrine; the Pārvatī and Nārāyaṇa shrines are added.
1644 Daḷavāyi Vikramarāya installs the great stone Nandi, about 8 ft tall.
18th c. Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan; the healing of the elephant and the emerald liṅga.
1845 Devājammaṇṇi, queen of Kṛṣṇarāja Woḍeyar III, builds the great gopuram.

Royal temple. The Woḍeyars of Mysuru built the gopuram and several maṇḍapas and made many grants. A stone sculpture of Kṛṣṇarāja Woḍeyar III with his four queens stands inside the temple.

THE TEMPLE

Plan and gopuram

🏯 The gopuram

About 120 ft tall, raised in 1845 by Queen Devājammaṇṇi. Sources give it seven or nine tiers. It is crowned by seven gold-plated kalaśas, each about 3 m tall.

📐 The size

About 385 × 160 ft, some 50,000 sq ft: one of the largest temple complexes in Karnataka, with pillared maṇḍapas and yāḷi pillars at the main door.

ZAPPY: Sixty-five liṅgas in one temple?

PROF. BOLT: Kings, queens and devotees kept adding them over centuries. Walk the corridors and you're walking through a thousand years of offerings.

THE FIVE CHARIOTS

Dodda Jātre: the Pañca Mahā Rathotsava

The big fair. Dodda Jātre, “the great fair”, is the temple’s annual festival in spring (March–April). Its high point is the Pañca Mahā Rathotsava: five chariots drawn through the chariot streets on the same morning.

The five. Chariots of Gaṇapati, Caṇḍikeśvara, Subrahmaṇya and Pārvatī, and the great Gautama Ratha, which carries Śrīkaṇṭheśvara.

The giant. The Gautama Ratha rises nearly as high as the gopuram: reports put it at about 90 to 100 ft and well over a hundred tonnes. It moves only because thousands pull together.

Only one. It is described as the only festival in South India where five great chariots are drawn at once.

ZAPPY: Why name the biggest chariot after Gautama and not Śiva?

PROF. BOLT: Because Gautama brought Śiva here! The chariot carries the Lord, but it remembers the sage who installed him.

AROUND NAÑJANAGŪḌU

The confluence and nearby shrines
Paraśurāma KṣetraThe saṅgama of the Kapilā and Kauṇḍinya (Gundlu), with a shrine of Paraśurāma where he is said to have been freed of his sin
Snāna ghāṭThe bathing ghāṭ on the Kapilā below the temple
HemmaragālaA Kṛṣṇa temple linked with the sage Kauṇḍinya, with the image of Gopāla playing the flute in the three-bend pose
In the townShrines of Māruti and Basaveśvara near the saṅgama, a Rāghavendra Svāmī maṭha, and a large statue of Śiva near the temple

Hari and Hara. As at the Raṅga temples upstream, Śiva and Viṣṇu sit side by side: a Nārāyaṇa shrine inside Śrīkaṇṭheśvara’s temple, and a Gopāla temple of the sage Kauṇḍinya nearby.

More temples around Mysuru: Ādi Raṅga at Śrīraṅgapaṭṇa and Madhya Raṅga at Śivanasamudra.
