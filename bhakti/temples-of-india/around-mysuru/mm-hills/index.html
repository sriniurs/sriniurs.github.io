MM HILLS · MĀDAPPA · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The saint who rode a tiger

FOREWORD

Far in the south-east of Karnataka, where the Kāverī and the Pālār wrap around a tangle of forested hills, a saint once rode in on a tiger.

Six hundred years later he is Male Mahadēśvara, Śiva of the hills, lovingly called Mādappa. Lakhs climb to his temple, and singers with bronze cymbals still sing his epic through the night.

This guide tells his story, his hills and his songs.

WHERE IT IS

Hills between two rivers, two states

The hills. MM Hills rise in the far south-east of Karnataka, beside Tamil Nadu. With the Biḷigiri Raṅgana hills, they form a bridge between the Eastern and Western Ghats.

The rivers. The Kāverī bounds them on the north and east, the Pālār on the south; the two meet at the south-east corner of the forest.

The forest. Sandalwood and bamboo cover the slopes, home to elephants, gaur, sambar and sloth bears; tigers and leopards are rarely seen. Much of it is now the Male Mahadēśvara Wildlife Sanctuary.

WHO WAS MAHADĒŚVARA?

Saint, healer, Śiva on earth
BirthBorn in the Kali Yuga, by legend, to Uttarājamma and Candraśēkhara Mūrti of the Ādi Jāmbava community
GuidesGuided by the heads of the Vīraśaiva Liṅgāyat Suttūr and Kunthūr maṭhas
JourneyCame from Śrīśaila, working miracles on the way, and settled on the seven hills to do penance
HealerLived as a siddha, practising medicine and teaching non-violence; tradition tells of him curing diseased cattle
LineageLiṅgāyats count him as their own; one account makes him the third head of the Haradanahaḷḷi maṭha

ZAPPY: Was he a real person or a god?

PROF. BOLT: Both, for his devotees: a 15th-century saint who lived here, and Śiva himself in human form.

THE TIGER RIDER

Huli vāhana

His mount. Mahadēśvara is said to have travelled the forest on a tiger, the huli vāhana, and to have worked many miracles around the hills to protect the people and sages living there.

Tamer of the wild. For the forest people, a man who could ride a tiger was proof of his power over the jungle and all its dangers.

The great statue. In 2023 a 108-ft statue of Mahadēśvara on his tiger, trident in hand, was unveiled on a rock on the hilltop, visible from across the hills.

THE SEVEN HILLS

Eḷu male: Mahadēśvara’s mountains

The seven peaks. Tradition names seven hills that together make up Male Mahadēśvara Beṭṭa: Anumale, Jēnumale, Kānumale, Pacchemale, Pavaḷamale, Ponnācimale and Koṅgumale. In the songs, the saint is the Lord of the seven hills.

Male means hill. Male is Kannada for hill or hill-forest: Male Mahadēśvara is simply “Mahadēśvara of the hills”.

Ponnāci. The highest point of the whole range is Ponnāci Beṭṭa, 1,514 m, clothed in evergreen forest.

ZAPPY: Seven hills, like Tirumala?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly the same idea: the Lord who lives on seven hills. In the south, seven hills is the address of a god!

THE EPIC OF MĀDAPPA

Male Mādēśvara Kāvya
PartWhat it tells
1The saint’s miraculous birth, childhood and spiritual quest
2How he wins over the rich Junje Gowḍa to build him a temple on the seven hills
3The destruction of the evil king Śravaṇāsura
4Saṅkamma: the longest part, the ordeals of a proud wife who keeps her dignity
5Bēvinaṭṭi Kāḷamma: a comic tale of a vain, miserly woman
6The simple, god-fearing couple Mūgayya and his wife become devotees
7The benediction, and a short retelling of the whole epic

An oral epic. The Male Mādēśvara Kāvya, also called Mādappana Kathe, has been sung for centuries without a fixed written text. With the Manteswamy Kāvya, it is one of the two most renowned oral epics in Kannada.

Seven nights. The full epic, in seven parts, can take seven nights to sing; pilgrims sing it on their way to the hill fairs.

A familiar shape. Scholars note its outline echoes the Rāmāyaṇa: the god comes to earth to destroy an evil king.

THE LEGENDS

Junje Gowḍa, Śravaṇāsura and Saṅkamma
Junje Gowḍa. Junje Gowḍa was a wealthy Kuruba landlord, a devotee of the god Bēredēvara. Mahadēśvara came to his house and worked miracles there until Junje Gowḍa became his follower, and built his temple on the hill. His descendants are said to live still at Kadamboor in Hanur taluk.
Śravaṇāsura. An evil king who oppressed the land. In the epic, Śiva comes down as Mahadēśvara above all to destroy him.
Saṅkamma. The heart of the epic: a proud, virtuous wife who suffers and endures. Many listeners say her story is the most moving of all.

THE KAMSĀLE SINGERS

Keepers of the epic
The nameKamsāle comes from kāṃsya tāḷa, bronze cymbals: the singers keep time by clashing them as they dance
The singersCalled Dēvara Guḍḍas, “servants of the god”, and Kamsāleyavaru
A vowOnly those who have taken a dīkṣā, a vow of devotion to Mahadēśvara, may perform it; it is taught by a teacher
OralThey learn the songs by heart, with no printed text
WhenAt the hill fairs of Dīpāvaḷi, Śivarātri and Ugādi, and on Kārtika Mondays

ZAPPY: They learn a seven-night epic by heart?

PROF. BOLT: Every word, passed from teacher to student. Kannada's living library!

THE TEMPLE

The liṅga where the saint still sits

🕉 The liṅga

In the sanctum is a self-born (svayambhū) liṅga. Devotees believe Mahadēśvara is still there, deep in penance.

💧 Antaragaṅge

A pilgrimage here includes a dip in the Antaragaṅge, a stream fed by a spring that never runs dry.

A hill town. The temple grounds cover about 155 acres, and the temple also holds lands in nearby villages. In 2023 a silver chariot was unveiled for the Lord.

HISTORY

From saint to sanctuary
15th c. Mahadēśvara lives and does penance on the hills; by tradition, Junje Gowḍa builds the temple.
Sālūr maṭha The Sālūr maṭha, said to have been founded by Mahadēśvara himself, manages the temple for centuries.
1953 The Madras Government takes over the temple: the hills then lay in Madras State’s Kollegal taluk.
After 1956 With the formation of Karnataka, a trust committee under the Muzrāi Department takes charge.
2013 The forest is notified as the Male Mahadēśvara Wildlife Sanctuary.
2023 The 108-ft statue and the silver chariot are unveiled.

Note. Sources agree on the 15th century for the saint and the temple’s origin, but give no inscriptional date for the building; Junje Gowḍa’s role comes from legend and the epic.

THE HILL FAIRS

When the hills fill with song
DīpāvaḷiThe great fair, with Kamsāle singers and pilgrims walking up the hills
MahāśivarātriNight-long worship of the Lord of the hills; the great statue is lit up
UgādiThe Kannada New Year fair
Kārtika MondaysSpecial days of worship, when Kamsāle is most often seen

Walking and singing. Many pilgrims, especially from the villages of Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, walk to the hills for the fairs, singing parts of the epic as they go.

ZAPPY: Do they sing the whole epic on the way?

PROF. BOLT: Some groups sing for seven nights! Others pick their favourite parts. Nobody is in a hurry here.

THE PEOPLE OF THE HILLS

A god of the forest people
SoligasMahadēśvara is the family god of the Soligas and other forest peoples of the hills; Soliga lore traces their origin to Kārayya, a son of Mahadēśvara
KurubasJunje Gowḍa, the builder of legend, was a Kuruba landlord
LiṅgāyatsCount the saint as one of their own; the maṭhas of Suttūr, Kunthūr and Sālūr are part of his story
Two statesKannada and Tamil devotees alike: the hills sit on the border

Everyone’s Mādappa. A saint born to the Ādi Jāmbava community, guided by Liṅgāyat maṭhas, housed by a Kuruba landlord and worshipped by forest people: MM Hills gathers communities that rarely share one god.

Next door: Biḷigiri Raṅgana Beṭṭa, where the Soligas call Viṣṇu their brother-in-law.
