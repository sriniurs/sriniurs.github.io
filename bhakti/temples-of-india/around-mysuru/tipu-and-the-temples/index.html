TIPU SULTAN AND THE TEMPLES

ŚRĪRAṄGAPAṬṬAṆA · NAÑJANAGŪḌU · MELUKŌṬE · ŚṚṄGERI · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The record, and the disputes

FOREWORD

Visit the temples around Mysuru and the name Tipu Sultan keeps coming up: on a silver cup, in a god’s nickname, in a royal letter, on a battlefield, and in a day of mourning.

Few figures in Indian history are argued over so fiercely. This guide does not try to settle the argument. It gathers what is on record, temple by temple, sets the disputed memories beside it, and names the sources for both.

WHO WAS TIPU SULTAN?

The Tiger of Mysore, in brief
1751 Born on 1 December at Devanahalli, son of Hyder Ali, the general who took power in the Woḍeyar kingdom of Mysuru.
1782 Becomes ruler after Hyder Ali’s death in December; rules from Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa.
1780s–90s Wars with the British East India Company, the Marāṭhās and the Nizām: the Second, Third and Fourth Anglo-Mysore Wars.
1792 Siege of Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa; by the Treaty of Seringapatam he gives up half his territory.
1799 Killed on 4 May defending the fort in the final siege. The British restore the Woḍeyars; Pūrṇayya becomes Dīvān.

The emblem. Tipu used the tiger as his symbol everywhere: on his throne, his weapons and his soldiers’ uniforms. That is why he is called the Tiger of Mysore.

His kingdom. Most of his subjects were Hindu, and several of his highest officers were Hindu. How he treated their temples is the question this guide looks at.

THE TEMPLES IN THIS GUIDE

Around Tipu’s capital

The centre. Tipu’s capital, the island fort of Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa, held the great Raṅganātha temple. Within a short ride lay Karighaṭṭa, Nimiṣāmbā, Melukōṭe, Nañjanagūḍu and Kalale.

Farther out. Biḷigiri Raṅgana Beṭṭa in the south-east forests, and Śṛṅgeri, far to the north-west in the Malnad, whose letters are the best-known record of all.

And land. Tipu is also recorded as taking back land grants to temples and Brahmins that had been made without proper authority; grants backed by a proper sanad were left alone.

NAÑJANAGŪḌU

Hakīm Nañjuṇḍa, the physician
The storyTemple tradition says Tipu’s royal elephant lost its sight; on the priests’ advice it was bathed in the Kapilā and its eyes washed with the Lord’s abhiṣeka water for 48 days, and it saw again
The giftA green marakata liṅga with an emerald necklace, still shown once a year at Girijākalyāṇa; and a jewelled cup
The nameHe called the Lord Hakīm Nañjuṇḍa, “Nañjuṇḍa the physician”
A second versionThe Mysore Gazetteer tells a similar elephant story of his father, Hyder Ali

Living memory. Of all the Tipu stories around Mysuru, this one is kept by the temple itself: the gifts are still there, and the name is still used.

ZAPPY: The Sultan named the god 'doctor'?

PROF. BOLT: Hakīm is the Persian word for physician. A grateful patient's name for the healer, and it stuck.

ŚRĪRAṄGAPAṬṬAṆA

A temple inside the Sultan’s fort

Raṅganātha. The great temple of Ādi Raṅga stood inside Tipu’s fort, close to his palace, through his whole reign. The record lists his gift of seven silver cups and a silver camphor burner to it.

The summer palace. East of the fort, his garden palace Dariya Daulat Bāgh (1784) stands on the road to Gañjām, where Nimiṣāmbā’s temple stands by the Kāverī.

The mosque. Near the temple he built the Masjid-i-Ala, whose origin is disputed (see page “The Masjid-i-Ala”).

Full guide: Ādi Raṅga ➜

MELUKŌṬE · I

A decree for the recitation
The templeThe Nārāyaṇa temple of Melukōṭe (Tirunārāyaṇapura), the great Śrīvaiṣṇava centre where Rāmānuja lived
The decreeA Kannada order that the Śrīvaiṣṇava invocatory verses at the temple be recited in their traditional form
The vesselsGold and silver vessels at the temple carry inscriptions naming the Sultan
How it is readHistorians including B. A. Saletore and Irfan Habib cite these to describe Tipu as a protector of Hindu worship

ZAPPY: He told priests how to chant?

PROF. BOLT: He ordered that the traditional form be kept. It's one of the most quoted pieces of the record.

MELUKŌṬE · II

Naraka Caturdaśī: a day of mourning

The memory. The Mandyam Iyengar community of Melukōṭe remember a massacre of their people by Tipu’s soldiers on Naraka Caturdaśī, the day of Dīpāvaḷi in South India. Many families of the community still do not celebrate Dīpāvaḷi, and keep the day as one of mourning.

The context they give. Community accounts link it to the Mysuru royal family’s attempts to restore Woḍeyar rule: the dowager queen Lakṣmammaṇṇi and the pradhāns Tirumala Iyengar and his brother, of the same community, were in secret correspondence with the British, which Tipu treated as treason.

The scale. Accounts give about 700 to 800 killed; a few give higher figures.

SourcesChiefly community history and oral tradition, including the community’s own published history; also later newspaper and magazine accounts
Dates givenDiffer widely: 1760, 1783 and 1790 all appear
Contemporary recordsWe did not find a contemporary record of the event in the sources we checked; mainstream histories rarely mention it
TodayThe memory is real and living; it is also used heavily in present-day political debate over Tipu

Both on one hill. The same town holds Tipu’s decree and vessels, and the community’s mourning. This guide records both and does not try to reconcile them.

THE MASJID-I-ALA

A mosque, and a disputed past
Built1786–87, inside the Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa fort near the Bangalore Gate; Persian inscriptions name Tipu as the builder
The claimHindu groups say it stands on the site of a Hanumān (Āñjaneya) temple, pointing to Hindu motifs on granite pillars, and have asked for permission to worship there
The 1935 reportThey cite the 1935 Annual Report of the Mysore Archaeological Department, which records a tradition that a fakir made the young Tipu promise to turn the temple into a mosque, and that he kept the promise
TodayAn active mosque with a madrasa, and a Monument of National Importance under the Archaeological Survey of India

What we can say. The report and the claim are on record; we found no court ruling or historians’ consensus settling the question. It remains disputed.

BIḶIGIRI RAṄGANA BEṬṬA

A hunting trip and a new name
Before TipuA copper plate of 1667 names the Lord of the white hill Tiru Veṅkaṭanātha
The visitTradition says Tipu visited on a hunting expedition and called it a temple of Raṅganātha
AfterThe name stuck: Biḷigiri Raṅganātha, as it is known today
PūrṇayyaTipu’s officer Pūrṇayya, later Dīvān under the Woḍeyars, is also recorded as endowing the temple

A name from a Sultan. If the tradition is right, one of Karnataka’s best-loved names for Viṣṇu came from a remark by Tipu.

KARIGHAṬṬA

A temple hill in a war
Feb 1792 Lord Cornwallis’s army, with Marāṭhā and Hyderabad allies, besieges Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa. British records call the hill Carigat or Karigaut Hill.
The fighting Among the actions of the siege is an attack on Tipu’s post at Carigat Hill; later, badly exposed, Cornwallis pulls his command back to the hill.
18 Mar 1792 The Treaty of Seringapatam ends the war.
1799 Local legend says British cannons stood on the hill in the final siege; the records we found confirm only 1792.

ŚṚṄGERI

The letters to the Śaṅkarācārya
1791 Marāṭhā horsemen under Raghunāth Rao Paṭvardhan raid the Śṛṅgeri temple and maṭha, killing and wounding people, plundering it and displacing the image of the goddess Śāradā.
The appeal The Śaṅkarācārya petitions Tipu. He orders his officer at Bednur to give the Svāmī 200 rahatis in cash and other gifts for the restoration.
1790s Letters continue between the court and the maṭha: about 30 in Kannada.
1916 The letters are found by the Director of Archaeology in Mysuru.

Why it matters. Śṛṅgeri is outside the Mysuru region, but these letters are the most cited document of Tipu’s dealings with Hindu religious institutions, so they belong in any account of this kind.

Two readings. Some take these appointments as evidence of an even-handed state; others note that a ruler of a mostly Hindu kingdom needed Hindu administrators whatever his personal views, and point out that men like Tirumala Iyengar also worked against him.

ZAPPY: So Hindus ran his treasury and his police?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, and his revenue. That's on record. What it tells us about his beliefs is where the arguing starts.

TWO VIEWS OF TIPU

How historians read the record

📜 The patron

Historians such as B. A. Saletore, Irfan Habib, Mohibbul Hasan, Kate Brittlebank and A. S. Chetty point to the grants, gifts, decrees and Śṛṅgeri letters. Several argue that many persecution stories come from early British writers such as Mark Wilks and James Kirkpatrick, who fought against Tipu and had reason to paint him as a tyrant.

⚔️ The persecutor

Critics point to campaigns in Kodagu and Malabar, the captivity of Mangalorean Catholics, accounts of forced conversions and destroyed temples and churches, and local memories such as Melukōṭe. Some read the temple gifts as political: buying loyalty in a Hindu-majority kingdom at war.

Where the evidence sits. Around Mysuru, the surviving objects and documents (cups, liṅga, decree, letters, sanads) mostly record patronage. The harshest accounts come mostly from the coast and the hills to the west, and from communal memory. Both are part of the history, and the debate over him remains live and political in Karnataka today.

ZAPPY: So who's right?

PROF. BOLT: Historians still disagree, and politics has made the debate louder. Our job here is to show you the record; the judging is yours.
