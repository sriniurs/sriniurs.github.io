TRIPURASUNDARĪ AND DEŚEŚVARA · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Tibbādevī and the Lord of the land

FOREWORD

South of T. Narasīpura, where the Kāverī and Kabini meet, is a village whose Goddess sits in black stone with the Śrīcakra at her feet. She is Tripurasundarī, Lalitā herself; the village calls her Tibbādevī.

Her temple’s legend begins with a Muslim chief, a dry stalk that sprouted overnight, and a promise to stay forever. Beside her stands Deśeśvara, Śiva, the Lord of the land.

This guide tells their stories, their temples and the festival of new shoots, when the Goddess’s sisters come to visit.

Jai Mā,
srini.presents 🙏

WHERE IT IS

South of the river-meeting

A Kāverī village. Mūgūru lies in T. Narasīpura taluk, south of the town where the Kāverī and Kabini meet, in the old temple country that includes Talakāḍu, Somanāthapura and Muḍukutore.

A large village. It is one of the bigger villages of the taluk, and its Goddess draws devotees from Mysuru, Mandya, Chāmarājanagara and Bengaluru.

Two shrines, one village. Tripurasundarī’s walled temple and the Deśeśvara Śiva temple stand close together; with Rākasamma and the Jain temples they make Mūgūru a small temple town.

HER NAMES

Tripurasundarī and Tibbādevī
Tripurasundarī“The beauty of the three cities or worlds”: the name of Lalitā, the supreme Goddess of the Śrīvidyā tradition
TibbādevīThe local name. Tibba is a mound or raised ground in Kannada; the name is often read as “the Goddess of the mound”. This is a common reading, not a recorded origin
Ammanavaru“The Mother”: how the village speaks of her
Mūgūru’s GoddessIn the Mysore University encyclopedia’s list of great village goddesses, she is named among the famous ones of the region

Two faces of one Goddess. Tripurasundarī is the high name, used in Sanskrit hymns and Śrīvidyā; Tibbādevī is the name of the fields and the festival. Mūgūru keeps both: a Śrīcakra at her feet, and a village jātre in her honour. That double life is common in Karnataka, where great Purāṇic goddesses are also the protecting mothers of particular villages.

The trinity in one. Devotees and writers describe her as the united power of Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Maheśvara. This matches Śrīvidyā’s teaching that the three great gods are the Goddess’s instruments, even her throne.

ZAPPY: So is she a village goddess or Lalitā?

PROF. BOLT: Both, Zappy. For Mūgūru she's the mother at the centre of the village. For the scriptures she's Lalitā.

THE GODDESS

Black stone and the Śrīcakra

🖤 The image

The Goddess is carved in black stone and shown seated, as a queen, rather than standing or fighting.

🔺 The Śrīcakra

A Śrīcakra, the yantra of Lalitā made of interlocking triangles, is placed near her feet and worshipped with her.

Why the Śrīcakra matters. In Śrīvidyā the Śrīcakra is the Goddess herself in geometric form: nine interlocking triangles around a central point, ringed by lotuses and a square enclosure. A temple that keeps one at the Goddess’s feet is announcing that she is Tripurasundarī in the full sense, not only a local guardian. Śaṅkara’s tradition is credited with placing Śrīcakras before goddesses across South India, most famously at Kāñcī.

Seated, not fierce. Unlike the flame-faced Jvālāmukhī Tripurasundarī of Uttanahaḷḷi, Mūgūru’s Goddess is calm and seated: Tripurasundarī in her gentle, royal aspect.

THE TEMPLE

High walls and a tower of goddesses
WallsHigh walls all round, crowned with small statuettes of the Devī in many forms
GatewayNo rājagopura: entry is through an arched mahādvāra
CourtyardA wide prākāra for circumambulation
ShrineThe main shrine stands on a high platform in the centre, reached by a few steps
HallsA small four-pillared mukhamaṇṭapa with plain walls and pillars
TowerA medium-sized vimāna over the sanctum, with colourful figures of the Devī on every side

A village style. The temple is not a carved Hoysaḷa monument like nearby Somanāthapura. Its plain walls and painted tower belong to the living village tradition, renewed many times. Its strength is its setting and its worship, not ornament.

THE LEGEND · I

Nawab Baba Saheb and the sprouting stalk
The rest. On an expedition, a Muslim pāḷegār (local chief) called Nawab Baba Saheb rested by a lake near present-day Hosahaḷḷi, sleeping under a jamun tree with his head on a stone.
The light. He saw a brilliant light; his soldiers seemed frozen. Tripurasundarī appeared and told him he had laid his head on her sacred presence.
The test. He doubted. He planted a dried jowar stalk upside down and said he would believe if it sprouted by the next evening.
The sprout. It sprouted. He begged forgiveness and was allowed to build her temple at Mūgūru.

This is the temple’s own tradition, repeated by the temple community; it is not a recorded historical event. Its point is plain: the Goddess chose her own devotee, and he was a Muslim chief.

THE LEGEND · II

Mūkāsura and the name of the village
The promise. The Goddess promised to destroy the line of the demon Mūkāsura, whose descendants troubled the region, and to stay in that place forever.
The name. From this promise, tradition says, the village came to be called Mūgūru: the place linked with Mūka’s end and the Goddess’s settling.
An old shrine. Local accounts call the temple about 850 years old. No inscription confirming this appeared in the sources we checked.

Place-name legends. Many Karnataka villages explain their names through a goddess’s victory over a demon: Mysuru itself is “Mahiṣa’s town”, the place of Mahiṣāsura. Mūgūru’s story follows that pattern. Linguists might suggest other roots for the name; the legend is what the village tells.

ZAPPY: A demon called Mūka?

PROF. BOLT: Mūka means 'mute'. In the Purāṇas, Arjuna and Śiva fight a demon Mūka who came as a boar. Whether it's the same demon, no one can say.

CHIGURU OḌEYUVA JĀTRE

The festival of new sprouts
WhenAround Saṅkrānti (January–February), over about three days
The name“The fair of the sprouting shoot”, after the jowar stalk that sprouted in the founding legend
The sistersImages of goddesses from neighbouring villages, said to be Tibbādevī’s sisters, are brought to visit her
ProcessionThey are taken round the village before coming to the temple
The cartsThe carts are decorated with tender leaves (chiguru), coconut fronds and areca spathes

A meeting of goddesses. In the Mysuru region, village goddesses are often thought of as sisters who visit one another at festivals, as Uttanahaḷḷi’s Goddess is Cāmuṇḍī’s sister. At Mūgūru the visit is the whole point: the sisters arrive, take her darśan and are paraded together.

A big village fair. Thousands come, from Mysuru, Bengaluru, Chāmarājanagara and beyond. Neighbouring villages organise free meals (anna santarpaṇe) for pilgrims, and in recent years a large rathotsava, a chariot festival, has been part of the celebrations, inaugurated by local seers and leaders.

THROUGH THE YEAR

Her other days
ĀṣāḍhaA flower festival (hūvina utsava) in the month of Āṣāḍha (June–July)
Fridays and TuesdaysThe Goddess’s days, when local families visit
NavarātriThe nine nights of the Goddess
VowsDevotees make vows (harake) and offer fruit, flowers and new clothes when their wishes come true

Living worship. Daily abhiṣeka with milk and tender coconut, kuṅkuma-arcane and betel-leaf offerings are common here. Many newly married couples come for her blessing, and women whose prayers have been answered return to offer thanks at the jātre.

DEŚEŚVARA

Śiva beside the Goddess
NameŚrī Deśeśvara Svāmi temple, also called the Śrī Deśeśvara Śiva–Pārvatī temple
DeityŚiva as a liṅga, with Pārvatī
MeaningDeśa + īśvara: “Lord of the land” or “Lord of the region”: a natural reading of the name
StandingListed among the Śiva temples of Mysuru district as Mūgūru’s Śiva temple
AgeCalled ancient locally; no dated inscription appeared in the sources we checked

Śiva and Śakti in one village. A great goddess and a Śiva temple side by side is the pattern of Śrīvidyā itself: Tripurasundarī with Kāmeśvara, Śakti with Śiva, never apart. In village life it means the Mother who protects and the Lord who rules the land watch over the same fields.

Lord of the land. Deśeśvara, if the name is read as “lord of the region”, makes Śiva the guardian of the whole territory, while Tibbādevī is the mother of the village: a division of care found in many Karnataka villages.

ZAPPY: Which do I visit first?

PROF. BOLT: Local custom varies. Most pilgrims go to the Goddess and also bow to Deśeśvara. Together they're one blessing.

THE OTHER SHRINES

Rākasamma and the Jain temples
RākasammaA shrine to Rākasamma, regarded locally as Tibbādevī’s sister
ĀdināthaA Digambara Jain temple of Ādinātha, the first Tīrthaṅkara, seated in paryaṅkāsana; an inscription on the pedestal is dated to about the 13th century
PārśvanāthaA second Jain temple of Pārśvanātha, standing in kāyotsarga
The inscriptionIt records disciples of the teacher Bhānukīrti Paṇḍitadeva of the Mūla Saṅgha, Deśī Gaṇa, Pustaka Gaccha, Koṇḍakunda lineage, and the leaders of Mūgūru, renovating a temple and installing an image

Many faiths, one village. Mūgūru has a Goddess whose temple tradition credits a Muslim chief, Jain temples whose inscription records the village’s own leaders, and a Śiva temple at its heart. Its history is a small picture of how Karnataka’s religious life has long been shared.
