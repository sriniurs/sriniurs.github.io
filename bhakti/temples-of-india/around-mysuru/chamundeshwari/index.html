CĀMUṆḌEŚVARĪ

CĀMUṆḌĪ HILLS · NĀḌA DEVĪ · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The Goddess who gave Mysuru its name

FOREWORD

Every Mysurean grows up under one hill. On top of it sits the Goddess who, the city believes, defeated the buffalo demon Mahiṣāsura and gave Mysuru its name.

She is Cāmuṇḍeśvarī: family Goddess of the Woḍeyars, Nāḍa Devī of Karnataka, and the Goddess who rides the golden howdah at Dasara.

This guide tells her legends, her temple, her hill and her festivals.

Jai Cāmuṇḍī,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Is this the most famous temple in Mysuru?

PROF. BOLT: No contest! The city, the palace and Dasara all lead back to her.

WHERE IT IS

The hill above Mysuru

The hill. Cāmuṇḍī Hills rise beside the city to about 1,060 m. From the top you can see the palace, Karañji Lake and the spread of Mysuru below.

The ways up. A stone stairway of about 1,000 steps, built in 1659, climbs from the city, passing the great Nandi; a road winds up the other way.

A forest too. The hill is a reserve forest: some 193 kinds of birds, 150 butterflies, and 442 flowering plants have been recorded on it.

THE GODDESS

Eight arms, seated in yoga

🗡 Aṣṭabhujā

The image in the sanctum has eight arms. She sits cross-legged in a yogic posture, her right heel pressed against the lowest of the seven cakras.

✨ The festival image

A golden utsava image goes out in procession; its consecration by a Maharaja is remembered each year at the Vardhanti.

Power and stillness. A warrior with eight weapons, seated in meditation: the Goddess who fought Mahiṣāsura is also the calm power at the root of every being.

THE LEGEND · I

Mahiṣāsura and the hill
The buffalo demon. In the Devī Māhātmya, Mahiṣāsura, a demon who could change into a buffalo, defeated the gods and took heaven itself.
The Goddess born of light. The gods’ combined anger blazed out as a single light, and from it rose the Goddess, armed with all their weapons, riding a lion.
The battle. She destroyed his armies and his generals, and at last Mahiṣāsura himself, as he tried to escape from the buffalo’s body.
On this hill. Mysuru’s tradition says the battle was fought here: Mahiṣāsura ruled this land, Mahiṣūru, “buffalo-town”, which became Mysuru.

Read the battle verse by verse: Devī Māhātmya · Mahiṣāsura Mardinī.

THE LEGEND · II

Folk tellings of the hill
The youngest sister. A Mysuru folk tale makes Cāmuṇḍī the youngest of seven warrior daughters of King Bijjalarāya of Ujjain, who came south, fought Mahiṣāsura here and stayed on the hill. Her fiery helper, Uri Māri, was given a home at the foot: see Uttanahaḷḷi.
The founding prince. Woḍeyar tradition says that in 1399 the young Yadurāya, by the Goddess’s grace, came to Mysuru and became the first Woḍeyar king. From then on she was the family’s Goddess.
Another view. Some Jain scholars hold that the image was first a Jain yakṣī, Cāvuṇḍa or Vasantikā Devī, later worshipped as Cāmuṇḍī. This is a scholars’ claim, not the temple’s tradition.

A ŚAKTI PĪṬHA

Where Satī’s hair fell

The story. When Satī gave up her life at her father Dakṣa’s sacrifice, Śiva wandered the worlds carrying her body. Where its parts fell, the Goddess is worshipped as a Śakti Pīṭha, a seat of power.

Here. Cāmuṇḍī Hills are counted among the 18 great Śakti Pīṭhas, as the Krauñca Pīṭha, named after Krauñcapurī, an old name of the region. It is said that Satī’s hair fell here.

Lists differ. Lists of the pīṭhas vary from text to text; the 18-pīṭha list is the one that includes Mysuru.

HISTORY · I

Before the Woḍeyars
c. 950 The Mahābaleśvara temple on the hill, the oldest, from the Gaṅga era; the hill was once called Mahābalādri after it.
12th c. The Hoysaḷas: Viṣṇuvardhana is said to have expanded Mahābaleśvara, and the original Cāmuṇḍeśvarī shrine is thought to date from Hoysaḷa rule.
1399 Woḍeyar tradition: Yadurāya founds the dynasty with the Goddess’s blessing.
17th c. Vijayanagara-era work on the temple’s tower, by some accounts.

Two temples, one hill. Śiva as Mahābaleśvara was on the hill first; the Goddess’s fame grew around him until the hill itself took her name.

ZAPPY: So Śiva was here first?

PROF. BOLT: On this hill, yes. Now everyone says Cāmuṇḍī Beṭṭa. Śiva doesn't mind; she's his Śakti!

HISTORY · II

The Woḍeyars and their Goddess
1610 Rāja Woḍeyar begins the royal celebration of the Goddess’s victory that grows into Mysuru Dasara.
1659 Doḍḍa Dēvarāja Woḍeyar builds the stairway of about 1,000 steps.
1664 He installs the great Nandi on the 700th step.
1799 on After Tipu’s fall, Mummaḍi Kṛṣṇarāja Woḍeyar III brings priests from Kāñcīpuram on the advice of the Kāñci seer.
1827 Kṛṣṇarāja Woḍeyar III repairs the shrine and builds the seven-tiered gopura (some say 1830); he gives the Siṃha Vāhana, other vehicles and jewels.
Later Kṛṣṇarāja Woḍeyar IV builds Lalitādri, a palace on the hill.

A king at prayer. Inside the temple stands a 6-ft statue of Kṛṣṇarāja Woḍeyar III, hands folded, with his three queens, Ramāvilāsa, Lakṣmīvilāsa and Kṛṣṇavilāsa, their names carved on the pedestals.

THE STEPS AND THE NANDI

A climb with a giant bull
The stepsAbout 1,000 stone steps (often counted as 1,008), built by Doḍḍa Dēvarāja Woḍeyar in 1659
A pilgrim customMany devotees climb on foot, often marking each step with turmeric and kuṅkuma as a vow
The NandiInstalled in 1664 on the 700th step, before a small Śiva shrine: about 4.9 m tall and 7.6 m long, carved from a single block of granite
Black or white?The stone is pale; years of oil abhiṣeka make it look black

ZAPPY: Do people really climb a thousand steps?

PROF. BOLT: Thousands do, especially on Āṣāḍha Fridays. The Nandi is the halfway cheer-up point!

THE MAHIṢĀSURA STATUE

The demon at the gate

The welcome. A towering, brightly painted statue of Mahiṣāsura, sword in his right hand and a cobra in his left, stands on the hilltop near the temple.

Why the demon? He is part of the hill’s story and of the city’s name. Visitors meet the defeated king first, then go in to the Goddess who defeated him.

A Mysuru icon. With the Nandi and the gopura, he is one of the three sights every child of Mysuru remembers from the hill.

ZAPPY: He looks scary!

PROF. BOLT: He was. That's why we needed the Goddess. Now he's the hill's most photographed guest!

THROUGH THE YEAR

Fridays, a birthday and Dasara
Āṣāḍha FridaysThe Fridays of Āṣāḍha (June–July) are especially sacred: vast crowds come from across Karnataka and neighbouring states
VardhantiThe Cāmuṇḍī Vardhanti in Āṣāḍha marks the Maharaja’s consecration of the festival image, which is taken round the temple in a golden palanquin
NavarātriThe chief festival: the Goddess is adorned in nine ways as the Navadurgās; on the 7th day, jewels given by the Maharajas are brought from the district treasury to adorn her
RathotsavaThe car festival, with the Siṃha Vāhana given by Kṛṣṇarāja Woḍeyar III

DASARA

The Goddess rides the golden howdah

Since 1610. Mysuru Dasara, Karnataka’s Nāḍa Habba (state festival), celebrates the Goddess’s victory over Mahiṣāsura. It grew from the celebration begun by Rāja Woḍeyar and is held at the palace every year.

The procession. On Vijayadaśamī, the idol of Cāmuṇḍeśvarī is placed in the golden howdah, about 750 kg, on the lead elephant, and the Jambū Savāri travels about 5.5 km from the palace to Bannimaṇṭapa, watched by lakhs.

Then and now. Once the Maharaja rode in the howdah; today the Goddess herself does.

ZAPPY: The Goddess rides an elephant through town?

PROF. BOLT: Once a year, in gold! The hill's Goddess comes down to bless her city.
