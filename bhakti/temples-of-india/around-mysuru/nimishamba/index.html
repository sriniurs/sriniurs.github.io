FOREWORD

On the bank of the Kāverī, just outside Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa, there is a Goddess who does not keep her devotees waiting.

When demons no weapon could touch broke up a sacred yajña, a sage cried out, and the Mother came in the blink of an eye. So she is Nimiṣāmbā, the Mother of the moment.

This guide tells her story, her Śrīcakra and her temple.

WHERE IT IS

On the Kāverī at Gañjām

Gañjām. The temple stands in Gañjām, once a thriving suburb of the Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa fort, on the road east from the island town toward the saṅgama, past Tipu’s summer palace.

The river. The temple stands right on the bank of the Kāverī.

Neighbours. Across the Lokapāvanī rises Karighaṭṭa hill, and on the island stands Ādi Raṅga.

THE MOTHER OF THE MOMENT

Nimiṣāmbā

⚡ Her name

Nimiṣa is the blink of an eye, a moment; ambā is mother. Nimiṣāmbā: the Mother who comes in a moment.

🔱 Her form

She is Pārvatī, Śiva’s consort, worshipped here as Śakti with a Śrīcakra before her.

Why people come. Devotees believe she removes obstacles and troubles within a moment: many come praying for obstacles to a marriage to be cleared, or for the end of a long worry.

THE LEGEND · I

The yajña and the demons
A yajña for the world. The temple’s story tells that Śiva, as the sage Mukthaka (Mauktikeśvara), set out to perform a great yajña for the welfare of all beings.
The demons. Two demons, Jānu and Sumaṇḍala, feared the yajña would end their rule. They sent army after army, and every army was beaten.
Their boon. Then they came themselves, protected by a boon that no weapon could harm them, and the sage could not defeat them.
The prayer. Mukthaka prayed to the Mother. In a moment Pārvatī appeared, rising, some tellings say, from the sacrificial fire.
Fire from her eyes. No weapon could touch them, so she burned the demons with the fire of her eyes. The sage, overjoyed, named her Nimiṣāmbā: she came within a nimiṣa.

THE LEGEND · II

Another telling: the king Muktarāja
The kingIn another telling, a king Sumanaska wished to hold a yajña for his people, and gave the task to the prince and to the royal sage Muktarāja
The demonsThe demons, named Janumaṇḍala and Sumaṇḍala, overpowered the prince and broke up the rite
The penanceMuktarāja, a king of the Soma (Moon) line by some accounts and the Sun line by others, did penance here, carved a Śrīcakra on stone and began elaborate worship
The answerThe Goddess appeared at once, destroyed the demons, and has stayed here ever since

Same heart, different details. Whether the yajña was Śiva’s own or a king’s, both tellings agree: the demons could not be beaten by weapons, a devotee called, and the Mother came in a moment.

The same pattern runs through the Devī Māhātmya: when the gods are helpless, the Goddess appears and does what no weapon could.

THE STONE ŚRĪCAKRA

The Goddess as a diagram

What it is. The Śrīcakra (Śrī Yantra) is the Goddess drawn as geometry: interlocking triangles, nine in all, pointing up and down, surrounded by lotus petals and a square gateway frame. It is worshipped as her very form.

This one. In front of Nimiṣāmbā is a Śrīcakra carved in black stone, with seed-syllables (bīja) and her mūla-mantra inscribed. It is described as rare and ancient, and receives daily pūjā.

The legend’s link. In the Muktarāja telling, the king himself carved a Śrīcakra in stone during his penance here.

THE TEMPLE

A family of five shrines

🕉 Śiva-pañcāyatana

The temple gathers five deities in one compound: the Goddess with Śiva as Mauktikeśvara, Lakṣmīnārāyaṇa, Sūrya and Gaṇapati, with Hanumān as well.

🔱 Mauktikeśvara

The Śiva of the shrine bears the sage’s name: Mauktikeśvara, the Lord who took the form of Mukthaka for the yajña.

ZAPPY: Viṣṇu in a Devī temple too?

PROF. BOLT: Lakṣmīnārāyaṇa! In a pañcāyatana all the great gods sit together. Nobody gets left out.

HISTORY

The Woḍeyars and their Goddess
Legend The yajña of Mukthaka, or of Muktarāja, at Gañjām; the Goddess appears.
1610 Rāja Woḍeyar of Mysuru takes Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa and makes it his capital.
1610–17 The temple is attributed to Rāja Woḍeyar’s reign (Karnataka Tourism).
19th c. Mummaḍi Kṛṣṇarāja Woḍeyar (Kṛṣṇarāja Woḍeyar III) renovates the temple; some accounts credit him with installing the Goddess.
Today Managed by the Karnataka Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments Department.

Sources differ. Some sources date the temple to the mid-1500s; most link it to Rāja Woḍeyar’s reign (1578–1617, in Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa from 1610); others say Mummaḍi Kṛṣṇarāja Woḍeyar built it “about 300 to 400 years ago”. Mummaḍi Kṛṣṇarāja ruled from 1799 to 1868, so his part is best read as renovation, as one source says.
