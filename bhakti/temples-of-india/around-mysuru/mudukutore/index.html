MUDUKUTORE

MALLIKĀRJUNA · ABHINAVA ŚRĪŚAILA · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Where the river turns, and Arjuna brought jasmine

FOREWORD

Just upstream of Talakāḍu, the Kāverī makes a sharp turn around a small hill. On top sits a temple of Śiva as Mallikārjuna, with his Goddess Bhramarāmbā: the same pair as at the great Śrīśaila.

Devotees call it Abhinava Śrīśaila, and it is the last of the five liṅgas of the Pañcaliṅga Darśana.

This guide tells its legends, its temple and its fair.

WHERE IT IS

Where the river turns

The hill. The temple crowns Somagiri, a small hill about 200 ft high on the bank of the Kāverī, near Talakāḍu. Steps and a road lead up to it.

The name. Here the river makes a turn and flows on: muduku, a turning aside, and tore, flowing on. Mudukutore is the river’s own signature.

The village. In government records the village is T. Beṭṭahaḷḷi (Tirumalakūḍu Beṭṭahaḷḷi); Mudukutore is the older, sacred name.

THE LEGENDS · I

Arjuna’s jasmine
An exile’s halt. During the Pāṇḍavas’ exile in the Mahābhārata, tradition says Arjuna stayed for a time on this hill.
Jasmine for Śiva. He installed (some tellings say carved) a liṅga of Śiva on the summit and worshipped it with mallikā, jasmine flowers.
The name. Mallikā plus Arjuna: the Lord became Mallikārjuna, “Arjuna’s jasmine Lord”.

ZAPPY: Is that also why Śrīśaila's Lord is Mallikārjuna?

PROF. BOLT: Śrīśaila has its own stories of the name. Here, the jasmine and Arjuna make it local!

THE LEGENDS · II

The cow and the hidden liṅga
A dry cow. A cowherd of Mudukutore found his cow gave no milk at home. He followed her up Somagiri, and saw her emptying her udder every day onto a mound on the hilltop.
The whip. Angry, he struck her. The cow leapt, and one hoof landed on the mound, breaking it open.
The liṅga. Beneath lay a liṅga, self-born. The mark of the cow’s hoof, devotees say, can still be seen on it.

ZAPPY: Cows keep finding liṅgas in these stories!

PROF. BOLT: Many temples tell it! A cow pours her milk on the hidden Lord and shows the people where He is.

THE LEGENDS · III

Kapila, and Bhramarāmbā the bee
Kapila’s liṅga. The temple priests tell another story: that the sage Kapila, travelling south in ancient times, installed the liṅga here.
The Goddess. Beside Mallikārjuna stands the shrine of his consort, Bhramarāmbā.
The bee. She is believed to have taken the form of a bhramara, a great bee, to destroy a demon named Sahasrākṣa, “the thousand-eyed”. So she is Bhramarāmbā, the Bee-Mother.
Abhinava Śrīśaila. Mallikārjuna and Bhramarāmbā are the same divine pair worshipped at Śrīśaila, the jyotirliṅga. So this hill is called the new Śrīśaila.

THE TEMPLE

Up the steps of Somagiri

🛕 The shrine

East-facing, with a three-tiered rājagopura, then the flag-pillar, Nandi and altar facing the sanctum, which has an ardha- and mahā-maṇḍapa.

🪜 The climb

An arch at the foot of the hill shows Śiva on Nandi between Gaṇeśa and Subrahmaṇya. Accounts count from about 40 to 100 steps to the top.

Other shrines: the festival images, Gaṇeśa, Subrahmaṇya and Bhairava; and, down a small path, the village Goddess Beṭṭaḷi Māramma.

THE FIFTH LIṄGA

Mudukutore and the Pañcaliṅga Darśana

One of five. Mudukutore’s Mallikārjuna is the fifth of the Pañcaliṅgas of Talakāḍu, held to be the five faces of Śiva. One account names him as the Vāmadeva face.

The Darśana. On the rare day of the Pañcaliṅga Darśana in Kārtika, pilgrims visit all five in one auspicious window: the three in the Talakāḍu dunes, Arkeśvara at Vijayapura, and finally this hill.

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. Four in the sand, one on the hill. The last climb is the sweetest.

HISTORY

From the Gaṅgas to a rebuilding
Legend Arjuna’s jasmine; Kapila’s liṅga; the cow on the hill.
Gaṅga period The temple is said to have been built by the Gaṅgas of Talakāḍu, over 800 years ago.
Vijayanagara The temple is developed further by the Vijayanagara kings.
Woḍeyars Mummaḍi Kṛṣṇarāja Woḍeyar is credited with the main entrance and the mukha-maṇḍapa.
2020 Called dilapidated, the temple and its Goddess shrine are taken up for rebuilding: the Chief Minister lays the foundation stone in November, with ₹16 crore for a first phase.

Note. The history comes mainly from tradition and from remarks at the 2020 ceremony; we found no dated inscription. Excavations nearby are reported to have found remains about 800 years old.

THE GREAT FAIR

Bullocks, grain and the Lord

When. Every year in January–February, a big fair is held at Mudukutore for about a week.

What. Farmers bring cattle and farm produce to trade, and thousands of visitors come for the Lord, the fair and the river.

Old Karnataka. Temple fairs like this have long been the great markets of the Kāverī villages: a festival for the god, and the year’s biggest bazaar for the farmer.

Sources differ. Most describe it as annual; one source calls the great Mudukutore jātre a 12-yearly event.
