GOPĀLASVĀMI BEṬṬA

HIMAVAD VEṆUGOPĀLA · BANDIPUR · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The cowherd in the clouds

FOREWORD

In the heart of Bandipur, above the teak and the tigers, one hill wears the clouds like a shawl for most of the year.

On its summit, in a small old temple, Kṛṣṇa stands under a tree playing his flute, his cows beside him. This is Himavad Gopālasvāmi Beṭṭa, the misty hill of the cowherd Lord.

This guide tells its names, its legends and its story.

PROF. BOLT: Right in the core of Bandipur! The Lord lives with tigers, elephants and mist. The forest gets to keep its peace too.

WHERE IT IS

The highest hill of Bandipur

The hill. Gopālasvāmi Beṭṭa rises to about 1,450 m in the south of Karnataka, near Gundlupet. It is the highest peak in Bandipur National Park, and the temple sits in the park’s core.

The mist. Dense fog wraps the hill for much of the year, which is why it is called Himavad, the misty one.

The forest. The slopes are thick with forest, home to tigers and leopards; the grassy tops draw elephants, deer and gaur.

THE LORD WITH THE FLUTE

Gopālasvāmi, Veṇugopāla

🎶 The image

About 6 ft tall, Kṛṣṇa plays the flute beneath a tree, in a dancing pose with his left big toe resting on the right. The temple faces north.

🐄 The panel

Around him the back panel shows the tree, his friends, his consorts Rukmiṇī and Satyabhāmā, and cows and cowherds on the right side.

Gopāla. Go is cow, pāla protector: the cowherd Kṛṣṇa of Vṛndāvana, and Veṇu is his flute. On this hill he stands as he stood among the cows of Gokula.

A HILL OF MANY NAMES

Himavad, Govardhana, Kamalācala
HimavadFrom the mist that covers the hill most of the year
GopālasvāmiThe Lord of the hill: Kṛṣṇa the cowherd
BeṭṭaKannada for hill
Dakṣiṇa Govardhanagiri“The southern Govardhana”: the hill of the cowherd god
Kamalācala“Lotus hill”, another old name
Haṃsa Tīrtha“The swan lake”, a name for the place and for its sacred tank

Why Govardhana? When Indra sent storms to drown Gokula, the boy Kṛṣṇa lifted Mount Govardhana on his finger and sheltered the cowherds and their cows beneath it for seven days. A cowherd’s god on a cloud-wrapped hill: the old name fits.

ZAPPY: Did he lift this hill too?

PROF. BOLT: No, that was Govardhana near Mathurā! This one is its southern namesake. But with all this mist, you'd want a hill-umbrella too.

THE LEGENDS · I

Agastya’s penance
The sage of the south. Agastya is the great sage of South India in the Purāṇas: the one who crossed the Vindhyas and brought the Vedic tradition south.
His penance. Tradition says Agastya performed intense penance on this hill.
The promise. Pleased, Viṣṇu blessed the place and promised to stay here. Temple tradition says Agastya himself installed the Lord as Gopālasvāmi.
A place of penance. Because sages worshipped and meditated here, the place came to be called Haṃsa Tīrtha: the swan stands for knowledge, calm and liberation.

THE LEGENDS · II

The crow that became a swan
The bath. The story goes that a crow bathed in one of the hill’s sacred tanks and rose out of it a swan.
The name. From then on the tank was called Haṃsa Tīrtha, the swan’s pool.
No crows. Devotees say that to this day no crows are seen on the hill.
77 tīrthas. Tradition counts about 77 sacred tanks and springs in and around the hill temple.

ZAPPY: A crow turns into a swan just by bathing?

PROF. BOLT: That's the point of a tīrtha: it purifies. The crow is ordinary us; the swan is who we can become.

THE WATER THAT NEVER STOPS

A blessing from above the door

A year-round drip. Water drips from above the door of the sanctum through the year, said to be cold and constant even in the dry months.

Shared as prasāda. The priest sprinkles this water on devotees while telling the legends of the hill.

Mist made visible. On a hill wrapped in fog for most of the year, the Lord’s own doorway seems to gather the mist and give it back as blessing.

HISTORY

Seven centuries on the hill
Legend Agastya’s penance; the Lord promises to stay.
1315 The temple is built. Sources disagree on the builder: most name a king Ballāḷa, called a Hoysaḷa (Vīra Ballāḷa) in some accounts and a local Cōḻa Ballāḷa of Terakaṇāmbi in others.
14th c. on The temple stands on the hill through Vijayanagara and later times.
Woḍeyars The Woḍeyars of Mysuru, devotees of Veṇugopāla, take a keen interest in maintaining the hill temple.
20th c. The hill falls within Bandipur, later a tiger reserve: the temple becomes a sanctuary within a sanctuary.

Which Ballāḷa? The Hoysaḷa king Vīra Ballāḷa III ruled the region in the early 1300s, so the Hoysaḷa reading fits the date. The “Cōḻa Ballāḷa” of Terakaṇāmbi, a nearby old town, may be a local ruler of the same name. We show both.

THE TEMPLE

Small, old and north-facing

🏯 The gateway

A single-tiered gopuram rests on the compound wall. Inside, the porch holds a dhvajastambha (flag-pillar) and a bali-pīṭha (altar).

🔟 Ten avatars

The parapet of the porch is carved with the Daśāvatāra, Viṣṇu’s ten avatars, with Kṛṣṇa at the centre. A śikhara rises over the sanctum.

Style. A compact Dravidian-style shrine, built to stand on an exposed, misty summit.

FESTIVALS

Brahma RathotsavaThe annual car festival in Phālguna (around March); the chariot is pulled not with ropes but with creepers gathered from the forest
Śrāvaṇa SaturdaysSpecial pūjās every Saturday in the month of Śrāvaṇa, drawing thousands of devotees
JātreA fair held with the festival

ZAPPY: Why creepers and not ropes?

PROF. BOLT: Because the forest is part of the family here! Bamboo creepers from Bandipur pull the Lord's chariot. Nice touch, no?

A TEMPLE IN A TIGER RESERVE

Where the Lord shares his hill
BandipurThe hill lies in the core of Bandipur National Park, under Project Tiger
WildlifeTigers, leopards and wild dogs; elephants, gaur, chital and sambar; peacocks and forest birds
TreesRosewood, teak and other forest trees on the slopes
ProtectionUnder the Wildlife (Protection) Act, the forest beyond the temple is protected: no trekking or wandering

A sanctuary within a sanctuary. Few great temples stand inside a tiger reserve. Here devotion and conservation share the same summit: the Lord on the hill, and the forest kept for the animals around him.

Read more about the tigers of this forest in our story Bandipur’s tigers.

ZAPPY: So the Lord protects the cows and the forest protects the tigers?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. Gopāla is the protector. Here he gets to protect everybody!
