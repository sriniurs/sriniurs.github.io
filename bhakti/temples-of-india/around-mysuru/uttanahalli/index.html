UTTANAHAḶḶI

JVĀLĀMUKHĪ TRIPURASUNDARĪ · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Cāmuṇḍī’s sister at the foot of the hill

FOREWORD

Everyone in Mysuru knows Cāmuṇḍeśvarī on her hill. Fewer know that at the foot of that hill lives her sister.

She is Jvālāmukhī Tripurasundarī of Uttanahaḷḷi: flame-faced, long-tongued, the Goddess who, the stories say, drank the demon’s blood so Cāmuṇḍī could win.

This guide tells her names, her legends and her temple.

Jai Mā,
srini.presents 🙏

WHERE IT IS

At the foot of the Goddess’s hill

Below the hill. The temple stands at Uttanahaḷḷi, a village at the foot of Cāmuṇḍī Hills, on Mysuru’s edge; a hill road branches down toward it from the top.

Raised and walled. The shrine sits on slightly raised ground, inside a compound wall, with a new courtyard built around it.

One pilgrimage, two sisters. Many families who worship Cāmuṇḍī on the hill also come down to pray to her sister.

HER NAMES

Fire-faced, and beautiful in three worlds
Jvālāmukhī“She whose face is flame”: the fierce Goddess
Jvālāmālinī“Garlanded with flames”, a name used in some accounts
Tripurasundarī“The beauty of the three worlds”: the name of Lalitā, the gracious supreme Goddess
Uri MāriIn a local folk story, the fiery sister born from Cāmuṇḍī’s sweat
Ammanavaru“The Mother”, as villagers simply call her

Fierce and gentle together. The name joins two faces of the one Goddess: the flame that burns away evil, and the beauty that rules the worlds. Here she is both at once.

ZAPPY: Why would a fierce goddess be called 'beautiful'?

PROF. BOLT: Because for her devotees, the power that protects them is the most beautiful thing of all!

THE GODDESS

A fierce face at the foot of the hill

👅 The image

In the sanctum the Goddess has a long, outstretched tongue, much like the Kālī of Kālīghāṭ in Kolkata: the tongue that drank the demon’s blood.

🪷 The festival image

A gentler utsava (festival) image of Tripurasundarī has its own shrine.

Footprints. Stones in the temple grounds carry what devotees believe are the Goddess’s footprints. There are also shrines of Gaṇeśa and of Śiva as Siddheśvara.

THE LEGEND · I

Raktabīja: the demon of a thousand copies
The demon. In the Devī Māhātmya, the demon Raktabīja, “blood-seed”, had a terrible boon: every drop of his blood that touched the ground rose up as another Raktabīja.
An army of copies. The more the Goddess’s forces wounded him, the more demons filled the battlefield.
The tongue. The Goddess as Cāmuṇḍā / Kālī spread out her great tongue and drank every drop before it could fall, until Raktabīja was bled dry and slain.
At Uttanahaḷḷi. Local tradition says it was Cāmuṇḍeśvarī’s sister, Jvālāmukhī, who helped her on that battlefield; hence the long tongue of her image.

The full Raktabīja battle is in Chapter 8 of the Devī Māhātmya: follow our Devī Māhātmya series.

THE LEGEND · II

Seven sisters and the sweat of the Goddess
Seven daughters. A local folk story says a king of Ujjain, Bijjalarāya, had seven warrior daughters. The eldest was Urimasaṇi, Piriyāpaṭṭaṇadamma; the youngest was Cāmuṇḍī.
South. After a quarrel the sisters scattered across the south and settled. Only Cāmuṇḍī found no home, until she came to Mahiṣamaṇḍala, Mahiṣāsura’s land.
The war. Mahiṣāsura wanted to marry her; she refused, and they fought. Every drop of his blood made demons. Hunting him, Cāmuṇḍī sweated, and from her sweat rose a fierce woman, Uri Māri, who said, “I will spread my tongue over all.”
A home for her. Uri Māri drank the demon’s blood and the war was won. Grateful, Cāmuṇḍī asked her to live at the foot of the hill, at Uttanahaḷḷi. She is Jvālāmukhī, and those who worship Cāmuṇḍī pray to her too.

THE LEGEND · III

The sister who held the foot of the hill

An oral telling. Some old Mysuru families tell it this way: when Cāmuṇḍī climbed the hill to fight Mahiṣāsura at the top, her younger sister stayed at its foot and slew as many demons as she could there, so that fewer reached the summit.

Elder or younger? The tellings differ on which sister was older, and even on which demon the sister helped to kill: Raktabīja, Mahiṣāsura, or both. All agree she fought beside Cāmuṇḍī, and stayed at the hill’s foot.

ZAPPY: So who's older, Cāmuṇḍī or Jvālāmukhī?

PROF. BOLT: Depends on who's telling! In every version, though, she's the sister who never let Cāmuṇḍī fight alone.

HISTORY

Royal patrons and old priests
Woḍeyar times The temple’s image is said to have been installed under the Woḍeyars, whose family Goddess was Cāmuṇḍeśvarī.
Hill and foot Researcher Shalva Pille Iyengar records that the Śivārcakas, the earlier priests of the Cāmuṇḍeśvarī temple, were given the Jvālāmukhī temple, renovated, after they protested when worship on the hill changed hands.
Mummaḍi Kṛṣṇarāja He brought priests from Kāñcīpuram to the hill temple on the advice of the Kāñci seer; old styles of worship continued at the foot of the hill.
Today A temple of the Karnataka Muzrāi (Endowments) Department, recently renovated, with a new courtyard around it.

Note. No dated inscription for the temple appeared in the sources we checked; the order of events in the middle rows is the researcher’s account as reported, not a recorded date.

THROUGH THE YEAR

When the sisters are worshipped
Āṣāḍha FridaysThe four or five Fridays of Āṣāḍha (June–July), when vast crowds come to Cāmuṇḍī Hills; the administration plans for both Cāmuṇḍeśvarī and Tripurasundarī together
NavarātriThe nine nights of the Goddess, the time of Mysuru Dasara
Fridays and TuesdaysThe Goddess’s days, when local families visit
AbhiṣekaThe morning bath of the Goddess, early each day

A Mysuru vow. Āṣāḍha Friday worship, kept as a vrata by many Mysuru families, has grown hugely in recent decades; many make the climb to Cāmuṇḍī and the visit to Uttanahaḷḷi on the same day.
