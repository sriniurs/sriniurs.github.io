TIRUMAKŪḌALU NARASIPURA

AGASTYEŚVARA · GUÑJA NARASIṂHA · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Where three waters meet

FOREWORD

South-east of Mysuru, the Kapilā runs into the Kāverī, and tradition says a third, hidden river rises to meet them. This is Tirumakūḍalu, the sacred meeting of three.

On one bank Śiva sits as a liṅga of sand that a sage made in a hurry; on the other Narasiṃha holds a tiny red seed that makes this place, they say, just a little holier than Kāśī.

This guide tells the stories of the rivers, the temples and the Kumbha Mela.

WHERE IT IS

Where three become one

The rivers. At T. Narasipura the Kapilā (the Kabini of Nañjanagūḍu) flows into the Kāverī. Tradition adds a third: the Sphaṭika Sarovara, a crystal lake said to rise unseen at the very meeting point.

Three-in-one. The Skanda Purāṇa counts such places of three waters as trimakūṭa kṣetras, among the holiest of all bathing places.

A rich land. The river-fed fields grow rice and silk; Talakāḍu, the old Gaṅga capital, lies in the same taluk.

Say it fast. Locals shorten it to T. Narasipura or simply Narsipur, but the full name tells the whole story: a holy three-river meeting, with Narasiṃha on its bank.

THE LEGEND OF AGASTYEŚVARA

A liṅga of sand
The sage’s wish. The sage Agastya, charmed by the meeting of the three waters, wanted to install a liṅga of Śiva here, and asked Hanumān to bring one from Kāśī.
The hour. The auspicious moment came, and Hanumān had not returned. So Agastya shaped a liṅga out of the river sand and consecrated it: a saikata liṅga.
Hanumān’s anger. Hanumān arrived, felt slighted, and struck off the top of the sand liṅga.
The water. From that cut summit, water has welled up ever since, said to be the water of the Gaṅgā; priests give it to pilgrims as tīrtha. The liṅga Hanumān brought was installed nearby: the Hanumān liṅga.

ZAPPY: Hanumān chopped a liṅga in a temper?

PROF. BOLT: He's devoted, not perfect! And the story ends well: two liṅgas instead of one, and a spring of holy water.

AGASTYEŚVARA TEMPLE

The older temple of the town
The liṅgaA saikata (sand) liṅga with a hollow at the top that always holds water; when filled, it overflows through an opening called the nābhi, the navel
AgeOlder than the Guñja Narasiṃha temple; its complex holds work of the Gaṅgas, Cōḻas, Hoysaḷas and Vijayanagara
InsideThe Hanumān liṅga in its own sanctum; pillared halls and old carvings
FestivalAn annual car festival and fair

Five liṅgas. Tradition also names a local Pañcaliṅga of T. Narasipura: Agastyeśvara, the Hanumān liṅga, Someśvara, Mārkaṇḍeśvara, and Gargeśvara at Gargeśvari village.

THE LEGEND OF GUÑJA NARASIṂHA

Holier than Kāśī by one seed
A washerman’s wish. A washerman who washed clothes every day at the confluence longed to go on pilgrimage to Kāśī.
A dream. Narasiṃha appeared to him in a dream and told him that his image lay beneath the very stone on which he beat the clothes, and that there was no need to go to Kāśī.
One seed more. The Lord declared that a pilgrimage here would bring merit greater than Kāśī’s by the weight of one guñja seed: the tiny red-and-black seed once used by goldsmiths as a weight.
The proof. The image of Narasiṃha here holds a guñja berry in his hand, and a guñja tree grows before the temple gate.

ZAPPY: A whole pilgrimage won by one tiny seed?

PROF. BOLT: A guñja seed weighs about an eighth of a gram! It's the town's proudest boast: just a little holier than Kāśī.

GUÑJA NARASIṂHA TEMPLE

A Vijayanagara temple on the bank
AgeVijayanagara period, about the 16th century; often said to be about 450 years old
InscriptionsRecords from the time of Kṛṣṇadevarāya, and many records in Nāgarī script
StyleDravidian with Hoysaḷa touches: an imposing gopura over the main gate, a four-pillared maṇḍapa before the sanctum, and a prākāra with minor shrines
PatronsLater, the Daḷavāy of Mysuru paid a yearly allowance for its upkeep (B. Lewis Rice)
The GoddessNarasiṃha is worshipped with Mahālakṣmī; there are shrines of the Āḻvārs and ācāryas
TodayA protected monument of the Archaeological Survey of India

THE KUMBHA MELA

South India’s great bath

Since 1989. T. Narasipura holds a Kumbha Mela every three years, begun in 1989: South India’s answer to the great melas of Prayāgarāj and Nāsik.

The bath. Lakhs of devotees, and sādhus from across the country, take the holy dip at the confluence of the three waters.

Old waters, new festival. The place is ancient; the Kumbha Mela here is recent. Both are part of its story.

ZAPPY: Can I do the Kumbha bath without going all the way north?

PROF. BOLT: That was exactly the idea! Every three years, Prayāga comes to Mysuru's doorstep.

HISTORY

From the Stone Age to the Woḍeyars
c. 2000 BCE A Neolithic settlement on the left bank of the Kāverī, near the Bhikṣeśvara temple: burials, pots, stone and metal tools, beads and bangles (excavated 1959–65).
Gaṅgas to Hoysaḷas Agastyeśvara and the temples around it gather work of the Gaṅgas, Cōḻas and Hoysaḷas.
16th c. Vijayanagara: the Guñja Narasiṃha temple, with records of Kṛṣṇadevarāya’s time.
Mysuru The Daḷavāys and kings of Mysuru support the temples; Mādhva maṭhas, including the Vyāsarāja maṭha at Sosale, flourish nearby.
1989 The first T. Narasipura Kumbha Mela.

Sons of the soil. The great Mādhva saint Vyāsatīrtha, guru of Purandara Dāsa, was born in this taluk, and the violinist T. Chowdiah was born in the town.
