AHOBILAM

NALLAMALA HILLS · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Where the man-lion burst from the pillar

FOREWORD

A demon king struck a pillar and dared his son’s God to come out. He did: Narasiṃha, the man-lion. Tradition says it happened here, in the gorges of the Nallamala hills, and points to a split rock peak as the pillar.

In these hills the Lord is worshipped in nine forms, from blazing to blissful. Here, too, a forest girl calmed him, and her people still call him their son-in-law.

This guide walks the nine shrines, tells the legends, and follows the kings and the Jīyars who kept them.

Jai Narasiṃha,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Do people really walk to all nine?

PROF. BOLT: Many do, some barefoot. Two are by the road; the rest are forest climbs. Pāvana is the hardest.

ZAPPY: Nine Narasiṃhas in one forest?

PROF. BOLT: Nine shrines, nine moods: fierce, calm, in yoga, with Lakṣmī, under a tree. Ahobilam is all of him.

WHERE IT IS

In the Nallamala hills

The forest. Ahobilam lies in the Nallamala hills, a wild stretch of the Eastern Ghats full of gorges, streams and caves, home of the forest-dwelling Cheñcu people.

Two Ahobilams. Lower Ahobilam (Diguva, “below”) is the village with the great Prahlādavarada temple. Upper Ahobilam (Eguva, “above”) is about 8 km up the ravine, with the oldest shrine and the trails to the others.

Neighbours. Śrīśailam lies north in the same hills; an old record measures Ahobilam’s distance from it.

THE NAMES

Strength, and a cave
Aho balam!“Oh, what strength!”: what the gods cried when they saw Narasiṃha kill Hiraṇyakaśipu
Aho bilam!“Oh, what a cave!”: the great cave where Garuḍa saw the Lord
Garuḍādri“Garuḍa’s hill”, for his penance, and because the peaks look like Garuḍa
Vedādri“Hill of the Vedas”, another name of the range
Siṅgavēḷ KuṉṟamThe Tamil name: “the hill of the lion”
Diguva / EguvaTelugu for Lower and Upper Ahobilam

A verse sung by pilgrims: aho vīryam aho śauryam aho bāhuparākramaḥ | nārasiṃhaṃ paraṃ daivam ahobilam aho balam: “Oh his valour, his courage, the might of his arms! Narasiṃha is the highest God: Ahobilam, oh what strength!”

ZAPPY: Strength or cave: which is right?

PROF. BOLT: Both, in one cry. Aho-bala, aho-bila. The place is a pun the gods made.

THE LEGEND

Prahlāda and the pillar
The boon. The demon king Hiraṇyakaśipu won a boon: no man or beast could kill him, not by day or night, not inside or outside, not on earth or in the sky, by no weapon.
The son. His little son Prahlāda worshipped Viṣṇu anyway. Torture could not stop him. “Is your Viṣṇu in this pillar too?” the king roared, and struck it.
The man-lion. From the pillar burst Narasiṃha, neither man nor beast. At dusk, on the threshold, holding the demon on his lap, he tore him apart with his claws: every condition of the boon kept, and broken.
Here. The Bhāgavata tells the story; Ahobilam’s tradition says it happened in these hills.

THE UGRA STAMBHA

The split rock

The pillar. High above Upper Ahobilam, a great rock peak is split into two by a narrow cleft. Pilgrims call it the Ugra Stambha, “the fierce pillar”: the pillar from which Narasiṃha emerged.

The climb. It is a steep, rough climb beyond the Jvālā Narasiṃha shrine, done by the hardy few.

Prahlāda Meṭṭu. Between the stambha and Upper Ahobilam, a small cave shrine on the hillside is dedicated to Prahlāda, the boy whose faith brought the Lord out of stone.

ZAPPY: A mountain is the pillar?

PROF. BOLT: When God steps out of a pillar, the pillar has to be the size of a mountain.

NINE MOODS

How the nine differ

Ugra: fierce

Jvālā: blazing

Mālola: with Lakṣmī

Yogānanda: in yoga

Ugra and Saumya. The nine run from fierce (Ahobila, Jvālā) to gentle (Mālola, Pāvana) and meditative (Yogānanda). The Prahlādavarada temple below, built last, shows him calm, granting boons to the boy: the story’s whole arc, laid out across a forest.

THE LEGEND · GARUḌA

The eagle’s penance
A wish. Garuḍa, Viṣṇu’s eagle, longed to see the Lord in his Narasiṃha form. His father, the sage Kaśyapa, sent him to these hills to do penance.
The vision. After long austerity, Narasiṃha appeared to him in a great cave, the aho-bila. The hills became Garuḍādri.
Nine forms. The local account says that the Lord then took his nine forms in these hills, for Garuḍa and for the sages who followed, Paraśurāma among them.

CHEÑCU LAKṢMĪ

The forest wedding
Still blazing. After the killing, the story goes, Narasiṃha wandered the Nallamala forest, still in his terrible form. The gods asked Lakṣmī to calm him.
The forest girl. She was born among the Cheñcus, the forest people, as Cheñcu Lakṣmī. The Lord saw her, fell in love, and asked to marry her; some say he refused ever to leave.
In-laws. The Cheñcus still treat Narasiṃha as their son-in-law and Cheñcu Lakṣmī as their daughter. Pillars in both temples show the pair.
In a play. The seventh Jīyar of the Ahobila Maṭha wrote a Sanskrit drama of the wedding, the Vāsantikā Pariṇayam, in the 16th century.

THE PRAHLĀDAVARADA TEMPLE

Lower Ahobilam
DeityPrahlādavarada Lakṣmī-Narasiṃha: “the boon-giver to Prahlāda”, the calm form; Goddess Amṛtavallī
BuiltMostly 16th century, possibly begun under Sāḷuva Narasiṃha (15th c.); Reḍḍi beginnings are also claimed
PlanThree enclosures, an east-facing seven-tiered gopura, a victory pillar (jayastambha) in front
CarvingPillared halls full of Vijayanagara sculpture: musicians, dancers, Rāma, Narasiṃha with Cheñcu Lakṣmī, and a figure of Narasa Nāyaka
Not one of the nineBuilt after the Āḻvār’s time, it is counted apart from the Nava Narasiṃhas

HISTORY

Kings of the man-lion
9th c. Tirumaṅgai Āḻvār sings of Ahobilam in the Periya Tirumoḻi: it becomes a Divya Deśam.
12th–16th c. Sanskrit and Telugu works mention the shrines.
13th–14th c. Kākatīya and Reḍḍi kings patronise the hills.
15th c. The Sāḷuvas; Prahlādavarada may be begun under Sāḷuva Narasiṃha Rāya.
16th c. The Tuluvas: most inscriptions date from now; Kṛṣṇadevarāya visits and gives gifts.
1579 A Golconda commander, Murahari Rao, raids Ahobilam; the jewelled image is carried off.
After With Vijayanagara gone, royal patronage fades; the Ahobila Maṭha keeps worship alive.

Old copper-plate records once kept near the Cheñcu Lakṣmī shrine are said to have been destroyed for their metal: a loss for history.

THE AHOBILA MAṬHA

Carrying Mālola across India
The founder. The maṭha traces itself to Ādivaṇ Śaṭhakopa Jīyar, a Vaḍakalai Śrī Vaiṣṇava teacher of the early 1400s. Tradition says Narasiṃha himself called him to Ahobilam and gave him sannyāsa; some scholars date the maṭha a century later.
The image. Legend says the festival image of Mālola Narasiṃha leapt into his hands and told him to travel. Ever since, each Jīyar carries Mālola with him wherever he goes.
Today. The maṭha, with its line of more than forty-five Jīyars, looks after the shrines and is one of the great institutions of Śrī Vaiṣṇavism.

ZAPPY: The god travels with the monk?

PROF. BOLT: For six hundred years. Wherever the Jīyar camps, Mālola is worshipped.

ZAPPY: Even Tirupati's poet came here?

PROF. BOLT: Tradition says he took his vows here. The hills of Tirumala and Ahobilam are on the same pilgrim road.
