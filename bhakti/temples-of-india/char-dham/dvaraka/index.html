DVĀRAKĀ

CHAR DHAM · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Kṛṣṇa’s city at the edge of the sea

FOREWORD

Kṛṣṇa grew up a cowherd by the Yamunā. He died a king by the sea. The kingdom in between was Dvārakā, a city of gates he built on land the ocean gave him, and which the ocean took back when he was gone.

Today a tall temple stands on the shore where his palace is said to have been, flying a flag with the sun and moon. Divers search the water below for the rest of the city.

This guide tells the story, the temple, the queens and friends around it, and the debate under the waves.

WHERE IT IS

The western edge

Land’s end. Dvārakā sits at the western tip of Saurāṣṭra, the last town before the open sea. The Gomatī creek meets the ocean right below the temple steps.

A city of three parts. Tradition counts the mainland town, the island of Bet Dvārakā to the north and the Śiva shrine at Nāgeśvara in between as one sacred Dvārakā.

Gate, and gateway
DvārakāFrom dvāra, “door, gate”: the gateway, to the sea and to liberation

Dvārakādhīś“Lord of Dvārakā”

ZAPPY: God is called 'the one who ran away'?

PROF. BOLT: Proudly. He left the battle to save his people. Sometimes the bravest thing is to walk away.

THE LEGEND · LEAVING MATHURĀ

Why Kṛṣṇa moved west
Attacked again and again. After Kṛṣṇa killed the tyrant Kaṃsa, Kaṃsa’s father-in-law Jarāsandha attacked Mathurā seventeen times.
The move. To spare his people, Kṛṣṇa led the Yādavas west to the coast, to Kuśasthalī.
A city from the sea. He asked the ocean for land, and Viśvakarmā, the gods’ architect, built a golden city of many gates on it: Dvārakā.
Raṇchoḍ. For leaving the fight, Kṛṣṇa earned the name “the one who left the battlefield”, worshipped here with love.

THE CITY UNDER THE SEA

When Kṛṣṇa left
The end of the Yādavas. The Mahābhārata’s Mausala Parva tells how, years after the war, the Yādavas destroyed one another in a drunken brawl, and Kṛṣṇa left the world.
The sea returns. Arjuna came to lead the survivors away. As they left, the ocean rose and swallowed Dvārakā, street by street.
What remains. Tradition says the present temple stands on the spot of Kṛṣṇa’s own palace, the Hari-gṛha.

ZAPPY: Is there really a city down there?

PROF. BOLT: Divers have found walls, stone blocks and anchors offshore. What they are, and how old, is the argument.

THE UNDERSEA FINDS

Evidence and argument

🌊 The finds

From 1979 and through the 1980s, marine archaeologists led by S. R. Rao, later at the National Institute of Oceanography, mapped submerged walls, blocks and stone anchors off Dvārakā and Bet Dvārakā.

🔎 The argument

Rao saw a planned, walled port city matching the Mahābhārata’s description. Other scholars date many finds to much later periods and say they cannot be tied to Kṛṣṇa’s city.

On land. Excavations in the town in the 1960s found settlement layers many centuries old, and archaeology suggests an early temple on the site from around 200 BCE at the earliest.

Dating. Rao himself noted that the undersea finds could not be dated directly and relied on pottery and sea-level change. The debate is open.

ZAPPY: So archaeology proves the legend?

PROF. BOLT: It proves people lived and built here very long ago. Whether it was his city is still argued.

THE JAGAT MANDIR

Five storeys by the sea
BuildingFive storeys on 72 pillars; sanctum, antechamber and a great hall; Māru-Gurjara (Cālukya) style, of limestone and sandstone
FoundingTradition says Vajranābha, Kṛṣṇa’s great-grandson, built the first temple over the Hari-gṛha
Two gatesSvarga Dvāra, the “gate to heaven”, opens onto 56 steps down to the Gomatī; Mokṣa Dvāra leads to the town (sources differ on which is entry and which exit)
WorshipRun in the Puṣṭimārga style of Vallabhācārya
Also withinShrines of Balarāma, Pradyumna, Aniruddha and others

THE FLAG

52 yards, five times a day
SizeA huge flag, said to be 52 yards long, flies from the spire
SymbolsThe sun and moon: Kṛṣṇa will be here as long as they shine
ChangedUp to five times a day; families vow and sponsor a flag, and wait their turn
52Said to stand for the clans of the Yādavas
56 stepsThe steps to the Gomatī are said to stand for 56 Yādava rulers

ZAPPY: People queue to give a flag?

PROF. BOLT: For years sometimes. Seeing your family's flag fly over Dvārakādhīś is a once-in-a-life moment.

RUKMIṆĪ’S TEMPLE

The queen who lives apart
WhereJust outside the town, a little to the north
TempleA small, finely carved shrine, usually dated to the 12th century
LegendKṛṣṇa and Rukmiṇī pulled the sage Durvāsā’s chariot. When Rukmiṇī, thirsty, drank water without first offering him any, he cursed her to live apart from her husband
SoPilgrims see Dvārakādhīś in town and Rukmiṇī out here, and complete the visit only by seeing both
WaterThe curse is also said to make fresh water scarce here: pilgrims donate water at the temple

ZAPPY: A queen cursed for a sip of water?

PROF. BOLT: Durvāsā was famous for his temper. Even Kṛṣṇa couldn't talk him down.

The island home
WhereAn island off Okhā, at the mouth of the Gulf of Kachchh
Also calledŚaṅkhoddhāra: where Viṣṇu is said to have slain the demon Śaṅkhāsura
TraditionKṛṣṇa’s family residence, with palace-shrines for his queens

ZAPPY: Sudāmā never even asked?

PROF. BOLT: That's the point. Kṛṣṇa ate the rice, and Sudāmā found a palace where his hut had been.

HISTORY

Sacked, rebuilt, still standing
Legend Vajranābha raises the first temple over Kṛṣṇa’s palace.
c. 200 BCE Earliest likely date of a temple on the site, from archaeology.
8th c. Ādi Śaṅkara visits; the Śāradā Pīṭha, by tradition.
12th c. Rukmiṇī’s temple is built.
1472–73 Mahmud Begada, Sultan of Gujarat, sacks the town and destroys the temple.
15th–16th c. The present Jagat Mandir is built and enlarged.
16th c. Vallabhācārya; worship follows the Puṣṭimārga.
1960s onward Excavations on land, then under the sea.
