WHERE GAṄGĀ CAME DOWN

Srini presents

GAṄGOTRĪ

CHAR DHAM · A REFERENCE GUIDE
A king’s penance, Śiva’s hair, heaven’s river

FOREWORD

Sixty thousand princes lay as ashes, and only the river of heaven could free them. Their descendant, King Bhagīratha, gave up his throne and sat in penance on a rock in the Himalaya until Gaṅgā agreed to come down, and Śiva agreed to catch her.

That rock is beside a white temple at Gaṅgotrī. The river rushing past still carries the king’s name, the Bhāgīrathī, and high above, it pours out of a cave of ice.

This guide tells the legend, the shrine and the places around it.

ZAPPY: Is the temple at the actual source?

PROF. BOLT: Close. The true source is the glacier's mouth, higher up. The temple marks where Bhagīratha prayed.

AT A GLANCE

The essential facts on one page
GoddessGaṅgā, white-robed, riding a makara
RiverThe Bhāgīrathī: named after King Bhagīratha; it becomes the Gaṅgā at Devaprayāg
WhereUttarkashi district, Uttarakhand, about 3,100 m up, on the bank of the Bhāgīrathī
The sourceGaumukha, “the cow’s mouth”: the snout of the Gaṅgotrī glacier, a long walk upstream
LegendBhagīratha’s penance brought Gaṅgā from heaven; Śiva caught her in his hair
TempleWhite granite, built by the Gorkha general Amar Singh Thapa
SeasonOpen from Akṣaya Tṛtīyā to Dīpāvalī; in winter Gaṅgā lives at Mukhbā

ZAPPY: If this is where Gaṅgā starts, why is the river called Bhāgīrathī?

PROF. BOLT: Because a king brought her. She keeps his name until she meets the Alakanandā.

WHERE IT IS

The second of four

The Bhāgīrathī valley. Gaṅgotrī stands on the river’s right bank in a deep pine valley, ringed by high peaks. It is the second stop on the Himalayan Char Dham, after Yamunotrī.

Upstream. The glacier’s snout, Gaumukha, lies much higher; beyond it is Tapovana, a meadow below the peak called Śivaliṅga.

THE LEGEND · KING SAGARA

Sixty thousand sons
The horse. King Sagara of Ayodhyā, of the Sun dynasty, held a great horse sacrifice. Indra stole the horse and hid it beside the sage Kapila.
The sons. Sagara’s 60,000 sons dug through the earth to find it, found it by Kapila, and accused him of theft.
Ashes. Kapila opened his eyes, and his fire burned them all to ashes. Only the waters of heaven, Gaṅgā, could purify them and free their souls.

THE LEGEND · BHAGĪRATHA

Bringing heaven down
Generations of effort. Sagara’s grandson Aṃśumān and his son Dilīpa tried and failed. Dilīpa’s son Bhagīratha left his throne and did fierce penance, here by tradition, on a rock now called the Bhagīratha Śilā.
Brahmā agrees. Pleased, Brahmā granted that Gaṅgā would descend. But her fall from heaven would shatter the earth.
One more penance. So Bhagīratha prayed to Śiva, the only one strong enough to take her weight.

ZAPPY: That's why we say a 'Bhagīratha effort'?

PROF. BOLT: Three generations, one river. Any near-impossible task finished by sheer persistence is 'Bhagīratha prayatna'.

ŚIVA AND THE RIVER

Caught in his hair
The fall. Proud Gaṅgā thought she would sweep Śiva away and fell with all her force.
The hair. Śiva caught her in his matted locks, the jaṭā, and held her there, wandering in the tangles, until her pride was gone.
Released gently. Then he let her out in a gentle stream. That is why Śiva is Gaṅgādhara, “bearer of Gaṅgā”.
The submerged liṅga. In the river by the temple is a natural rock liṅga, said to mark where Śiva sat to receive her. It shows only in winter, when the water is low.

JAHNU AND THE SEA

The rest of the journey
Bhagīratha leadsThe king drove his chariot ahead; Gaṅgā followed him across the land
JahnuShe flooded the hermitage of the sage Jahnu, who drank her up in anger. At Bhagīratha’s plea, he let her out from his ear. So she is Jāhnavī, “Jahnu’s daughter”
The seaAt last she reached the ocean, at Gaṅgāsāgara, flowed over the ashes, and freed the 60,000 sons
Three worldsShe flows in heaven, on earth and in the underworld: Tripathagā

ZAPPY: So Gaṅgā has three fathers' names on her?

PROF. BOLT: Bhāgīrathī for the king who called her, Jāhnavī for the sage who freed her, Gaṅgā for herself.

THE TEMPLE

White granite by the river
BuilderAmar Singh Thapa, the Gorkha general who ruled Garhwal for Nepal in the early 19th century
StoneWhite granite, with a curved tower
RestoredLater in the 19th century; some accounts credit a ruler of Jaipur with renewing it
ImagesGaṅgā, with Yamunā, Sarasvatī, Lakṣmī, Annapūrṇā, Bhagīratha and Ādi Śaṅkara, by most accounts
PriestsThe Semvāl family, from the village of Mukhbā

ZAPPY: A Nepali general built it?

PROF. BOLT: During Nepal's rule over Garhwal. Faith crossed the border before borders did.

AROUND GAṄGOTRĪ

Rock, liṅga, springs and caves
Bhagīratha ŚilāThe slab beside the temple where Bhagīratha meditated; Gaṅgā is said to have touched earth here
Jalamagna ŚivaliṅgaThe submerged natural liṅga in the river, seen when the water falls
Gaurī Kuṇḍa, Sūrya KuṇḍaPools a little downstream where the river roars through narrow rock channels
Pāṇḍava GuphāCaves where the Pāṇḍavas are said to have done penance
BhairavaBhairon Ghāṭī, below Gaṅgotrī, with a Bhairava temple that guards the way

GAUMUKHA

The cow’s mouth
WhatThe snout of the Gaṅgotrī glacier, where the Bhāgīrathī bursts out of an ice cave
NameIts shape was once likened to a cow’s mouth: gau-mukha
RetreatingThe glacier has been shrinking; the snout has moved back and changed shape over the decades
BeyondTapovana, a high meadow below the peaks Śivaliṅga and Meru
PilgrimsSome walk up to take water from the very source

ZAPPY: The 'cow's mouth' doesn't look like a cow any more?

PROF. BOLT: Glaciers move. The ice retreats; the faith stays put.

THE YEAR AT GAṄGOTRĪ

Summer up, winter down
OpeningOn Akṣaya Tṛtīyā, Gaṅgā’s image is carried up from Mukhbā
Gaṅgā DaśaharāJyeṣṭha, the tenth bright day: the day Gaṅgā is said to have descended
ClosingOn Dīpāvalī, the temple shuts and she is carried in a palanquin to Mukhbā
WinterWorship continues at Mukhbā, the priests’ home village, near Harsil
Gaṅgā jalaPilgrims carry water home from here, for rites and the last hours of the dying
