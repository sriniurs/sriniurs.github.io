BADRĪNĀTH

CHAR DHAM · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The Lord in the jujube forest

FOREWORD

High in the Garhwal Himalaya, where the Alakanandā runs between two mountains named after two sages, Viṣṇu is said to sit in meditation that has never ended. Lakṣmī stands over him as a tree.

His temple is open for only half the year. His priest comes from Kerala, at the other end of India. And his abode is the one place that belongs to both Char Dhams: the great circle around India and the four shrines of the Himalaya.

This guide tells the legends, the history and the sacred places of Badrīnāth.

Jai Badrī Viśāl,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why only half the year?

PROF. BOLT: Snow. From late autumn to spring the valley is buried, so the Lord's worship moves down to Jyotirmaṭh.

ZAPPY: Why is it called Badrī?

PROF. BOLT: Badarī is the jujube tree. The whole valley was once a jujube forest where Viṣṇu sat in penance.

WHERE IT IS

Between two mountains

The valley. Badrīnāth sits on the right bank of the Alakanandā, one of the two rivers that become the Gaṅgā, in a high valley ringed with snow.

Two mountains. The ranges on either side are named Nara and Nārāyaṇa, after the twin sages of the legend. Nīlakaṇṭha, a great snow pyramid, rises behind the temple.

Upstream. Mānā, the last village before the high passes, lies just above, where the little Sarasvatī joins the Alakanandā.

THE LEGEND · NARA-NĀRĀYAṆA

Penance since time began
The twin sages. The Bhāgavata says Viṣṇu came as two sages, Nara and Nārāyaṇa, sons of Dharma, and did penance at Badarī for the good of all beings.
Arjuna and Kṛṣṇa. In the Mahābhārata, Śiva tells Arjuna that in a former life he was Nara, doing fierce penance at Badarī with Nārāyaṇa. They were reborn as Arjuna and Kṛṣṇa.
Still sitting. The two mountains that bear their names are said to be the sages themselves, still in meditation.

THE LEGEND · THE BADRĪ TREE

Lakṣmī shades her Lord
Snow and sun. Viṣṇu sat so long in meditation here that he did not notice the snow, or later the burning sun.
The tree. Lakṣmī took the form of a badarī (jujube) tree and spread over him, shielding him through all seasons.
The name. Pleased, Viṣṇu named the place after her: Badarī-nātha, “Lord of the jujube”, the Lord who is hers.

ZAPPY: So 'Badrīnāth' really means Lakṣmī's Lord?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. The name is a thank-you note from Viṣṇu to his wife.

Self-manifestCounted among the eight svayaṃ-vyakta (self-manifested) images of Viṣṇu

ŚAṄKARA AND THE RIVER

The image found again
Lost. Tradition says the image had been hidden in the river long before, in troubled times; some accounts say it was thrown in.
Found. Ādi Śaṅkara is said to have recovered it from the Nārada Kuṇḍa, a pool in the Alakanandā, and enshrined it in a cave near the hot springs.
Moved. Centuries later, a king of Garhwal moved it to the present temple, in the 16th century by the usual account.
The priest. Śaṅkara, from Kerala, began the custom that the chief priest, the Rāval, should be a Nambūdiri brāhmaṇa from Kerala. It holds to this day.

THE RĀVAL

The south serves the north
WhoA Nambūdiri brāhmaṇa from Kerala, the head priest
Assisted byThe Ḍimrī paṇḍits, from the village of Ḍimmar in Garhwal
RiteThe worship follows southern rules, unusual in a North Indian temple
OfferingsSugar candy, tulasī and dried fruit

Two ends of India. Badrīnāth’s god sits in the far north; his priest comes from the far south. It is the clearest single picture of what the Char Dham means: one country, held together by pilgrims and priests travelling its length.

ZAPPY: Does the Rāval go home in winter?

PROF. BOLT: He goes down with the Lord to Jyotirmaṭh. Where Badrī Viśāl goes, his priest goes.

THE TEMPLE

A painted face in the snows
LookA tall, brightly painted stone front with arched windows: often said to resemble a Buddhist vihāra
PlanThree parts: the garbhagṛha (sanctum), the darśana maṇḍapa, and the sabhā maṇḍapa where pilgrims gather
RoofA gilded roof over the sanctum
RebuiltAgain and again, after avalanches and earthquakes

ZAPPY: Why does it look so different from southern temples?

PROF. BOLT: Snow, quakes and small valleys. Himalayan temples are built tight, tall and painted, not sprawling in stone.

HISTORY

A shrine that keeps coming back
Ancient Badarikāśrama is praised in the Mahābhārata and the Purāṇas.
Āḻvārs Tamil saints sing of “Vadari”: it becomes one of the 108 Divya Deśams.
8th–9th c. Ādi Śaṅkara re-establishes worship, by tradition; the cave shrine near Tapta Kuṇḍa.
16th c. A king of Garhwal moves the image to the present temple.
17th c. The Garhwal kings enlarge the temple.
1803 A great Garhwal earthquake badly damages it; the king of Jaipur funds the rebuilding.
British era The temple comes under British rule; the Garhwal king remains head of its committee.
1948 onward Managed under a state act, by a temple committee for Badrīnāth and Kedārnāth.

The Buddhist question. One tradition says the region was once Buddhist and the image was thrown into the river until Śaṅkara restored it. Others read the vihāra-like front as simply Himalayan style. The evidence is thin either way.

AROUND THE TEMPLE

Springs, a skull and a stone
Tapta KuṇḍaHot sulphur springs just below the temple: pilgrims bathe here before darśana, in air near freezing
Nārada KuṇḍaThe pool in the river where the image was found
Sūrya KuṇḍaA second sacred pool
Brahma KapālaA flat rock by the river where śrāddha rites for ancestors are done; held to give the dead the greatest peace
Pañca ŚilāFive sacred rocks around the springs, named after Nārada, Narasiṃha, Varāha, Garuḍa and Mārkaṇḍeya

ZAPPY: Hot water in the snows?

PROF. BOLT: God's own geyser. Tradition says Agni lives in Tapta Kuṇḍa, keeping it warm for the pilgrims.

MĀNĀ

Vyāsa’s cave and Bhīma’s bridge
MānāThe last village up the valley, just above Badrīnāth
Vyāsa GuphāThe cave where Vyāsa is said to have composed the Mahābhārata
Gaṇeśa GuphāWhere Gaṇeśa wrote it down as Vyāsa dictated
Bhīma PulA great rock across the Sarasvatī, said to have been laid by Bhīma so Draupadī could cross
VasudhārāA high waterfall beyond Mānā, on the path the Pāṇḍavas are said to have taken on their last journey to heaven

THE YEAR AT BADRĪNĀTH

Six months with men, six with gods
OpeningIn spring; the date is fixed each year on Vasanta Pañcamī
ClosingLate autumn, after the first snows
WinterThe festival image goes down to Jyotirmaṭh, where worship continues
The lampA lamp is left burning in the closed temple; tradition says it is found still alight in spring
BeliefFor six months people worship here; for the six of winter, the gods (and Nārada) take over
FestivalMātā Mūrti kā Melā, honouring the mother of Nara-Nārāyaṇa

ZAPPY: Kedārnāth and Badrīnāth are a pair?

PROF. BOLT: Śiva and Viṣṇu on neighbouring ridges. Tradition says do both, Kedār first.
