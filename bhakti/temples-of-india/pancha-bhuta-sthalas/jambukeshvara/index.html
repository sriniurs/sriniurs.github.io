Srini presents

JAMBUKEŚVARA

PAÑCABHŪTA STHALAS · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Tiruvānaikkāval: Śiva as water

FOREWORD

The second element flows. On the island of Śrīraṅgam in the Kāverī stands Jambukeśvara, where Śiva is worshipped as water. His liṅga sits in a low, small sanctum over a spring, and water quietly seeps up around it.

The place is named for an elephant: Tiru-āṉai-kkā, “the holy grove of the elephant”. An elephant and a spider both worshipped here, quarrelled, and died; the spider was reborn as the red-eyed Chola king Kocceṅgaṇ, who built temples no elephant could enter. Here too the Goddess Akhilāṇḍeśvarī came as a student, and every noon a priest dressed as her still offers worship to Śiva.

This guide tells the stories of the tree, the spider and the king, and walks through the five courtyards.

ZAPPY: A priest dressed as a woman?

PROF. BOLT: Every day at noon, as Akhilāṇḍeśvarī. It recalls her worship of Śiva here.

ZAPPY: Is the water really coming up from below?

PROF. BOLT: The sanctum sits over a spring. Priests and visitors describe water seeping around the liṅga, more in the rains.

THE SAGE AND THE ROSE-APPLE

Why “Jambukeśvara”
The fruit. A sage named Jambu did tapas, and Śiva gave him a rare rose-apple (jambu) fruit.
The seed. Out of reverence the sage swallowed it whole, seed and all, rather than throw away anything from Śiva’s hand.
The tree. A jambu tree sprang from his head, and he asked Śiva to stay forever beneath it. Śiva became Jambukeśvara, Lord of the jambu.

ZAPPY: A tree from his head?

PROF. BOLT: Sthala stories are vivid. The point: he wasted nothing that came from the Lord.

THE STUDENT GODDESS

Akhilāṇḍeśvarī learns from Śiva
Why she camePārvatī had laughed at Śiva’s austerity; he sent her to earth to do penance
Her liṅgaShe shaped a liṅga out of the water of the Kāverī, under the white jambu tree
The lessonŚiva appeared and taught her Śiva-jñāna, the knowledge of Śiva
FacingShe sat facing east as the student; he faced west as the teacher. The two shrines still face each other that way
Upadeśa sthalaA “place of teaching”: for this reason, it is said, no divine wedding festival is held here

ZAPPY: No wedding festival at a Śiva temple?

PROF. BOLT: Here they are guru and disciple, not bride and groom. So the wedding is left out.

THE NOON PŪJĀ

A priest dressed as the Goddess
WhenEvery day at the noon pūjā (ucchi-kāla)
WhatA priest from the Goddess’s shrine puts on a saree and goes to Jambukeśvara’s sanctum to offer worship
WhyHe re-enacts Akhilāṇḍeśvarī worshipping Śiva here, as she did in her penance
AlsoAfter the pūjā he is said to worship the cow as well, as the Goddess would

ZAPPY: Do people come just to see this?

PROF. BOLT: Many do. It's one of the most unusual daily rites in any Śiva temple.

THE SPIDER AND THE ELEPHANT

Two devotees, one quarrel
Two cursed gaṇas. Two of Śiva’s attendants, Mālyavān and Puṣpadanta, quarrelled and cursed each other: one became a spider, the other an elephant.
Two ways to worship. The elephant brought Kāverī water in its trunk and bathed the liṅga every day. The spider spun a web over it to keep off falling leaves and the sun.
The quarrel. The elephant thought the web was dirt and washed it away; the spider spun it again. At last the angry spider crept into the elephant’s trunk and bit it. In agony the elephant crushed itself and the spider died with it.

ZAPPY: Who was right?

PROF. BOLT: Both loved Śiva. Each thought the other was spoiling the worship. Śiva freed them both.

THE RED-EYED KING

Kocceṅgaṇ Cōḻaṉ
RebornThe elephant gained liberation; the spider was reborn as a Chola prince
Red eyesHis mother, told that a child born at a certain hour would rule the world, asked to be held upside down to delay the birth; the baby was born with reddened eyes: Ko-ceṅ-kaṇ, “king of the red eyes”
His vowRemembering his past life, he built temples raised on high platforms with narrow entrances that no elephant could enter: the māḍakkōyil
SeventyHe is credited with about 70 such temples; this one is the most famous
SaintHe is one of the 63 Nāyaṉmārs

ZAPPY: He held a grudge from his last life?

PROF. BOLT: Call it caution. The sanctum door here is still low and narrow.

THE KING IN HISTORY

Between legend and record
Sangam poemThe Kaḷavaḻi Nāṟpatu of the poet Poykaiyār celebrates a Chola king Ceṅkaṇāṉ who defeated the Cēra ruler Kaṇaikkāl Irumpoṟai
Same king?Tradition identifies him with Kocceṅgaṇ of the temples; historians place him in the early centuries CE, but dates are uncertain
UraiyūrThe early Chola capital, now part of Tiruchirāppaḷḷi
Later buildersCholas, Pāṇḍyas, Vijayanagara and Nāyaka rulers all added to the temple

ZAPPY: So was he real?

PROF. BOLT: Probably a real early king, wrapped in a beautiful story.

THE WATER SANCTUM

What you see inside
EntranceThe doorway to the sanctum is low and small; devotees bow to enter or to look
LiṅgaSmall, standing in a recess where water rises from the spring
WetThe water level rises in the rains; a cloth around the liṅga is often soaked
WindowDevotees view the liṅga through a small opening with nine holes, according to many descriptions
LessonThe water liṅga can only be seen by bending low

ŚAṄKARA AND THE GODDESS

The Śrī Cakra earrings
Fierce onceTradition says Akhilāṇḍeśvarī was once very fierce
ŚaṅkaraĀdi Śaṅkara is said to have calmed her by placing a pair of tāṭaṅka (ear ornaments) shaped as Śrī Cakras in her ears
Her sonHe also placed Gaṇeśa (Prasanna Vināyaka) facing her, so that seeing her child she would soften
RenewedThe earrings are re-polished and restored at intervals; the Kāñcī Kāmakoṭi Pīṭha is associated with this
OmSome say the Goddess and Vināyaka shrines together trace the shape of Om

ZAPPY: Earrings to calm a Goddess?

PROF. BOLT: Not ordinary ones. Each is a Śrī Cakra, her own diagram of power, turned to grace.

THE WALL BUILT BY ŚIVA

Tiruneeṟṟāṉ Matil
The storyWhen the outer wall was being built, Śiva came as a labourer, a siddha, and worked with the masons
The wagesHe paid the workers in sacred ash (vibhūti); for each one it turned to gold in proportion to the work done
The nameThe fifth enclosure wall is called Tiruneeṟṟāṉ Matil, “the wall of the one with holy ash”
The scaleThe outer wall runs for over a kilometre around the complex
MeaningHonest work is paid by the Lord exactly

ZAPPY: Ash that turned into gold?

PROF. BOLT: Only as much as you had earned. A fair employer, Śiva.

WHAT WATER TEACHES

The wisdom of the flowing element
HumilityThe water liṅga is seen only by bowing low
LearningEven the Goddess came here as a student
Devotion has many formsThe web and the water were both worship
Honest workThe wall paid in ash, turned gold by effort

ZAPPY: What should I take from water?

PROF. BOLT: Flow, bow, learn. And don't wash away someone else's way of loving God.
