ARUṆĀCALEŚVARA

PAÑCABHŪTA STHALAS · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Tiruvaṇṇāmalai: Śiva as fire

FOREWORD

The third element burns. At Tiruvaṇṇāmalai Śiva is worshipped as fire, and the whole red hill of Aruṇācala is held to be his body. Here, the story says, he once stood as an endless column of flame that neither Brahmā nor Viṣṇu could measure.

Every year on Kārttikai Dīpam a beacon is lit on the summit and seen for miles around. On full-moon nights tens of thousands walk barefoot around the hill. Below it spreads one of the largest temples in India, Aruṇācaleśvara, with towers raised by Hoysala and Vijayanagara kings. A sixteen-year-old boy came here in 1896 and stayed for life: Ramaṇa Maharṣi.

This guide tells the stories of the column and the hill, the festival and the walk, and the saints of Aruṇācala.

Aṇṇāmalaikku arōhara,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: A hill that is God?

PROF. BOLT: Here the temple is at the foot, but the hill itself is the liṅga. People walk around it as they would a shrine.

Part of the Pañcabhūta Sthalas, Śiva’s five temples of the five elements. Previous: Jambukeśvara, Tiruvānaikkāval (water). Next: Kāḷahastīśvara, Śrīkāḷahasti (air).

AT A GLANCE

The essential facts on one page
ElementFire (Agni): the third Pañcabhūta Sthala
Śiva asAruṇācaleśvara or Aṇṇāmalaiyār, Lord of the red hill
LiṅgaThe Agni liṅga in the temple, and the hill itself, about 800 m high, worshipped as Śiva
GoddessUnnāmulai Ammaṉ (Apītakucāmbā)
TempleAbout 10 hectares (25 acres); the eastern Rāja Gopuram is about 66 m tall
FestivalKārttikai Dīpam (Nov–Dec): the beacon on the summit
WalkGirivalam: about 14 km around the hill, barefoot, especially at full moon
SaintsAruṇagirinātar, Ramaṇa Maharṣi, and the Tēvāram poets

ZAPPY: Is this the biggest of the five?

PROF. BOLT: It is one of the largest temples in India. And no other has a whole mountain as its liṅga.

ZAPPY: Unreachable hill?

PROF. BOLT: Because in the story no one could reach the top of the fire. The name keeps the story.

THE ENDLESS COLUMN

Liṅgodbhava
The quarrel. Brahmā and Viṣṇu argued over which of them was the greater.
The column. Between them blazed up a pillar of fire without beginning or end. Whoever found an end, they agreed, would be the greater.
The search. Brahmā became a swan and flew up; Viṣṇu became a boar and dug down. Neither found the end.
The lie. Brahmā claimed he had seen the top, and brought a falling ketakī flower as false witness. Śiva appeared from the column, praised Viṣṇu’s honesty, and cursed the lie.

THE FIRE BECOMES A HILL

Why the hill is Śiva
The pleaThe fire was too fierce for the world to bear; the gods asked Śiva to take a gentler form
The hillHe cooled into the red hill of Aruṇācala, which keeps the fire within
Older than mountainsTradition calls it the oldest hill on earth; geologists do find very old rocks in this region
WorshipDevotees bow to the hill, walk around it, and treat it as the liṅga itself
The cursesBrahmā lost his worship in temples; the ketakī flower is not offered to Śiva

ZAPPY: So the beacon on Dīpam is the column again?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. Once a year the hill shows its fire.

HALF OF ŚIVA

Ardhanārīśvara at Aruṇācala
Her penancePārvatī did tapas at the foot of Aruṇācala
The giftŚiva gave her the left half of his body: he became Ardhanārīśvara, half woman
Once a yearThe Ardhanārīśvara image comes out on Kārttikai Dīpam, when the beacon is lit
MeaningŚiva and Śakti are one; neither is complete alone

ZAPPY: Half man, half woman?

PROF. BOLT: Half Śiva, half Pārvatī. The Tamil hymns love this form.

KĀRTTIKAI DĪPAM

The beacon on the summit
WhenFull moon of the Tamil month Kārttikai (Nov–Dec), at the end of a ten-day festival
At dawnBharaṇi Dīpam: a lamp in the sanctum is used to light five lamps for the five elements, which are brought back together into one
At duskAs the Ardhanārīśvara image appears, the Mahā Dīpam is lit in a great vessel on the summit, fed with large quantities of ghee
SeenFrom far across the plain, and kept burning for several days
CrowdsReckoned in millions, among the largest gatherings in Tamil Nadu

ZAPPY: Five lamps made into one?

PROF. BOLT: The five elements returning to their one source. The whole Pañcabhūta idea in one ritual.

GIRIVALAM

Walking around the hill
DistanceAbout 14 km, done barefoot, clockwise, keeping the hill on the right
WhenAny day; above all at full moon and on Kārttikai Dīpam
Eight liṅgasThe Aṣṭaliṅgas at the eight directions: Indra, Agni, Yama, Nirṛti, Varuṇa, Vāyu, Kubera and Īśāna
PaceTradition says to walk slowly, “like a queen in her ninth month”, in silence or chanting
MeaningTo circle the hill is to circle Śiva himself

ZAPPY: Why barefoot?

PROF. BOLT: You're walking around God. Shoes come off, as at a sanctum door.

LIBERATION BY REMEMBERING

Aruṇācala’s promise
The versedarśanād abhrasadasi, jananāt kamalālaye, kāśyāṃ tu maraṇān muktiḥ, smaraṇād aruṇācale
MeaningLiberation by seeing Cidambaram, by being born at Tiruvārūr, by dying at Kāśī, and by merely remembering Aruṇācala
Why it mattersOf the four, Aruṇācala asks the least: only remembrance
RamaṇaHe loved to quote it

ZAPPY: Just thinking of it is enough?

PROF. BOLT: That is the claim. The easiest of the four ways.

See also: Cidambaram (liberation by seeing)

THE TEMPLE

Four towers and five courtyards
SizeAbout 10 hectares; walls about 210 m on the east and west, 450–480 m on the north and south
Rāja GopuramThe eastern tower, about 66 m and eleven tiers, begun under Kṛṣṇadevarāya and completed by the Thanjavur Nāyaka Sevappa
Other towersVallāḷa Mahārāja Gopuram; Kiḷi (parrot) Gopuram; the northern tower raised by the woman ascetic Ammaṇi Ammāḷ
InsideFive courtyards, a thousand-pillared hall and the underground Pātāla Liṅga shrine
Oldest partThe main shrine of Aṇṇāmalaiyār; most of the rest is Chola to Nāyaka

THE HOYSALA KING

Vallāḷa and the son who was Śiva
WhoVīra Ballāḷa III, the last great Hoysala king of Karnataka, made Tiruvaṇṇāmalai his capital in the 14th century
The towerThe Vallāḷa Mahārāja Gopuram is named after him
The legendHe had no son to perform his funeral rites; Śiva promised to do it himself
Every yearIn the month of Māsi, Aruṇācaleśvara is taken in procession to perform the rites for Vallāḷa as his son
Karnataka linkA Hoysala king of Karnataka remembered every year in a Tamil temple

ZAPPY: Śiva does a king's śrāddha?

PROF. BOLT: Every year, the story says. A son's promise kept by God.

THE PARROT TOWER

Aruṇagirinātar
WhoAruṇagirinātar, a 15th-century poet of Tiruvaṇṇāmalai, devotee of Murugan
TiruppukaḻHis great collection of songs to Murugan, still sung everywhere
Turned backTradition says Murugan saved him when he tried to end his life by jumping from this tower
The parrotLegend says he took the form of a parrot; the Kiḷi Gopuram recalls it
ShrineA shrine of Murugan in the temple is tied to him

RAMAṆA MAHARṢI

The sage of Aruṇācala
ArrivalVenkataraman, a boy of 16 from near Madurai, came to Tiruvaṇṇāmalai in 1896 after a sudden experience of the Self
Pātāla LiṅgaHe first sat in the underground shrine inside the temple, lost to the world
The hillHe lived on Aruṇācala’s slopes, in Virūpākṣa cave and Skandāśramam, and never left it again
TeachingSelf-inquiry: ask “Who am I?” and rest in the answer
HymnsHe wrote five hymns to Aruṇācala, including the Akṣaramaṇamālai
Died1950; his āśrama at the foot of the hill draws seekers from all over the world

OTHER SAINTS AND POETS

A hill of holy people
TēvāramAppar and Campantar sang Aṇṇāmalai
MāṇikkavācakarTradition says he composed the Tiruvempāvai here, sung in Mārkaḻi
Guhai NamaśivāyaA 16th-century saint who lived in a cave on the hill
Śeṣādri SvāmīA saint of the town, contemporary of Ramaṇa
TodayMany āśramas and seekers line the Girivalam path

WHAT FIRE TEACHES

The wisdom of the flame
HumilityNeither Brahmā nor Viṣṇu could find the end
HonestyViṣṇu admitted failure and was praised; Brahmā lied and was cursed
UnionŚiva gave half of himself to Śakti here
Inner fireRamaṇa’s question, “Who am I?”, burns away the ego

ZAPPY: What should I take from fire?

PROF. BOLT: Admit what you can't measure. And let the fire burn what isn't you.
