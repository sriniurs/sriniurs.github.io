PAÑCA KEDĀR · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Śiva’s five Himalayan shrines

FOREWORD

After the great war, the five Pāṇḍava brothers went looking for Śiva to wash away their sin. He hid from them as a bull, and when Bhīma caught him, he dived into the Himalayan earth. His hump stayed at Kedārnāth; his arms, face, navel and hair rose in four other valleys.

These are the Pañca Kedār, the five Kedārs. This overview tells the legend and what binds them, from the winter journeys of their gods to the Karnataka priests who serve at Kedārnāth. Then five guides take each shrine.

ZAPPY: Five temples for one bull?

PROF. BOLT: Each holds a part of him. Together they make the whole Śiva.

The Pañca Kedār, “five Kedārs”, are five Śiva temples scattered across the high valleys of Garhwal, between the Mandākinī and Alaknandā rivers. Each holds a different part of Śiva’s body, according to a regional legend.

Kedārnāth is famous as a Jyotirliṅga and as one of the Char Dhām; the other four are smaller and remoter, reached on foot through forests and alpine meadows. Together they are one of the great Himalayan pilgrimages.

THE PĀṆḌAVAS AND THE BULL

The founding legend
After the warThe Pāṇḍavas, stained by killing their own kin at Kurukṣetra, sought Śiva’s forgiveness
Śiva hidesUnwilling to meet them, he hid, first at Guptakāśī (“hidden Kāśī”), then as a bull among cattle
BhīmaBhīma straddled the valley; all the cattle passed under him except one bull, which he knew was Śiva
Into the earthAs Bhīma grabbed it, the bull dived into the ground; Bhīma held the hump
Five placesThe hump stayed at Kedārnāth; the arms, face, navel and hair rose at four other places

ZAPPY: Is this in the Mahābhārata?

PROF. BOLT: No. It's a Garhwal tradition, told in the Kedāra Khaṇḍa and by local priests.

The legend is told with local variations. In most, the Pāṇḍavas, after the war, are told by sages that only Śiva can free them from the sin of killing their kin and teachers. Śiva, displeased, avoids them, and Guptakāśī, “hidden Kāśī”, takes its name from his hiding place.

In the climactic scene, Bhīma, the Wind’s son, stands astride a gorge so that the herd must pass beneath him. The bull Śiva refuses to pass, and Bhīma lunges for it. The bull sinks into the ground, leaving its hump in Bhīma’s hands. Śiva, moved by their determination, forgives them and remains in five places.

AND THE HEAD?

Nepal’s share
PaśupatināthA common telling: the head appeared at Paśupatināth in Kathmandu
DoleśvarAnother Nepali tradition places the head at Doleśvar Mahādev, near Bhaktapur
Kedār linkedBoth are treated by some as the head of the Kedār bull
DifferentThe Pañca Kedār themselves are the five shrines in Garhwal

The link with Nepal shows how the Himalayan pilgrimage world crosses modern borders. Paśupatināth, on the Bāgmatī in Kathmandu, is Nepal’s greatest Śiva temple. Some local traditions there tell that the bull’s head emerged at Paśupatināth.

In recent decades, Doleśvar Mahādev near Bhaktapur has been promoted as the head of the Kedār bull, and pilgrimage links between it and Kedārnāth have been publicised.

THE FIVE IN ORDER

The traditional sequence
1. KedārnāthThe hump; a Jyotirliṅga; about 3,583 m
2. TuṅganāthThe arms; about 3,680 m, called the highest Śiva temple in the world
3. RudranāthThe face; in a cave-temple among high meadows
4. MadhyamaheśvarThe navel; in a high valley under Chaukhamba
5. KalpeśvarThe matted hair; a rock cave in the Urgam valley, open all year

The traditional order of visiting is Kedārnāth, Tuṅganāth, Rudranāth, Madhyamaheśvar and Kalpeśvar. Pilgrims who complete all five are said to complete the whole Śiva.

The five are spread over two districts and different valleys; most are reached by mountain paths, and only Kalpeśvar is close to a road. We describe each in its own article, as a place of worship rather than a travel route.

WINTER SEATS

When the snow comes
ClosingAround Dīpāvalī and Bhai Dūj the temples close; the movable image (utsava mūrti) is carried down in a palanquin (ḍolī)
UkhimathOmkāreśvar temple at Ukhimath is the winter seat of Kedārnāth and Madhyamaheśvar
MakkumathTuṅganāth’s winter seat
GopeśvarRudranāth’s image goes to the Gopīnāth temple
KalpeśvarStays open: worship continues there through winter

ZAPPY: Who looks after Śiva in winter?

PROF. BOLT: The same priests. They walk down with him and worship him below till spring.

Snow closes four of the five shrines for about six months each year. The closing and opening dates are fixed by priests and almanac, and the movement of the ḍolī, the palanquin carrying the utsava mūrti, is a festival in itself, with villages along the route turning out to welcome the god.

At the winter seats worship continues every day. Pilgrims who cannot climb in summer can take darśan at Ukhimath, Makkumath and Gopeśvar instead.

THE PRIESTS

Karnataka in the Himalaya

MadhyamaheśvarAlso served by South Indian Jaṅgama priests
TuṅganāthAn exception: local brahmins of Makkumath
RudranāthLocal priests from Gopeśvar
TraditionThe South Indian link is traced to Ādi Śaṅkara

ZAPPY: Kannada in Kedārnāth?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. A Karnataka family line has served there for centuries.

The Rāval of Kedārnāth, the hereditary chief priest, is drawn from the Vīraśaiva (Liṅgāyat) Jaṅgama community of Karnataka, and Kannada prayers are part of the daily worship. Ukhimath, the winter seat, is a centre of this tradition.

This is one of the most remarkable living links between South India and the Himalaya. Tradition attributes the arrangement to Ādi Śaṅkara, who is said to have placed South Indian priests in several Himalayan shrines.

ĀDI ŚAṄKARA

Samādhi at Kedār
TraditionĀdi Śaṅkara is said to have revived worship in these mountains and reached Kedār at the end of his life
SamādhiHis samādhi shrine stands behind Kedārnāth temple
2013The flood destroyed it; a new samādhi with a 12-foot statue was unveiled in 2021
DatesŚaṅkara’s dates and the place of his death are debated: Kāñcī tradition differs
See: Kedārnāth in our Jyotirliṅga series

A strong tradition holds that Ādi Śaṅkara entered samādhi at Kedārnāth at the age of 32. Other traditions, notably the Kāñcī Maṭha, place his passing at Kāñcīpuram. Historians date him to the 8th century, though some traditions place him much earlier.

The 2013 Kedārnāth flood washed away the old samādhi shrine. In 2021 a new samādhi was inaugurated with a 12-foot statue of Śaṅkara carved from chlorite schist in Mysuru.

KEDĀRA KHAṆḌA

The land of Kedār
TextThe Kedāra Khaṇḍa, a section of the Skanda Purāṇa, praises the Garhwal Himalaya as Śiva’s land
KedāraThe region itself was called Kedāra-maṇḍala
PilgrimsPilgrims came on foot for centuries, often together with Badrīnāth
Pañca BadrīA matching set of five Viṣṇu shrines, the Pañca Badrī, lies nearby

The Kedāra Khaṇḍa is a long māhātmya, a glorification text, attached to the Skanda Purāṇa. It describes the Himalayan region from Haridvār to the high peaks as Śiva’s field, full of tīrthas, rivers and shrines.

The Pañca Badrī, five Viṣṇu shrines around Badrīnāth, and the Pañca Prayāga, five river confluences on the Alaknandā, form matching sets that pilgrims combine with the Pañca Kedār.

WHAT THE FIVE TEACH

A body spread over the mountains
Whole and partsTo see the whole Śiva you must visit every part
ForgivenessThe Pāṇḍavas’ search is a search for absolution, earned by effort
MountainsThe Himalaya itself as the god’s body
SeasonsSix months in the snow, six below: the god goes where the people are

The image of a god’s body spread across a mountain range is powerful. It turns the whole Garhwal Himalaya into a sacred body, and the pilgrimage into a slow assembling of the divine from its parts.

The legend also has a moral: forgiveness for a great wrong is not given cheaply; the Pāṇḍavas had to search, climb and hold on.
