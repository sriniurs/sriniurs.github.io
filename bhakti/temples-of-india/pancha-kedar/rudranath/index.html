RUDRANĀTH

PAÑCA KEDĀR · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The face of Śiva

FOREWORD

Rudranāth holds Śiva’s face. It is a cave-temple set in a rock face among high meadows, where the god is worshipped as Nīlakaṇṭha, the blue-throated one.

It is called the hardest of the five to reach. Nearby flows the Vaitaraṇī, the river of the dead, where pilgrims make offerings for their ancestors.

ZAPPY: The face in a cave?

PROF. BOLT: A rock face that looks like a face, decorated for worship.

Rudranāth’s setting is wild: a rock overhang among meadows and forest, with distant snow peaks. Many pilgrims consider the walk the hardest of the five, and the remoteness keeps crowds small.

Its form, a face in natural rock, makes it unlike any other Kedār: the worshipper stands face to face with Śiva.

THE FACE

Rudra’s face
LegendThe bull’s face emerged here
ImageA natural rock face, decorated so the features can be seen
NīlakaṇṭhaHe is worshipped as the god who drank the poison at the churning
Rudra“The fierce one”: the Vedic name of Śiva

The deity is a natural rock formation decorated to show eyes, nose and mouth. As Nīlakaṇṭha, he recalls the churning of the ocean, when Śiva drank the poison Hālāhala to save the world and it stained his throat blue.

Rudra, “the roarer” or “the fierce one”, is the oldest Vedic name for Śiva, celebrated in the Śrī Rudram, which we have explained on this site.

ZAPPY: Why for ancestors?

PROF. BOLT: The river of the dead flows here. Families come to help their departed across.

In Purāṇic belief, the Vaitaraṇī is a terrible river that the souls of the dead must cross on the way to Yama; good deeds and gifts made in life, or rites by descendants, help them across.

At Rudranāth the local stream bears this name, and the shrine has become a place for ancestral offerings, like Gayā in Bihar or Badrīnāth’s Brahma Kapāl.

THE MEADOWS

Bugyāls
BugyālHigh alpine grasslands, green in summer, snow in winter
FlowersRich in wild flowers after the monsoon
ShepherdsGrazing grounds for local herders
PathThe shrine is reached by a long mountain walk through them

Bugyāls are the high pastures of Garhwal, used by shepherds in summer. Around Rudranāth they stretch for miles, and in late summer are full of wild flowers.

Pilgrimage and pastoral life share the same land: the path to the god is also the path of the herds.

PRIESTS AND WINTER

Who keeps Rudranāth
PriestsLocal priests from Gopeśvar
WinterThe image goes to the Gopīnāth temple in Gopeśvar
GopīnāthAn old Śiva temple with a famous iron trident
FairA fair at the ḍolī’s return, attended mainly by locals

In winter the image is carried to the Gopīnāth temple in Gopeśvar, the district headquarters of Chamoli. Gopīnāth is an old temple known for an ancient metal trident with inscriptions.

The return journey of the ḍolī in spring is marked by a fair, attended mostly by local villagers.

WHAT RUDRANĀTH TEACHES

Facing God
Face to facePrayer as meeting someone
AncestorsRemember those who came before
The hard roadThe hardest walk to the most personal darśan

Rudranāth’s lesson is intimacy: after the hardest walk comes the most personal meeting, face to face with the god. Its link with ancestors adds a second: remember those who walked before you.
