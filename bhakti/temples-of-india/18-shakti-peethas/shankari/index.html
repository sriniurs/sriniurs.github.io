LAṄKĀ · TRINCOMALEE · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The Goddess on the cliff above the sea

FOREWORD

The hymn of the eighteen Śakti Pīṭhas begins far from India, on an island: laṅkāyāṃ śāṅkarī devī.

Pilgrims find her on Swami Rock at Trincomalee, beside the ancient Śiva temple of Koṇeśvaram: sung by the Tēvāram saints, torn down by the Portuguese, and rebuilt from images found in a well.

This guide tells her story, the temple’s, and the open question of which Laṅkā the hymn meant.

Jai Mā Śāṅkarī,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Is she the most famous of the eighteen?

PROF. BOLT: Among the least visited! But she comes first in the hymn, so she comes first here.

ZAPPY: The very first pīṭha is outside India?

PROF. BOLT: On an island cliff above the sea. The hymn begins where the land ends.

WHERE IT IS

A headland on the north-east coast

The rock. Trincomalee stands on a peninsula guarding one of the world’s great natural harbours. At its tip is Swami Rock, a cliff that drops about 120 m straight into the Indian Ocean. Koṇeśvaram and the Śāṅkarī shrine stand on top.

The fort. Today you reach the rock through the gate of Fort Frederick, the fort the Portuguese built from the temple’s own stones.

Kailāsa’s line. Trincomalee lies on almost the same longitude as Mount Kailāsa, which is why it is called the Kailāsa of the South.

ZAPPY: Is Śāṅkarī different from Mātumai Ammai?

PROF. BOLT: Two shrines, one Goddess. Śāṅkarī is her name as the pīṭha; Mātumai Ammai is her name as Koṇeśvara's consort.

THE PĪṬHA

The first of eighteen

The verse. The hymn opens: laṅkāyāṃ śāṅkarī devī, “in Laṅkā, Śāṅkarī Devī”. Of all eighteen, she is named first.

What fell here. The usual South Indian list says Satī’s groin. Some lists say her heart. The Bengali 51-list puts Laṅkā’s pīṭha elsewhere and says her anklets fell there (see “Which Laṅkā?”).

Why first? No text explains the order. One reading: the hymn starts at the far south of the sacred land and works its way north to Kashmir.

THE GODDESS TODAY

A small shrine on the great rock

🛕 Her shrine

Śāṅkarī Devī has her own small shrine inside the Koṇeśvaram complex on Swami Rock, built in the Dravidian style. Pilgrims’ accounts describe a standing, four-armed image.

🪨 The old site

Temple tradition says her original shrine stood at the cliff edge and was destroyed with the rest in 1622–24. A lone pillar is said to mark the spot today.

Several writers date the present Śāṅkarī shrine to the revival of the 1950s; we could not confirm the exact year.

ZAPPY: So the pīṭha is inside a Śiva temple?

PROF. BOLT: Like Śrīśailam and Kāśī. Where the Goddess sits, Śiva stays to guard her.

THE LEGEND · RĀVAṆA

The cleft in the rock
A devotee king. In local tradition Rāvaṇa, king of Laṅkā and a great devotee of Śiva, came here with his mother to worship Koṇeśvara.
The sword. When his mother grew too ill to make the journey, Rāvaṇa tried to prise the whole temple loose and carry it to her. Śiva made him drop his sword; it left a cleft in the rock, still called Rāvaṇaṉ Veṭṭu, “Rāvaṇa’s cut”.
The hot wells. His mother died; grieving, Rāvaṇa struck the ground at nearby Kanniya, and hot springs gushed out, where he performed her last rites. Hindus there still perform rites for the dead.
An echo. The story mirrors the Kailāsa legend, where Rāvaṇa tries to lift Śiva’s mountain and is pinned by Śiva’s toe.

THE LEGEND · THE BUILDER

Kulakkoṭṭaṉ and the stone chronicle
The builder. Tamil chronicles tell of Kulakkoṭṭaṉ, “builder of tank and temple”, a Chola prince who restored Koṇeśvaram and the great Kantalai reservoir that fed its lands. The Konesar Kalveṭṭu places his work in 432–440 CE; others date him much earlier.
The stone chronicle. The Konesar Kalveṭṭu is a 17th-century Tamil verse chronicle. It gives the shrine a founding date of about 1580 BCE, a figure of tradition, not of archaeology.
A prophecy. It also “foretells” that the Paraṅkis (the Portuguese) would destroy the temple. Since it was composed after 1622, historians read this as history told as prophecy.

WHICH LAṄKĀ?

The debate, both sides

📍 Trincomalee

The popular identification, especially among Sri Lankan Tamils and South Indian pilgrims: Laṅkā’s Śāṅkarī is the Goddess of Koṇeśvaram, the island’s most famous ancient Śiva temple.

📍 Nainativu

The Nākapūśaṇi Ammaṉ temple on Nainativu island, off Jaffna, is counted as Laṅkā’s pīṭha in the 51/64 traditions: there the Goddess is Indrākṣī and her anklets fell.

What historians say. Some reference works call the hymn’s Laṅkā “historically undetermined”, and say that Trincomalee’s claim is a fairly recent popular identification. Others point out that Koṇeśvaram has been a major Śākta-Śaiva centre for over a thousand years. The hymn itself names only “Laṅkā”.

HISTORY · I

From Purāṇa to pilgrim kings
c. 300 CE The Vāyu Purāṇa is cited as the earliest mention of the shrine.
6th–7th c. Campantar sings of Tirukoṇamalai in the Tēvāram; Cuntarar follows.
537–668 The thousand-pillared hall (Āyiram Kāl Maṇṭapam) is dated to this Pallava period.
1131–53 Gajabāhu II of Polonnaruwa is remembered as a devotee and donor.
1223 A Kalinga king, Codaganga Deva, makes gifts after worshipping on Tamil New Year’s Day.
1262 Jaṭāvarman Vīra Pāṇḍya sets up the Pāṇḍya bull and double-fish emblems here.
1380 The Dakṣiṇa Kailāsa Purāṇam is compiled under the Jaffna kings.
1468 Aruṇakirinātar praises the temple in his Tiruppukaḻ.

ZAPPY: Sinhala kings AND Tamil kings gave gifts here?

PROF. BOLT: And Pāṇḍyas, Pallavas, a Kalinga king. The rock was holy to everyone who sailed past.

HISTORY · II

The fall: 1622–1624
A temple seen from the sea. In 1613 a Jesuit, Manuel Barradas, described the temple as a massive granite structure on the headland.
14 April 1622. On Tamil New Year’s Day, while the images were out in procession, troops of the Portuguese captain-general Constantino de Sá de Noronha attacked. Priests and pilgrims were killed and the shrines torn down; much was thrown into the sea.
1624. The last structures fell. Their stones built Fort Frederick across the neck of the headland.
Saved. Some images escaped, carried inland. They came to rest in the Ati Koṇanāyakar temple at Tampalakāmam, built in the 17th century to house them.

HISTORY · III

Three centuries of waiting, then revival
1687 The Dutch re-erect a memorial pillar on the rock; a romantic “Lover’s Leap” tale later grows around it.
1738 Dutch governor van Imhoff records visiting the ruined site.
1870 The first photograph of the temple’s remains.
1950 Digging a well near the site, the Trincomalee Urban Council finds bronze images of the old temple.
1951 An annual three-day procession after Mahāśivarātri begins.
1956–57 Arthur C. Clarke and Mike Wilson dive off the rock and find carved temple stones on the seabed.
1963 On 3 March, the rebuilt temple is consecrated with the recovered images.
2003 The Tēr chariot festival returns after 379 years.
2018 The great Śiva statue on the rock is rebuilt.

ZAPPY: The gods were under the sea for three hundred years?

PROF. BOLT: Some of them. A science-fiction writer went diving and found pieces of a temple. Truth is stranger!

THE TEMPLE UNDER THE SEA

What the divers found

The dive. In 1956 the writer Arthur C. Clarke and photographer Mike Wilson dived beneath Swami Rock and found worked temple stones on the seabed, debris from 1622. Clarke told the story in The Reefs of Taprobane (1957).

On land. Six years earlier, workmen digging a well had found bronze images of the old temple. Between the well and the sea, Koṇeśvaram got some of its past back.

The temple’s festivals
TērA 22-day festival with the great chariot, ending at the Tamil New Year in April, the very day of the 1622 attack
TīrttamThe sacred bath at the Pāpanāśam well
NavarātriNine nights of the Goddess: Śāṅkarī’s own season

ZAPPY: They hold the chariot festival on the day the temple was destroyed?

PROF. BOLT: And with that festival back since 2003, the day that ended it now belongs to it again.

The New Year procession, broken in 1622, runs again.
