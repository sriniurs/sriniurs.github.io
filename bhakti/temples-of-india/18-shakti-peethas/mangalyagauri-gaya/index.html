MĀṄGALYAGAURĪ

GAYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The Mother who nourishes, in the city of the ancestors

FOREWORD

Gayā is where Hindus come to feed their dead. At the footprint of Viṣṇu by the sandy Phalgu, families offer rice-balls so their ancestors may rest.

Above the city, on a rocky hill, sits the Goddess who feeds the living: Maṅgalā Gaurī, the Māṅgalyagaurī of the Śakti Pīṭha hymn. Here, tradition says, Satī’s breast fell, and she is worshipped as two stones in a small dark shrine.

This guide tells her story, and the stories of Gayā around her: a giant who gave his body, a river cursed to hide, and a banyan that cannot die.

Jai Maṅgalā Gaurī,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why would a Goddess of good fortune live above a city of funerals?

PROF. BOLT: Because grief and blessing go together. You free your parents below, and ask the Mother to look after your children above.

ZAPPY: A Goddess of good fortune in a city of funeral rites?

PROF. BOLT: The living come to her for blessings; the dead are fed at Viṣṇupada below. Gayā holds both ends of life.

WHERE IT IS

Hills around a sandy river

Ringed by hills. Gayā sits among rocky hills on three sides, Maṅgalā Gaurī, Rāmaśilā, Pretaśilā and Brahmayoni among them, with the Phalgu on the fourth.

Bodh Gayā, where the Buddha was enlightened, is a short way south.

ZAPPY: Why is she linked with marriage?

PROF. BOLT: Māṅgalya is the auspicious state of a married woman. Women pray to her for long, happy marriages.

THE PĪṬHA

Where the breast fell
The breast. As Śiva carried Satī’s body home toward Kailāsa, her breast fell on the Bhasmakūṭa hill at Gayā.
Two stones. The Kālikā Purāṇa, as quoted locally, says it took the shape of two stones, worshipped in the sanctum to this day.
Nourishment. The breast feeds; so here the Goddess is worshipped as the giver of nourishment, kindness and plenty.
Lists differ. Some lists count Gayā as a lesser (upa-) Pīṭha; the Aṣṭādaśa hymn counts it among the eighteen great ones.

THE TEMPLE

A small shrine on a rocky hill
FacesEast, on the top of the hill
Reached byA flight of steps, or a road
SanctumCave-like and dark; the two stones are draped and worshipped by lamplight
AgeThe present building is often dated to the 15th century
ComplexShrines of Kālī, Gaṇeśa, Śiva and Hanumān
LampAn undying lamp is said to burn in the sanctum

GAYĀSURA

The demon-saint’s head
Too holy. The Vāyu Purāṇa’s Gayā Māhātmya tells of Gayāsura, a giant whose penance made him so pure that whoever touched him went straight to heaven, emptying hell.
The altar. The gods asked for his body as a place for a sacrifice. He lay down; when he still stirred, Viṣṇu pressed him still with his foot.
His boon. Gayāsura asked that the gods stay on his body for ever, and that rites done there for the dead free them. So Gayā became the place of piṇḍa-dāna.
Three Gayās. His head is at Gayā, his navel at Jājpur (Śakti Pīṭha 11) and his feet at Pīṭhāpuram (Śakti Pīṭha 10).

ZAPPY: Three Śakti Pīṭhas sit on one giant?

PROF. BOLT: Gayā, Jājpur, Pīṭhāpuram: his head, navel and feet are three Pīṭhas too. The two stories run side by side.

VIṢṆUPADA

The footprint in stone
WhatA footprint about 40 cm long, carved in basalt, ringed by a silver basin
BeliefWhere Viṣṇu set his foot to hold Gayāsura down
TempleThe present temple, by the Phalgu, was rebuilt by Ahilyābāī Holkar of Indore in 1787
PriestsThe hereditary Gayāwāl paṇḍās guide the rites
AkṣayavaṭaAn undying banyan nearby, where the last piṇḍas are offered

THE PHALGU AND SĪTĀ’S CURSE

The river that hides
Daśaratha’s piṇḍa. Rāma, Lakṣmaṇa and Sītā came to Gayā to offer piṇḍas for King Daśaratha. While the brothers were away fetching things for the rite, the king’s spirit appeared and asked Sītā for his offering at once.
Balls of sand. With nothing else at hand, Sītā offered piṇḍas of sand, with the river, a cow, a ketakī flower and a brāhmaṇa as her witnesses.
Lies and a curse. When Rāma returned, all but the banyan denied it. Sītā cursed the Phalgu to flow hidden under the sand; she blessed the banyan, the Akṣayavaṭa, to live for ever.
To this day. For much of the year the Phalgu is a wide bed of sand; pilgrims dig to reach its water.

PIṆḌA-DĀNA

Feeding the ancestors
WhatOffering piṇḍas, balls of rice or barley, to the departed, so they may find peace
Why GayāGayāsura’s boon: rites done here free the ancestors
VedīsRites are performed at many altars (vedīs) around the city: Viṣṇupada, the Phalgu, the Akṣayavaṭa, Pretaśilā, Rāmaśilā and more
Pitṛ PakṣaThe fortnight of the ancestors, in Bhādrapada–Āśvina, brings the largest crowds
Gayā ŚrāddhaMany Hindus aim to perform it once in life for their parents

ZAPPY: And Maṅgalā Gaurī fits in how?

PROF. BOLT: Pilgrims who come to free their dead also climb the hill to ask the Mother's blessing for the living.

MAṄGALĀ GAURĪ VRATA

Tuesdays in Śrāvaṇa
WhenEvery Tuesday (Maṅgalavāra) of the month of Śrāvaṇa
WhoChiefly newly married women, for the first years of marriage
WhyFor a long life for one’s husband and a happy home
NameMaṅgalā Gaurī: Gaurī of auspiciousness, honoured on Maṅgala’s day
At GayāTuesdays and Śrāvaṇa bring women in great numbers to the hill

ZAPPY: The same Gaurī they worship at home?

PROF. BOLT: The vrata is done all over India. At Gayā they worship her at her own Pīṭha.

GAYĀ AND BODH GAYĀ

Two traditions, one land
Bodh GayāA short way south: where the Buddha attained enlightenment under the Bodhi tree
SharedGayā is holy to Hindus and Buddhists alike
An old readingA 19th-century scholar read Gayāsura’s story as an allegory of Buddhism’s eclipse at Gayā; others reject it
Ancient GayāMaurya-era caves in the nearby Barābar and Nāgārjunī hills

HISTORY

A city of rites
Purāṇas The Vāyu Purāṇa’s Gayā Māhātmya; the Padma, Agni and Devī Bhāgavata name Maṅgalā Gaurī.
6th–5th c. BCE The Buddha’s enlightenment at Bodh Gayā.
3rd c. BCE Mauryan cave-shelters in the Barābar hills nearby.
15th c. The present Maṅgalā Gaurī shrine, by common dating.
1787 Ahilyābāī Holkar rebuilds Viṣṇupada.
Today Millions come in Pitṛ Pakṣa; Navarātri and Śrāvaṇa crowd the hill.
