VIŚĀLĀKṢĪ

VĀRĀṆASĪ · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The wide-eyed Mother of Kāśī

FOREWORD

In the narrow lanes of Kāśī, a few steps from the ghāṭ where the city’s funeral fires never go out, stands the shrine of Viśālākṣī, the wide-eyed Goddess, wife of Viśvanātha. Here, tradition says, the jewel of Satī’s earring fell, and gave Maṇikarṇikā its name.

Her temple, oddly for the North, has a small Tamil gateway: it was built by merchants from Chettinad, for whom she is one of three great “eyed” Goddesses, with Kāmākṣī and Mīnākṣī.

This guide tells her story, her neighbours in Kāśī and the people who keep her temple.

Jai Viśālākṣī Mā,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Most pilgrims go to Viśvanātha. Do they miss her?

PROF. BOLT: Many do, sadly. Tradition says a Kāśī pilgrimage without Viśālākṣī is incomplete.

WhereMīr Ghāṭ, Vārāṇasī (Kāśī), Uttar Pradesh, by the Gaṅgā, near Maṇikarṇikā
PīṭhaSatī’s earring (karṇa-kuṇḍala), or its jewel, fell here
BhairavaKāla Bhairava, the guardian of Kāśī

ZAPPY: A Tamil-style temple in Kāśī?

PROF. BOLT: Built by Tamil merchants. She's Viśvanātha's consort, so the South came north to honour her.

WHERE IT IS

By the burning ghāṭ

In the lanes. The temple stands in the narrow lanes behind Mīr Ghāṭ, close to Maṇikarṇikā, the great cremation ghāṭ, and to Viśvanātha.

Kāśī. One of the seven sacred cities and the city of liberation.

THE NAMES

Wide eyes, jewelled ear
Viśālākṣīviśāla “wide” + akṣī “eyed”
Viśālākṣī GaurīHer fuller name in Kāśī
Maṇikarṇī“Jewel-eared”, after the Pīṭha legend
MaṇikarṇikāThe ghāṭ named after the fallen ear-jewel
Kāśī · Vārāṇasī · Avimukta“City of light”; between the Varuṇā and Asī rivers; “never forsaken” by Śiva

ZAPPY: Why 'wide-eyed'?

PROF. BOLT: Her gaze takes in the whole world, they say, and nothing escapes it. It's also a classic mark of beauty.

THE PĪṬHA

The fallen earring
The earring. The Pīṭha-nirṇaya says Satī’s kuṇḍala, her earring, fell at Vārāṇasī; the Goddess is Viśālākṣī and the Bhairava Kāla Bhairava.
The jewel. Many tellings say it was the jewel (maṇi) from her ear (karṇa), and so the place became Maṇikarṇikā.
Two Goddesses. One tradition names two Pīṭha-goddesses in Kāśī: Viśālākṣī with Kāla Bhairava, and Annapūrṇā with Viśveśvara.
Lists vary. Some later versions leave Vārāṇasī out of the 51; a few say her face fell here. The Aṣṭādaśa hymn counts her seventeenth.

MAṆIKARṆIKĀ

Where Kāśī burns its dead
WhatThe most sacred cremation ghāṭ in India; fires burn day and night
BeliefTo die in Kāśī is liberation: Śiva whispers the tāraka mantra into the ear of the dying
The kuṇḍaAbove the steps is the Maṇikarṇikā Kuṇḍa, said to have been dug by Viṣṇu at the dawn of creation
Another legendAn earring of Śiva or Pārvatī fell into Viṣṇu’s tank; hence the name. The Pīṭha story is a third telling of the same name
NearbyViśālākṣī stands only a few lanes away

ZAPPY: A Goddess temple next to the burning ghāṭ?

PROF. BOLT: In Kāśī, death is the doorway. The wide-eyed Mother watches it.

THE GODDESS

Two images, one Mother
FormA gracious standing Goddess, richly dressed and crowned
Two imagesTradition says there are two: a newer one in front and an older, smaller one behind it
OfferingsSindūr, bangles, saris and flowers; women pray for marriage and children
RitualDevotees often bathe in the Gaṅgā first, then come for darśana
AnnapūrṇāSome say Viśālākṣī and Annapūrṇā, the giver of food, are the same Mother in two forms

KĀLA BHAIRAVA

The kotwāl of Kāśī
WhoŚiva’s fierce form; Viśālākṣī’s Bhairava
RoleThe kotwāl, police chief and guardian of Kāśī: no one may live or die there without his leave
Brahmā’s headBhairava cut off Brahmā’s fifth head; it stuck to his hand until he reached Kāśī, where it fell
PilgrimsMany visit Kāla Bhairava first, to ask permission to stay in the city
His vehicleA dog

VIŚVANĀTHA AND ANNAPŪRṆĀ

The household of Kāśī
ViśvanāthaŚiva as Lord of the Universe, the Jyotirliṅga of Kāśī: Viśālākṣī’s Lord
AnnapūrṇāThe Goddess of food, who feeds all who live in Kāśī; her temple is near Viśvanātha
ḌhuṇḍhirājaGaṇeśa, who must be greeted near Viśvanātha
Six key placesA pilgrimage to Kāśī is said to include the Gaṅgā, Viśvanātha, Viśālākṣī, Kāla Bhairava, Ḍhuṇḍhirāja and Daṇḍapāṇi
Full guide: Kāśī Viśvanātha

ZAPPY: Father, mother, guardian, son?

PROF. BOLT: Viśvanātha, Viśālākṣī and Annapūrṇā, Kāla Bhairava, Ḍhuṇḍhirāja. Kāśī is one divine family's home.

THE TEMPLE

The South in the lanes of Kāśī
Built1893, by the Nāṭṭukkōṭṭai Nagarattār (Chettiars), a merchant community from Chettinad, Tamil Nadu
StyleA North Indian haveli-like building with southern features: a small Dravidian gopuram at the entrance
GatewayCarved lions at the door and a relief of Lakṣmī above
InsidePainted pillars before the shrine, and many Śiva-liṅgas around it
RenewedRenovated again in 1971 with the help of South Indian devotees

ZAPPY: Why would Tamil merchants build here?

PROF. BOLT: The Nagarattār have served Kāśī for generations, funding rites at Viśvanātha too. For them, Kāśī is home.

KĀMĀKṢĪ, MĪNĀKṢĪ, VIŚĀLĀKṢĪ

The three “eyed” Goddesses
KāmākṣīKāñcī: “eyes of love”; also a Śakti Pīṭha (No. 2)
MīnākṣīMadurai: “fish-eyed”
ViśālākṣīKāśī: “wide-eyed”
The sayingIn Tamil homes the three are named together as the great Goddesses of Kāñcī, Madurai and Kāśī
Why it mattersIt explains the southern devotion to this northern shrine, and the Nagarattār temple
See also: Kāmākṣī of Kāñcī

THROUGH THE YEAR

Kajalī Tīj and Navarātri
Kajalī TījThe temple’s own festival: the third day of the dark fortnight of Bhādrapada, at the end of the rains; women sing kajalī songs
NavarātriBoth seasons; the autumn one with Durgā Pūjā
AnnakūṭaAfter Dīpāvalī, with Annapūrṇā
DailyBath in the Gaṅgā, darśana, and sindūr for married women

HISTORY

Old shrine, new temple
Purāṇas The Devī Bhāgavata and Kāśī Khaṇḍa praise Viśālākṣī of Kāśī.
Medieval The shrine shares Kāśī’s many destructions and rebuildings.
18th c. Kāśī’s temples revive under the Marāṭhās; Ahilyābāī Holkar rebuilds Viśvanātha (1780).
1893 The Nagarattār build the present Viśālākṣī temple.
1971 Further renovation with South Indian help.

Origins. No firm date is known for the first shrine; tradition places it in the Purāṇic past.
