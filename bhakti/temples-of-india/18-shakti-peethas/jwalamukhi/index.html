JVĀLĀMUKHĪ

VAIṢṆAVĪ OF JVĀLĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The Goddess who is a living flame

FOREWORD

In the low hills of Kangra, inside a temple with a golden dome, there is no statue. There is a rock wall, and out of its cracks rise small flames, blue and orange, burning without oil or wick. This is Jvālāmukhī, the flame-mouthed Goddess, where Satī’s tongue fell.

A cowherd found her; a king built her temple; an emperor, the story goes, tried to put her out and then offered her gold, which she refused. A Sikh mahārājā gilded her dome.

This guide tells those stories, counts the flames, and looks at what science says burns here.

Jai Mātā Jvālā Jī,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Have the flames ever gone out?

PROF. BOLT: Not in living memory, and legend says never. The priests keep watch, just in case.

ZAPPY: A goddess who is fire?

PROF. BOLT: No stone, no metal: just flame from the earth. Here the Mother speaks with a tongue of fire.

WHERE IT IS

The Kangra valley

Devī’s valley. Kangra is full of goddess shrines: Vajreśvarī at Kangra town, Cāmuṇḍā near Dharamshala, Cintpūrṇī to the south-west, and Jvālā Jī in the low hills between.

THE PĪṬHA

Where the tongue fell
The tongue. When Satī’s body was cut by Viṣṇu’s discus, her tongue fell in these hills.
Fire with it. Some tellings say the fire of her yogic power fell with it, or her burning clothes, and the flame has never gone out.
No image needed. So the Goddess is worshipped here as flame alone.

ZAPPY: Why a tongue, and why fire?

PROF. BOLT: The tongue speaks and tastes; Agni, fire, is called the gods' tongue. The two go together.

VAIṢṆAVĪ AT JVĀLĀ

Which Goddess does the hymn mean?

🔥 Jvālāmukhī

Most lists of the Aṣṭādaśa Pīṭhas read jvālāyām as “at Jvālā” and place No. 15 here, at the flames of Kangra, with Satī’s tongue.

🏔️ Vaiṣṇo Devī

Some readers take vaiṣṇavī devī as Vaiṣṇo Devī of the Trikūṭa hills in Jammu, whose cave also holds a sacred flame. That shrine is not usually counted among the 51 Pīṭhas.

The usual answer. This series follows the common reading: Jvālāmukhī is the Pīṭha, and “Vaiṣṇavī” is a name of the Goddess there, the power of Viṣṇu.

ZAPPY: Is it the same Goddess anyway?

PROF. BOLT: Pilgrims on the Nau Devī circuit visit both. To them, she's one Mother in many hills.

LEGEND · THE COWHERD AND THE KING

How the flames were found
The cow. A cowherd found his cow giving no milk. He followed her and saw a little girl come out of the forest and drink it.
The dream. He told the king, Bhūmi Cand Katoch of Kangra, a devotee of Durgā, who had dreamt of a sacred place in the hills.
The flames. The king’s men searched and found flames burning from the rock. Bhūmi Cand built a temple around them and appointed priests.
The Pāṇḍavas. Tradition adds that the Pāṇḍavas came later and renewed the temple.

LEGEND · AKBAR’S UMBRELLA

The fire that would not go out
The test. The Mughal emperor Akbar, the story goes, heard of the flames and set out to put them out: he had water channelled onto them, and tried to smother them with iron.
They burned on. The flames kept burning. Humbled, Akbar offered the Goddess a golden umbrella (chatra).
Refused. The gold turned into a dull, unknown metal: the Goddess, says the legend, did not accept it.
Still there. A metal umbrella is kept in the temple; it is brass or copper. Whether it is Akbar’s is unknown.

ZAPPY: Did Akbar really come?

PROF. BOLT: He campaigned in these hills, so it's possible. The umbrella story is legend; the metal umbrella is real.

LEGEND · DHYĀNU BHAGAT

A devotee’s head
WhoDhyānu Bhagat, a devotee of Jvālā Jī in Akbar’s time, leading pilgrims to the shrine
The challengeAkbar, the story goes, stopped the pilgrims and cut off the head of Dhyānu’s horse: “If your Goddess is real, let her restore it”
The answerDhyānu prayed at the flames, offering his own head; the Goddess restored the horse, and Dhyānu too
RememberedDhyānu is credited with spreading the Goddess’s fame; a 1978 film tells his story

ZAPPY: Same story as the umbrella?

PROF. BOLT: Two halves of one legend: first the miracle, then the emperor's gift.

GORAKH ḌIBBĪ

The pot that boils cold
WhereA small shrine just above the main temple
LegendGorakhnāth, the great yogi, came here. The Goddess put water on to heat for him while he went to beg for rice. He never came back, so the water waits for him, still bubbling
The poolThe water bubbles as if boiling but is cold to the touch: gas rising through it
FlameA flame can be lit just above its surface

ZAPPY: Boiling but cold?

PROF. BOLT: Gas bubbles, not heat. The legend says it's still waiting for Gorakhnāth's rice.

THE SCIENCE OF THE FLAME

Gas from the earth
What burnsNatural gas seeping up through cracks in the rock, mostly methane
Lit byKept lit by the priests; once lit, the jets burn steadily
BelowGeologists link the seeps to gas-bearing rocks in the Himalayan foothills
ExplorationThe area has been surveyed for gas; the shrine’s flames have never been tapped
BeliefFor devotees, the cause doesn’t matter: the flame that rises unbidden from the earth is the Goddess

ZAPPY: So it's just gas?

PROF. BOLT: The gas is how. Why here, and why for so many centuries, is the question pilgrims answer with faith.

THE TEMPLE

Gold dome, silver doors
StyleA modern building, often called Indo-Sikh, set against a hill
DomeGilded with gold given by Mahārājā Ranjit Singh
DoorsSilver-plated folding doors from his son Kharak Singh
BellA great bell given by a king of Nepal, by tradition
SanctumA square pit in the floor with the flames in the rock wall
AroundGorakh Ḍibbī, the Caturbhuj temple and smaller shrines

HISTORY

Katoch, Mughal and Sikh
Legend The Pāṇḍavas worship here; the Mahābhārata praises the region.
Medieval Rājā Bhūmi Cand Katoch of Kangra builds the first temple.
16th c. Akbar’s campaigns in the hills; the umbrella and Dhyānu legends.
1809 Ranjit Singh meets the Kangra ruler Sansār Cand here and seals an agreement at the temple.
c. 1813–15 Ranjit Singh gilds the dome in thanksgiving.
Later Kharak Singh’s silver doors; the present temple completed in the 19th century.

Dates differ: sources give 1809, 1813 or 1815 for Ranjit Singh’s visit and gift, and 1835 for the completion of the present building.
