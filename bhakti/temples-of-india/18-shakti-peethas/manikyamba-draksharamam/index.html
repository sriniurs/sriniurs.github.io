MĀṆIKYĀMBĀ

DRĀKṢĀRĀMAM · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The ruby Mother, in Dakṣa’s garden

FOREWORD

Every Śakti Pīṭha begins with one story: Dakṣa’s sacrifice, where Satī gave up her life. In the Godāvarī delta, the town of Drākṣārāmam, “Dakṣa’s garden”, says that sacrifice happened right here, and that Satī’s left cheek fell back on the same ground.

There she is Māṇikyāmbā, the ruby Mother. Her Lord, Bhīmeśvara, is a striped liṅga so tall that it is worshipped from an upper floor, one of five pieces of a demon’s shattered liṅga.

This guide tells those legends, the temple of an Eastern Chalukya king, and how Vyāsa found a second Kāśī here.

Jai Māṇikyāmbā,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Is this a Śiva temple or a Goddess temple?

PROF. BOLT: Both, equally. Bhīmeśvara draws the Śaivas, Māṇikyāmbā the Śāktas, and everyone visits both.

WHERE IT IS

In the Godāvarī delta

Two Pīṭhas close together. Drākṣārāmam and Pīṭhāpuram (No. 10) sit a short way apart in the same delta.

Delta country. Paddy, coconut and canals: the Godāvarī splits into many mouths here before reaching the sea.

ZAPPY: A grape garden?

PROF. BOLT: Drākṣā does mean grape, but the name is really Dakṣa-ārāma. The grapes crept in later.

THE LEGEND · DAKṢA’S SACRIFICE

It began here
The insult. Dakṣa, chief of the Prajāpatis and Satī’s father, held a great sacrifice and invited every god except his son-in-law Śiva: a nirīśvara yāga, a sacrifice without the Lord.
Satī goes. She went anyway, hoping for a welcome. Her father insulted her husband in front of everyone.
The fire. Satī gave up her body in the sacrificial fire. Śiva’s fury destroyed the sacrifice; then, grieving, he carried her body across the world.
Here. Drākṣārāmam holds that this was Dakṣa’s own garden, and that Satī’s left cheek later fell back on the same ground.

ZAPPY: Don't other places claim the sacrifice too?

PROF. BOLT: Kanakhal by Haridvār is the best-known claim. Drākṣārāmam's is the southern one. Both are honoured.

MĀṆIKYĀMBĀ

The ruby Mother
ShrineIn the back left corner of the temple enclosure, by most accounts
FacingShe looks to her left: read as a sign of vāmācāra, the “left-hand” Tantric worship once offered here
Local legendA devoted girl named Māṇikyāmbā, who longed for Śiva, won Bhīmeśvara as her husband
Equal footingOne of the few temples where Lord and Goddess are given equal importance

ZAPPY: Why would facing left matter?

PROF. BOLT: In Tantra, 'left' is the secret, powerful path. Her gaze keeps the memory of how she was once worshipped.

The legend. The demon Tārakāsura wore a liṅga at his throat that made him unbeatable. Kumāra shattered it; the five pieces fell in the Telugu land and began to rejoin, chanting Oṃ, so five gods fixed them in place.

BHĪMEŚVARA

A liṅga two storeys tall
HeightVery tall, around 2.6 m by one account (some say nearly 9 ft): it rises through two floors
WorshipThe base is seen on the ground floor; the top is bathed and worshipped from the upper floor
LookWhite, crystal-like stone with dark stripes on the upper part
SvayambhūHeld to be self-born; tradition says Sūrya installed it
TriliṅgaWith Śrīśailam and Kāleśvaram, one of the three liṅgas said to give the Telugu land its name
See also: Śrīśailam (Mallikārjuna)

VYĀSA’S SOUTHERN KĀŚĪ

The hungry sage
No alms. In Kāśī, the sage Vyāsa and his disciples went a day without food. Furious, Vyāsa raised his hand to curse the city.
Annapūrṇā. The Goddess Annapūrṇā fed them, and Śiva told the angry sage to leave Kāśī.
Go south. The Goddess sent him to Drākṣārāmam, “equal to Kāśī”. Vyāsa settled here, worshipped Bhīmeśvara and called it Dakṣiṇa Kāśī.

ZAPPY: Vyāsa got thrown out of Kāśī?

PROF. BOLT: For losing his temper. Even the greatest sage had to learn patience, and found a second Kāśī in the bargain.

See also: Kāśī Viśvanātha

SAPTA GODĀVARĪ

Seven rivers in one tank
WhatThe temple tank, east of the shrine
LegendThe seven sages, the Saptarṣis, each brought a stream of the Godāvarī here to bathe the Lord: seven Godāvarīs in one
LateThe river was late in coming, by one telling; Śiva had already appeared on his own
BathA dip here before darśana is held to equal bathing in the Godāvarī

ZAPPY: The Godāvarī came late for Śiva?

PROF. BOLT: And he didn't wait. That's why they say Bhīmeśvara is self-born.

THE TEMPLE

A fortress of stone
BuilderBhīma I of the Eastern Chalukyas of Veṅgī, around 892–922 CE
PlanTwo enclosing walls, like a fort; the inner shrine is built on two storeys
TwinThe Kumārārāma temple at Sāmarlakoṭa, by the same king, follows almost the same plan
InscriptionsHundreds, in Telugu, Sanskrit and Tamil: Chalukyas, Cholas, Velanāṭi Coḍas, Kākatīyas, Reḍḍis
LaterPatronage from the Cholas and the Vijayanagara kings

ŚRĪNĀTHA’S BHĪMAKHAṆḌAM

A poet’s praise
PoetŚrīnātha, the great Telugu poet of the Reḍḍi courts (c. 1400)
WorkBhīmakhaṇḍam (Bhīmeśvara Purāṇam): Drākṣārāmam’s glory in Telugu verse, based on the Skanda Purāṇa
TellsThe Pañcārāmas, Vyāsa’s Southern Kāśī, Māṇikyāmbā and Bhīmeśvara
Skanda PurāṇaThe Sanskrit source tradition credits to Vyāsa

ZAPPY: A whole book on one temple?

PROF. BOLT: Śrīnātha wrote it like a love letter. Telugu readers still quote it.
