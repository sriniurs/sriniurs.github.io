MAHĀKĀLĪ

UJJAIN · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The Great Goddess in the city of Great Time

FOREWORD

Ujjain belongs to Mahākāla, Śiva as Great Time. The hymn says it belongs just as much to Mahākālī.

North of the old city, on Bhairav Parvat, a saffron Kālī with a silver crown and a red tongue is said to have made a fool into Kālidāsa. Near Mahākāla, two lamp towers blaze for Harsiddhi. And a short walk away, Kālabhairava drinks what his devotees pour.

This guide sorts out which temple is which, and why Ujjain has more than one claim to the Goddess.

Jai Mahākālī,
srini.presents 🙏

WHERE IT IS

A city of Śiva and Śakti

The city. Ujjain, old Avanti, stands on the east bank of the Kṣiprā in the Mālwā plateau. It is a jyotirliṅga city, a mokṣapurī and a Kumbha city all at once.

The Goddess’s places. Gaḍhkālikā stands north of the old city on a rise called Bhairav Parvat, near the river; Harsiddhi stands close to Mahākāla, by the Rudra Sāgar lake.

Read more. Mahākāleśvara · the Saptapurī.

ZAPPY: Kālī of the fort? Is there a fort?

PROF. BOLT: Old Ujjain was walled, and its oldest part was the gaḍh. She was the fort's own Goddess.

THE PĪṬHA

The upper lip and the elbow

The verse. ujjayinyāṃ mahākālī, pīṭhikāyāṃ puruhūtikā: Ujjain opens the hymn’s third verse.

Two pieces, one city. The usual 18-list says Satī’s upper lip fell in Ujjain. The 51-list counts Ujjain twice: the Bhairava Parvata pīṭha (upper lip, Goddess Avantī) and the Ujjayinī pīṭha (elbow, Goddess Maṅgala Caṇḍikā).

Why it matters. That double listing is why Ujjain has two pīṭha temples, and why sources point to different ones for the hymn’s Mahākālī.

Our reading. The hymn names Mahākālī; Gaḍhkālikā is the temple of Kālī herself and is most often named as her seat, so this guide centres on it. Harsiddhi, a pīṭha in its own right, gets its own pages. One travel guide even says Gaḍhkālikā is not a pīṭha at all: in Ujjain, every claim has an opposite.

ZAPPY: Can a city have two Śakti Pīṭhas?

PROF. BOLT: Ujjain does: the 51-list counts it twice, once for the lip and once for the elbow.

GAḌHKĀLIKĀ

The Goddess with the tongue

🔥 The image

A stone image painted saffron, with a silver crown, wide eyes and a protruding tongue: Kālī smiling and fierce at once.

🦁 The lion

Her mount, a stone lion, sits at the entrance facing her. The temple faces west, toward the Kṣiprā.

Around her: a Gaura Bhairava shrine (one of Ujjain’s eight Bhairavas), Śiva shrines and a lamp pillar.

THE LEGEND · KĀLIDĀSA

The fool who became a poet
The fool. Folk tradition says the young Kālidāsa was so simple that he was found sawing off the branch he sat on. Tricked into marrying a learned princess, he was mocked by her.
The Goddess. In shame he turned to Kālī, and by her grace became the greatest poet of Sanskrit. Ujjain says this was Gaḍhkālikā; some tellings say she wrote on his tongue.
The poet. His works, the Meghadūta, Śakuntalā, Raghuvaṃśa, made Ujjain immortal. Students still come to her before exams.

A legend: Kālidāsa’s life is almost unknown, and his link to Ujjain and to a King Vikramāditya is traditional.

HISTORY

From ancient bricks to the Scindias
Ancient The temple board speaks of images and bricks from about the 1st century BCE found at the site.
Purāṇa The Avanti Khaṇḍa of the Skanda Purāṇa counts Gaḍhkālikā among Ujjain’s Mother Goddesses.
7th c. King Harṣavardhana is said to have renovated the temple (one source gives 606).
10th–13th c. Further work under the Paramāras of Mālwā.
19th–20th c. Rebuilt by the Scindia rulers of Gwalior state; renovated again in 1944.
Today The temple stands near the Bhartṛhari caves and Kālabhairava, north of the city.

ZAPPY: Was Harṣavardhana really here?

PROF. BOLT: That's the tradition the temple and many guides repeat. Like much in Ujjain, it's old memory more than inscription.

GoddessHarsiddhi, Maṅgala Caṇḍikā; an image smeared with turmeric and vermilion, with Mahālakṣmī and Mahāsarasvatī
PīṭhaThe Ujjayinī pīṭha of the 51-list: Satī’s elbow; Bhairava Kapilāmbara
LegendThe Skanda Purāṇa tells how Caṇḍī killed the demons who invaded Kailāsa; Śiva named her Harsiddhi, “she who vanquishes all”. Folk tales link her to King Vikramāditya
TempleRebuilt in the Maratha period; its two lamp towers (dīpastambhas) are lit with hundreds of lamps in Navarātri
WhereBetween Mahākāla and the Rudra Sāgar lake

KĀLABHAIRAVA

The guardian who drinks

The guardian. Every Śakti Pīṭha has its Bhairava. Gaḍhkālikā’s is Lambakarṇa (“long-eared”) in the 51-list; in practice, pilgrims pair her with Ujjain’s famous Kālabhairava temple, close by.

The offering. Kālabhairava is offered liquor, poured at his lips; devotees say the image drinks it, and some is given back as prasāda. It is one of the most talked-about rites in India.

Eight Bhairavas. Ujjain keeps a circle of eight Bhairava shrines; Gaḍhkālikā’s compound holds one of them.

MAHĀKĀLA AND MAHĀKĀLĪ

Time and the Goddess of time

Mahākāla, “Great Time”, is Śiva who devours time itself; his south-facing liṅga in Ujjain is a jyotirliṅga.

Mahākālī is his power: the Goddess as time, who even Time cannot outlast. In the Devī Māhātmya she is the first of the three great forms, who wakes Viṣṇu to slay Madhu and Kaiṭabha.

One city. The hymn puts her here because this is Mahākāla’s city; some lists simply place her in his temple.
