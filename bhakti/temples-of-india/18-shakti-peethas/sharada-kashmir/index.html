ŚĀRADĀ

SARASVATĪ OF KASHMIR · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The Goddess of learning, beyond the line

FOREWORD

The Śakti Pīṭha hymn ends in Kashmir: kāśmīre tu sarasvatī. The last seat of the Mother is the Goddess of learning, Śāradā, whose temple in the Neelum valley was once among the great universities of Asia. Kashmir’s script was named after it; tradition says Śaṅkara mounted its Throne of Omniscience.

Today it is a roofless ruin across the Line of Control, which Indian pilgrims have not reached since 1947. Yet children all over India still begin their lessons with a prayer to “Śāradā who lives in Kashmir”.

This guide tells its story, its scholars, its ruin and the hopes of reopening it.

Namaste Śārade Devi,

ZAPPY: Why does the list end with learning?

PROF. BOLT: Maybe that's the point: after all the forms of power, the last gift is knowledge.

PROF. BOLT: For now. But the Goddess of learning lives wherever someone studies. That's the consolation, and the hope.

WHERE IT IS

A valley across the line

The site. About 1,980 m up, where the Madhumatī stream meets the Neelum (Kiṣangaṅgā), below Mount Harmukh, held by Kashmiri Paṇḍits to be Śiva’s abode.

The border. It lies on the far side of the Line of Control, about 10 km from it.

THE NAMES

Śāradā, Sarasvatī, Sarvajña
ŚāradāThe Kashmiri name of Sarasvatī: “of autumn”, bright as the autumn moon
Śāradā Pīṭha“Seat of Śāradā”
Sarvajña Pīṭha“Seat of omniscience”: the throne that only the all-knowing may mount
Kāśmīrapuravāsinī“Dweller in Kashmir”, from a famous prayer
Neelum · KiṣangaṅgāTwo names of the same river

A prayer known all over India, said before study: namaste śārade devi kāśmīrapuravāsini | tvām ahaṃ prārthaye nityaṃ vidyādānaṃ ca dehi me: “Salutations, Śāradā Devī, who lives in Kashmir; I pray to you always: give me the gift of learning.”

ZAPPY: Children who've never heard of the ruin still say that prayer?

PROF. BOLT: Every day, across India. The temple is closed; the prayer never was.

THE PĪṬHA

Where the right hand fell
The right hand. Common lists place Satī’s right hand at Śāradā: the hand that writes and gives.
Sarasvatī. The Aṣṭādaśa hymn closes with her: kāśmīre tu sarasvatī. The eighteenth seat is the Goddess of learning.
Lists differ. Some older lists do not count Śāradā among the 51; others name a different part. The hymn and Kashmiri tradition hold her dear.

THE TEMPLE-UNIVERSITY

Where scholars came
HeightBetween the 6th and 12th centuries, among the foremost centres of learning in the subcontinent
FoundingPerhaps commissioned by the Kashmiri king Lalitāditya Muktāpīḍa (8th c.); no definite proof
ScholarsTradition links Kalhaṇa, Śaṅkara and others to it; Buddhist scholars too
HemacandraThe Jain grammarian asked for grammar texts kept here, to compile his own (per a 13th-c. Jain chronicle)
RāmānujaŚrī Vaiṣṇava tradition says he came to consult an old commentary on the Brahma Sūtras kept here

ZAPPY: Was it a temple or a college?

PROF. BOLT: Both, most likely. In old India, the Goddess of learning presided over the library.

THE ŚĀRADĀ SCRIPT

Kashmir’s own letters
NameCalled after the Pīṭha, where it was used so much
OriginIt did not begin in Kashmir, scholars say, but took its name and fame from Śāradā
UseKashmir’s chief script for centuries: Sanskrit and Kashmiri manuscripts, inscriptions
SurvivalKept alive by Kashmiri Paṇḍit priests for horoscopes and ritual texts
FamilyAn ancestor of later Himalayan scripts such as Ṭākarī

ŚAṄKARA AND THE THRONE

The southern door
Four doors. Tradition says the temple had four doors, for scholars of the four directions; the southern door had never been opened, for no southerner had proved himself all-knowing.
Śaṅkara arrives. Ādi Śaṅkara came from Kerala, answered every challenger, philosophers of every school, and the southern door was opened for him.
The throne. He ascended the Sarvajña Pīṭha, the Throne of Omniscience. The 14th-c. Mādhavīya Śaṅkaravijaya tells the story.
To the south. Southern tradition links Śṛṅgeri’s Goddess Śāradā with this Kashmiri seat; even the Teetwal temple of 2023 received its image from Śṛṅgeri.

IN THE OLD BOOKS

Kalhaṇa, Bilhaṇa, Al-Bīrūnī
Nīlamata PurāṇaAn early Kashmiri text listing sacred places, Śāradā among them
KalhaṇaThe Rājataraṅgiṇī (1148–49), Kashmir’s great chronicle, describes the shrine and a siege nearby
BilhaṇaThe 11th-c. Kashmiri poet credits his learning to Śāradā, “whose crown is gold from the Madhumatī”
Al-BīrūnīIn the 11th c. he wrote of a revered shrine with a wooden image of Śāradā, though from hearsay, never having entered Kashmir
Aurel SteinThe British archaeologist photographed the ruins in 1893 and identified the site from Kalhaṇa

ZAPPY: A Muslim scholar wrote about it a thousand years ago?

PROF. BOLT: Al-Bīrūnī listed it among India's most revered shrines. Its fame reached far beyond Kashmir.

THE RUINS

What stands today
PlanA square shrine on a high stone plinth, in the Kashmiri style, reached by a long flight of steps
DoorwayA tall doorway under a pointed, trefoil-style arch
RoofGone; the walls stand open to the sky
DamageCenturies of neglect, then the 2005 Kashmir earthquake
StonesBlocks from fallen buildings have likely been reused by villagers over the years

SINCE 1947

A pilgrimage cut off
Before 1947 Kashmiri Paṇḍits made the pilgrimage, with Teetwal as a base on the way.
1947 Partition and the war in Kashmir; the site falls on the Pakistani side of the line.
2005 The Kashmir earthquake damages the ruins further.
2007 onward Visits by Kashmiri scholars from the other side revive the call to reopen it.
2019 After the Kartarpur corridor opens for Sikh pilgrims, the Save Sharda Committee asks for a similar corridor.
22 Mar 2023 A Śāradā temple and a gurdwārā at Teetwal, Kupwara, on the Indian side, are opened; the Union Home Minister says the government will try for a corridor.

Both sides. Indian groups have complained of encroachments at the site; Pakistani writers have also argued for opening it to pilgrims. No corridor exists as of this writing.

THE TEETWAL TEMPLE

The Goddess at the line
WhereTeetwal, in the Karnah area of Kupwara district, where the Kiṣangaṅgā marks the Line of Control
Built byThe Save Sharda Committee Kashmir, with the Śṛṅgeri Śāradā Pīṭha; local Muslims supported it
Opened22 March 2023, with a gurdwārā opened soon after
PurposeA base camp for the old route, and a place to worship Śāradā within reach

ZAPPY: A Hindu temple, a gurdwārā and Muslim neighbours, at the border?

PROF. BOLT: Built side by side, on the old pilgrim road. It's a small picture of what Kashmir once was.
