ŚṚṄKHALĀ DEVĪ

PRADYUMNA · PANDUA · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The seat with no temple

FOREWORD

The hymn’s third name is the hardest to visit, because there is nothing to visit: pradyumne śṛṅkhalā devī.

Most writers place her at Pandua, a town in Bengal’s Hooghly district, where a tall brick minaret and a ruined mosque stand on an open field. Tradition says her temple stood there; historians argue about what really happened.

This guide sets out what is known, what is told, and where else people look for the Goddess of the chain.

Jai Mā Śṛṅkhalā,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: How do you write a guide about a temple that isn't there?

PROF. BOLT: Carefully. Tell the story, show the stones, and say plainly what nobody knows.

WHERE IT IS

Up the Hooghly from Kolkata

The town. Pandua (often “Choṭo Pāṇḍuyā”, to tell it from the bigger Pandua in Malda) lies on the Grand Trunk Road and the Howrah–Bardhaman railway, inland from the Hooghly river.

The landmark. Its tall minaret can be seen from trains and from the road.

Old ground. This was the Saptagram–Tribeni region, one of Bengal’s busiest river ports in the Middle Ages.

ZAPPY: A goddess named 'chain'? That sounds scary.

PROF. BOLT: Not a prison chain. A bond, like a mother's to her baby. The chain is love.

THE PĪṬHA

Where Satī’s stomach fell

The verse. pradyumne śṛṅkhalā devī: third in the hymn, after Laṅkā and Kāñcī, and the first in the north.

What fell here. Satī’s stomach, say the usual lists. The image fits her name: the womb-mother, bound to the child.

The puzzle. “Pradyumna” is not the name of any town today. Most writers place it at Pandua in Hooghly; nobody has found an old inscription that settles it.

THE SITE TODAY

A minaret, a mosque, a door

🗼 The minar

A tapering brick tower about 38 m (125 ft) high, with a spiral stair inside, among the tallest in Bengal. It lost some height in a 19th-century earthquake and was repaired by the ASI.

🕌 The Bari Masjid

The “big mosque”, also called the 22-door mosque: a long brick ruin, no longer in use. Both buildings are Monuments of National Importance.

The doorway. Visitors report an old stone doorway at the site, kept locked, which local people identify as the entrance of a Hindu temple. Its origin has not been published.

HISTORY · THE KING

Rāja Pāṇḍu and the Sultanate
The legend. Local tradition says a Hindu king, Pāṇḍu Rāja, ruled here, with a magic well that brought the dead back to life. A Sufi warrior, Shāh Sufīuddīn, came against him; when the well’s secret was revealed, the king fell.
The history. Historians find no evidence of a king named Pāṇḍu, but several minor Hindu rulers held the Saptagram–Pandua area. Sultanate armies reached Tribeni by the late 13th century and Pandua in the early 14th.
Shāh Sufīuddīn is remembered as the builder of the Bari Masjid; his dargāh stands nearby.

HISTORY · THE MINAR

Two dates, three readings
Date 1c. 1340: built by Shāh Sufīuddīn as a victory tower (older local histories and several travel writers)
Date 21477: built by Sultan Shamsuddīn Yūsuf Shāh of Bengal (cited in recent references)
Reading 1A victory tower, later used to give the call to prayer for the Bari Masjid
Reading 2Built with the mosque in place of the Śṛṅkhalā temple, which was destroyed: the local Hindu tradition, put in print by Partha Sharma (2018)
Reading 3The tower was once the top of a Hindu temple, a Viṣṇu temple, or an observatory, later converted: a view some local historians hold

What can be said. No inscription or excavation report we found names a Śṛṅkhalā temple here. Equally, nothing rules one out. The tradition that the Goddess stood at Pandua is old enough that most pīṭha lists name it.

ZAPPY: So was the temple here or not?

PROF. BOLT: Nobody can prove it either way. The tradition says yes, the stones are silent, and the books disagree on the date.

THE LEGEND · ṚṢYAŚṚṄGA

The deer-horned sage
The sage. Ṛṣyaśṛṅga, the boy with a deer’s horn on his brow, raised in the forest by his father and pure as a child, is a famous figure of the Rāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata.
A devotee. A story told about this pīṭha makes him a devotee of Śṛṅkhalā, who built her temple where Satī’s stomach fell.
To the south. At her command, it says, he went south to the hills that bear his name, Ṛṣyaśṛṅga-giri, the origin of the name Śṛṅgeri, and the Goddess went with him.

This is a popular story; it is not part of Śṛṅgeri’s own temple tradition, which honours Śāradā, and no Śṛṅkhalā shrine is known there.

THE LEGEND · RUKMIṆĪ

A mother bound by a vow
The test. After their wedding, a story goes, Kṛṣṇa and Rukmiṇī pulled the sage Durvāsas in his cart. Rukmiṇī grew thirsty and Kṛṣṇa gave her water without asking the sage.
The curse. Durvāsas ordered the couple to live apart for twelve years. Rukmiṇī stayed outside the city.
The child. There she bore Pradyumna, who was stolen as a baby by a demon. The mother bound by a vow and by love for her lost child is read as Śṛṅkhalā, and the son’s name as the hymn’s “Pradyumna”.

The curse story is best known from Dvārakā in Gujarat, where Rukmiṇī’s temple stands outside the city. It may explain why one list places this pīṭha in Gujarat. The link to Śṛṅkhalā is a modern devotional reading.

WHERE IS SHE NOW?

Five answers
PanduaThe usual identification, on the strength of local tradition and the stone remains
GaṅgāsāgarSome place the Goddess at the mouth of the Hooghly, at Sāgar island
ŚṛṅgeriThe Ṛṣyaśṛṅga story says she went south with the sage
A Hooghly templeOne writer suggests a Goddess temple elsewhere in the district, such as Haṃseśvarī at Bansberia
GujaratAt least one published list gives “Pradyumna (Gujarat)”

ZAPPY: Five places for one Goddess?

PROF. BOLT: When a temple vanishes, the Goddess gets claimed everywhere. That's a kind of love too.

THE FAIR

A field that still gathers people

Melā Talā. Every year in the Bengali month of Māgh (around February), a great fair is held on the field around the minaret. It runs for about a month.

Everyone comes. Hindus and Muslims from the district come together for it: one of the few things on this ground that everyone shares.

ZAPPY: A month-long fair on the old temple ground?

PROF. BOLT: Whatever stood here, people never stopped gathering. The fair is the site's living memory.

AROUND PANDUA

The old river country
Tribeni“Three braids”, where the Hooghly splits: a sacred bathing place, with one of Bengal’s oldest mosques
SaptagramThe great medieval river port of the region, later silted up
MahanadA village with an old Kālī temple, linked to the Pāṇḍu Rāja legend
BansberiaThe Haṃseśvarī temple, a striking Tantric temple of the early 19th century
GaṅgāsāgarThe river’s mouth, with the Kapila Muni temple and a famous winter bathing festival

ZAPPY: Lots of history for a quiet bit of Bengal!

PROF. BOLT: River ports made kings, and kings made temples and mosques. The Hooghly carried all of it.
