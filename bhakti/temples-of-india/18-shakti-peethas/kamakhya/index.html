KĀMĀKHYĀ

KĀMARŪPA · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The Goddess who is not carved, but flows

FOREWORD

On a green hill above the Brahmaputra, pilgrims climb down steep stone steps into a dark cave. There is no statue at the bottom. There is a cleft in the rock, always wet from a spring, covered in red cloth and hibiscus. This is Kāmākhyā, where Satī’s yoni fell, the source of creation.

Here the god of love got his body back, a demon-king lost a bet to a rooster, and once a year the temple shuts for three days while the Goddess rests, as the monsoon soaks the earth.

This is the greatest seat of Tantra in India. This guide tells its legends, its rites, its ten Mahāvidyās and the kings who built and rebuilt it.

Jai Mā Kāmākhyā,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Is Kāmākhyā scary? People talk about Tantra and black magic.

PROF. BOLT: It's powerful, not sinister. Families come for blessings and children; the Tantra is about the power of creation.

ZAPPY: A temple with no image at all?

PROF. BOLT: There's nothing carved because nothing needs to be. The rock and the spring are the Goddess.

WHERE IT IS

A hill over the Brahmaputra

The blue hill. Nīlācala, “the blue mountain”, also Kāmagiri, rises on the south bank of the Brahmaputra at Guwahati.

The river. Bhairava Umānanda sits on a small island in mid-stream, facing the hill.

THE NAMES

Desire, form and place
Kāmākhyā“She who is called Desire” or “the one who grants desire”
Kāmarūpa“Form of desire”: old name of Assam, where Kāma regained his form
Nīlācala · KāmagiriThe blue hill · the hill of desire
Kubjikā PīṭhaAnother Tantric name for the place
Prāgjyotiṣa“Light of the East”: the ancient kingdom, capital near Guwahati
Kāmarūpī · HarikṣetraThe hymn’s names: the Goddess who takes any form, in “Hari’s field”

ZAPPY: Kāmarūpa is a place and a story?

PROF. BOLT: A god burned to ash got his body back here. The land kept the name 'form of desire'.

THE PĪṬHA

Where the yoni fell
The fall. As Śiva carried Satī’s body, Viṣṇu’s discus cut it piece by piece. Her yoni, the source of creation, fell on Nīlācala.
The hill turned blue. The Kālikā Purāṇa says the hill turned blue (nīla) as it received her: hence Nīlācala.
Among the oldest. Old texts count Kāmākhyā among the four Ādi (first) Pīṭhas, with Vimalā (feet), Tārā-Tāriṇī (breasts) and Dakṣiṇa Kālikā (face).

ZAPPY: Why is the yoni Pīṭha considered the greatest?

PROF. BOLT: Because it stands for creation itself. Every other Pīṭha is a part; this is the source.

LEGEND · KĀMADEVA REBORN

The god of love gets his body back
The arrow. To wake Śiva from meditation so he would marry Pārvatī, the gods sent Kāma, god of love, to shoot him with a flower arrow.
Ashes. Śiva opened his third eye and burned Kāma to ashes. His wife Rati wept.
Form again. The Kālikā Purāṇa says Kāma regained his form (rūpa) by the grace of the Goddess here; so the land is Kāmarūpa, and she is Kāmākhyā.
A temple for her. In thanks, Kāma is said to have had Viśvakarmā build her a shrine.

LEGEND · NARAKA AND THE STEPS

A demon in love
The suitor. Naraka, the demon-king of Prāgjyotiṣa, wanted to marry Kāmākhyā.
The test. She set a condition: build a stone stairway up Nīlācala in one night, before the cock crowed.
The trick. He nearly finished. Just before dawn the Goddess made a rooster crow early. Naraka, beaten, killed the bird in rage.
The path. The steep stone way up the hill is called the Mekhelā-ujowā path and linked to Naraka’s unfinished stairs. Kṛṣṇa later killed Naraka.

ZAPPY: A rooster again? Like Pīṭhāpuram!

PROF. BOLT: Gods in India love an early rooster. It's the oldest trick for ending a deadline.

THE SANCTUM

The cave of the spring
Below groundThe garbhagṛha is a small, dark cave, reached down narrow, steep stone steps
The stoneA sheet of stone slopes down from both sides into a yoni-shaped hollow, about ten inches deep
The springThe hollow is always full: a perennial underground spring feeds it
CoveredThe stone is kept under red cloth and flowers; pilgrims touch the water
FacingThe temple faces east, but the yoni is worshipped facing north, by one account

THE TEXTS

Kālikā Purāṇa and Yoginī Tantra
Kālikā PurāṇaAn Upapurāṇa of around the 10th–11th century, likely from Kāmarūpa: the fullest account of the Goddess, her rites, the hill and the land
IconographyThere she has six heads of different colours, each with a third eye, and twelve arms
NarakaThe Purāṇa makes Naraka, ancestor of Kāmarūpa’s kings, a devotee of the Goddess, tying king, land and Goddess together
Yoginī TantraA later text (16th c.) that links Kāmākhyā closely with Kālī and dwells on the yoni as the source of creation
KulācāraThe Tantric “path of the clan” centred here

ZAPPY: A whole Purāṇa about one hill?

PROF. BOLT: Kāmarūpa wrote its own scripture. That's how important this hill was to Assam's kings.

Why here? The ten Mahāvidyās are ten faces of the one Goddess, from terrifying to gracious. Nowhere else are all ten worshipped together on one sacred hill.

AMBUBĀCĪ MELĀ

When the Goddess rests
WhenMid-Āṣāḍha (June), at the height of the monsoon, as the Sun enters Mṛgaśirā’s last quarter
BeliefThe Goddess, like the earth soaked by rain, has her yearly menstruation
ClosedThe temple doors stay shut for three days; farming, digging and some rites pause across the region
ReopeningOn the fourth day she is bathed and the doors reopen to huge crowds
PrasādaPieces of red cloth (raktavastra), said to be soaked in her flow, are prized
CrowdsSādhus, tāntrikas and householders gather in their hundreds of thousands

ZAPPY: A festival for menstruation?

PROF. BOLT: For the earth's fertility. In Assam, the Goddess and the soil rest together, then wake.

WORSHIP AND BALI

An old Tantric rite
DailyWorship of the yoni-stone with flowers, red cloth, lamps; Kumārī pūjā of young girls as the Goddess
BaliAnimal sacrifice still takes place: chiefly goats, buffaloes, pigeons and ducks, all male; females are never offered
SubstitutesMany devotees offer pumpkins, sugarcane or gourds instead
DebateSome call it a living tradition; others oppose it on grounds of cruelty. Courts and governments have been asked to rule more than once
PriestsHereditary priestly families of the Nīlācala, the Bordeuri and others

ZAPPY: Do people still sacrifice animals here?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, as Tantric rites have for centuries. It's argued over openly, and many offer fruit instead.

THE TEMPLE

The Nīlācala style
ShapeA stone cruciform base topped by a polygonal, beehive-like brick dome, ringed by small domes: the Nīlācala type
Four chambersGarbhagṛha (the cave), calanta, pañcaratna and the naṭamandira (hall for music and dance)
Old baseThe lower stone parts belong to the early Kāmarūpa-era temple; the dome is Koch work
SculptureOlder stone carvings are set into the outer walls
NaṭamandiraBuilt under the Ahom king Rājeśvar Siṃha; inscriptions of 1759 and 1782 inside

HISTORY · TO 1565

From Mlecchas to Koch
5th–7th c. Archaeology hints at a structure this old under the temple.
9th c. First inscription to name Kāmākhyā: the Tezpur plates of Vanamālavarman of the Mleccha dynasty; a large 8th–9th-c. temple, Nāgara in style.
10th–11th c. The Kālikā Purāṇa is written; the temple complex grows.
Early 16th c. Tradition says the temple was destroyed, often blamed on Kālāpāhāṛ; historians doubt he reached here.
Early 1500s Viśva Siṃha, founder of the Koch dynasty, finds the ruins and revives worship.
1565 His son King Naranārāyaṇa completes the rebuilding, supervised by his brother Chilārāy.

The dome. After two failed tries at rebuilding the stone tower, a Koch artisan, Meghamukdam, is said to have built it in brick: the beginning of the Nīlācala style.

HISTORY · AFTER 1565

The Ahom era
Koch legend A curse is said to forbid Koch royals from seeing the temple; it is still remembered.
1658 The Ahoms under Jayadhvaj Siṃha take Kāmarūpa; their interest in the temple grows.
c. 1700 Rudra Siṃha invites the Śākta teacher Kṛṣṇarām Bhaṭṭācārya from Nadia in Bengal.
1714–44 Śiva Siṃha places the temple and hill under Kṛṣṇarām’s line, the Parvatiyā Gosāins.
1759, 1782 Rājeśvar Siṃha and Gaurīnāth Siṃha build and record; the naṭamandira.
1826 The British annex Assam; the temple carries on under its priests.
2015 The Supreme Court hands administration to the Bordeuri Samāj.
