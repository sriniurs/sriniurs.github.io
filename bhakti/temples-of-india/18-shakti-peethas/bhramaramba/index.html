BHRAMARĀMBIKĀ

ŚRĪŚAILAM · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The Mother of the bees

FOREWORD

High on the auspicious mountain, above the gorge of the Kṛṣṇā, Śiva as Mallikārjuna shares his temple with the Goddess: Bhramarāmbikā, the Mother of bees.

She defeated a demon whom nothing with two or four legs could kill, by becoming a swarm of six-legged bees. Pilgrims still press an ear to her temple wall to hear them hum.

This guide tells her story, the princess who gave Śiva his jasmine name, the king who knelt for a sword, and the great Kumbhotsavam.

WHERE IT IS

The auspicious mountain

The hill. Śrīśaila, “the auspicious mountain”, rises in the Nallamala forest above a deep gorge of the Kṛṣṇā. The river below is called Pātālagaṅgā, “the Gaṅgā of the underworld”, reached by a long flight of steps.

One complex. Bhramarāmbā’s shrine and Mallikārjuna’s stand inside the same high walls.

Two pīṭhas in a row. The hymn pairs her with Jogulāmbā of Alampur, upstream, the “western gate” of Śrīśailam.

ZAPPY: Bhramara is a bee?

PROF. BOLT: The big black bee that hums around flowers. Her name is literally a buzz.

THE PĪṬHA

Where Satī’s neck fell

The verse. śrīśaile bhramarāmbikā: sixth in the hymn, right after Alampur.

What fell here. Satī’s neck. In the 52-pīṭha lists too, Śrīśaila is counted.

Śiva and Śakti together. Śrīśailam is often called the one temple where a jyotirliṅga and a Śakti Pīṭha share a single complex. Kāśī and Ujjain have both too, but in separate temples.

THE GODDESS

Eight arms and a Śrī Yantra

⚔️ Eight arms

She is shown as a warrior Goddess with eight arms, each with a weapon, dressed in a silk sari and gold.

🔯 The Śrī Yantra

A Śrī Yantra is installed in front of her sanctum: the sign of the Goddess as Lalitā, as at Kāñcī.

Many faces. In Navarātri she is dressed in turn as different Goddesses, among them Kālarātri, Rājarājeśvarī and Mahāgaurī.

THE LEGEND · ARUṆĀSURA

Death on six legs
The boon. The demon Aruṇa did fierce penance to Brahmā and won a boon: no creature with two or four legs could kill him. He drove the gods from heaven.
The loophole. The gods prayed to the Goddess. She appeared surrounded by bees, which have six legs, and sent out swarm upon swarm.
The end. The bees covered Aruṇāsura and his army and destroyed them. The Goddess, now Bhrāmarī, settled on Śrīśaila as Bhramarāmbā beside Mallikārjuna.

ZAPPY: So the boon had a gap the size of a bee?

PROF. BOLT: Every demon's boon has a gap. The Goddess always finds it.

IN THE TEXTS

A form foretold
Devī MāhātmyaIn its eleventh chapter the Goddess foretells her own future forms. One of them: she will take the form of countless bees to slay the demon Aruṇa, and the world will call her Bhrāmarī
Devī BhāgavataTells the Aruṇāsura story in full, with Brahmā’s boon and the swarm
Lakṣmī TantraLakṣmī herself declares that she is Bhrāmarī
YogaBhrāmarī prāṇāyāma, the humming breath, is named after the bee

THE HUMMING WALL

Listen at the back of the shrine

The tradition. Pilgrims put an ear to a small opening in the wall behind Bhramarāmbā’s shrine and listen. Many say they hear a low humming, like bees.

Two explanations. Devotees hear the Goddess’s bees, still with her. Others say real bees have nested in the old granite walls. Either way, they have a good name: by every account, they have never stung a pilgrim.

ZAPPY: Did you hear it?

PROF. BOLT: Put your own ear to the wall one day and decide. That's the only fair test.

THE LEGEND · CANDRAVATĪ

The jasmine princess
A princess in the forest. Candravatī, daughter of a king across the Kṛṣṇā, fled her home and settled on this hill with a few servants.
The cow. She saw a cow pour its milk over a rock shaped like a liṅga. In a dream she learned it was Śiva, the Lord of Śrīparvata, and began to worship him.
The jasmine. Every day she offered him garlands of mallikā, jasmine. When Śiva appeared, she asked him to wear her jasmine always. So he is Mallikārjuna.

The better-known story of how Śiva and Pārvatī came here to be near their son Kārttikeya is in our Mallikārjuna guide.

ŚIVĀJĪ AND THE SWORD

A king at her feet, 1677
The visit. In 1677, during his southern campaign, the Maratha king Śivājī came to Śrīśailam.
The sword. Tradition says the Goddess blessed his sword, and that it brought him victory after victory. A sculpture in the temple shows him kneeling before her to receive it.
His gift. Śivājī is remembered as a builder here too: the northern gopura is credited to him.

ZAPPY: The bee goddess gave Śivājī his sword?

PROF. BOLT: That's the story Śrīśailam tells. The Marathas had their own Bhavānī of Tuljāpur too; it seems one goddess wasn't enough.

HISTORY

Kings of the auspicious mountain
2nd c. Sātavāhana inscriptions are the earliest evidence of the shrine.
Epics The hill is praised as Śrīparvata, a great tīrtha.
12th–14th c. The Kākatīyas and then the Reḍḍi kings, devotees of “Bhramarāmbā Mallikārjuna”, build halls and the steps to Pātālagaṅgā.
14th–15th c. Vijayanagara kings, beginning with Harihara I, make most of the later additions.
1677 Śivājī visits; the north gopura is credited to him.
Today The complex is run by the Śrīśaila Devasthānam; the Kṛṣṇā below is held by the Śrīśailam dam.

The Mallikārjuna guide covers the complex’s history in more detail.

THE KUMBHOTSAVAM

Her own great day
When. The first Tuesday or Friday after the full moon of Caitra, at the start of the year.
Behind closed doors. Priests worship the Goddess alone with the Navāvaraṇa, Triśatī and Khaḍgamālā rites; ordinary darśan pauses. Mallikārjuna is covered in curd rice and his doors stay shut until the next morning.
The offering. She is smeared with turmeric and kuṅkuma and given a sāttvika bali, a “pure sacrifice”: heaps of cooked rice, broken pumpkins, coconuts, and more than fifty thousand lemons.
Kumbhārati. In the evening a man dressed in a sari offers the lamp, and the doors open. The forest-dwelling Cheñcu people dance for her.

THROUGH THE YEAR

Her other festivals
DasaraNine days with Caṇḍī-yāga and Rudra-yāga, and the Goddess dressed as the nine Durgās
MahāśivarātriThe great festival of Mallikārjuna, when she is honoured with him
UgādiThe Telugu New Year, when pilgrims walk in from Karnataka and Maharashtra
KārtikaThe month of lamps
FridaysHer weekly day; married women pray to her for the family

ZAPPY: Does the whole hill celebrate together?

PROF. BOLT: Śiva's nights and the Goddess's nights. Śrīśailam is busy all year.

AKKA MAHĀDEVĪ

A poet who came to the mountain

The saint. Akka Mahādevī, the 12th-century Kannada poet-saint of the Vīraśaiva movement, gave up everything for Śiva, whom she called “Cennamallikārjuna”, the beautiful Lord white as jasmine.

Her end. Tradition says she came to Śrīśaila and lived her last days in a cave above the Kṛṣṇā, now called the Akka Mahādevī caves.

Why here. Her vacanas are poems of love to Mallikārjuna; this is his mountain, and the Goddess beside him is the Mother she sought.
