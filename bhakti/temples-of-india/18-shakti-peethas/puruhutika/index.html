PURUHŪTIKĀ

PĪṬHĀPURAM · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The Goddess of Indra, and the rooster at midnight

FOREWORD

In a corner of a Śiva temple in the Godāvarī delta sits Puruhūtikā, the Goddess whom Indra, king of the gods, worshipped when he had lost everything.

Her Lord is a rooster. Long ago, the story says, Śiva crowed at midnight to trick a giant, and the giant’s feet still rest here, beside a tank where pilgrims make offerings to their ancestors.

This guide tells both stories, maps the three Gayās, and visits the old town of Piṣṭapura.

Jai Puruhūtikā,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why do people bring their ancestors here?

PROF. BOLT: Because a giant asked for it. His feet are here, and his last wish was that the dead be freed at his feet.

ZAPPY: A Śiva who's a rooster?

PROF. BOLT: A rooster who crowed at midnight and ended a sacrifice early. You'll see why.

WHERE IT IS

In the Godāvarī delta

The town. Pīṭhāpuram lies a short way inland from the sea, north of Kakinada, in the rich rice country of the Godāvarī delta.

Two pīṭhas close together. Drākṣārāmam, home of the next-but-one pīṭha, Māṇikyāmbā, is a short distance away across the delta.

The temple. The Kukkuṭeśvara complex stands on the town’s edge, with the Goddess’s shrine in its north-east corner.

ZAPPY: The town's name means 'seat'?

PROF. BOLT: Pīṭha-puram, the city of the seat. Some say the Goddess's seat gave the town its name.

THE PĪṬHA

Where Satī’s hand fell

The verse. ujjayinyāṃ mahākālī, pīṭhikāyāṃ puruhūtikā: Ujjain, then straight to the eastern coast.

What fell here. Satī’s left hand, by most local accounts; other lists say her back or the rear of her body.

Three-fold holy. Pīṭhāpuram is at once a Śiva kṣetra (Kukkuṭeśvara), a Śakti Pīṭha (Puruhūtikā) and a Gayā for the dead, and, for many, a Datta kṣetra too.

THE GODDESS

In the north-east corner

🛕 Her shrine

Puruhūtikā’s shrine stands in the north-east corner of the Kukkuṭeśvara complex. The image has four arms. Small carvings of all eighteen great pīṭhas run round its walls.

🔱 Her worship

In her worship, Indra is named as the seer and Kukkuṭeśvara as the guru. A local tale says the original image lies buried beneath the shrine.

ZAPPY: All eighteen carved on one shrine?

PROF. BOLT: A whole pilgrimage on four walls. Walk round her and you've greeted every one.

THE LEGEND · INDRA

How she got her name
A king disgraced. Indra, king of the gods, wronged Ahalyā, wife of the sage Gautama. The sage’s curse left him maimed and shamed.
The penance. Leaving his throne, Indra came to Pīṭhikāpuri and did long penance to the Divine Mother.
The name. She appeared, healed him and restored his fortune. Indra, “Puruhūta”, worshipped her ever after, and she became Puruhūtikā.

ZAPPY: Even the king of the gods needed her help?

PROF. BOLT: Especially him. Pride falls hardest, and the Mother picks it up.

THE LEGEND · GAYĀSURA

The giant and the rooster
The giant. Gayāsura, a pious asura, grew so mighty by penance that he ruled the three worlds and displaced Indra.
The request. Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva came to him as Brahmins and asked for a holy place for a sacrifice. He offered his own body: it stretched from Gayā in Bihar to Pīṭhāpuram. The rite would last seven days, from cock-crow to cock-crow.
The rooster. On the sixth night, Śiva took the form of a rooster and crowed at midnight. Thinking the rite complete, Gayāsura stirred, and the sacrifice was spoiled.
His boon. Instead of asking for himself, he asked that Viṣṇu stay at his head, Brahmā at his navel and Śiva, as the rooster, at his feet, and that rites for the dead there should free the ancestors. So Śiva is Kukkuṭeśvara.

KUKKUṬEŚVARA

FacesThe temple faces north
The tankPāda Gayā Sarovar just inside the gate, with the footprints of Gayāsura and of Viṣṇu; a bath here is held equal to bathing in the Gaṅgā

ŚRĪPĀDA ŚRĪVALLABHA

A Datta saint’s birthplace

The saint. Śrīpāda Śrīvallabha, born at Pīṭhāpuram in the 14th century, is honoured by his followers as the first incarnation of Dattātreya in this age, before Narasiṃha Sarasvatī.

The shrine. The Śrīpāda Śrīvallabha Anagha Datta Kṣetram in the town draws Datta devotees from Maharashtra, Karnataka and Andhra. Tradition says he too worshipped Puruhūtikā.

A Datta thread. Māhur, the eighth pīṭha, is a Datta birthplace too: the hymn’s Goddesses keep meeting the Datta tradition.

HISTORY

Ancient Piṣṭapura
c. 350 Samudragupta’s Prayāga pillar names Piṣṭapura and its king Mahendra among those he defeated in the South.
4th–5th c. Vāsiṣṭha and Māṭhara kings rule here; inscriptions place Piṣṭapura in Kaliṅga.
7th c. Pulakeśin II takes the town; it is briefly the first capital of the Eastern Chalukyas before Veṅgī.
12th–14th c. Velanāṭi Choḍas (1186–1207), the Pithapuram Chalukyas, the Konas and the Reḍḍi kings.
15th c. The poet Śrīnātha mentions the town in his Bhīmeśvara Purāṇam.
1571–1947 The Pithapuram family: jāgīr in 1571, zamīndārī in 1749, British settlement in 1802; its Mahārājas fund a great Telugu dictionary and the first Telugu typewriter.

KUNTĪ MĀDHAVA

Viṣṇu in the town too
Kuntī MādhavaThe town’s great Viṣṇu temple, by tradition one of the Pañca Mādhava shrines, with Bindu Mādhava of Kāśī and Veṇī Mādhava of Prayāga
LegendKuntī, mother of the Pāṇḍavas, is said to have worshipped Mādhava here
Three in one townŚiva (Kukkuṭeśvara), Śakti (Puruhūtikā) and Viṣṇu (Kuntī Mādhava), plus a Datta shrine

ZAPPY: Is there any god not worshipped in Pīṭhāpuram?

PROF. BOLT: It would be hard to find one. Even the demon Gayāsura has a statue.
