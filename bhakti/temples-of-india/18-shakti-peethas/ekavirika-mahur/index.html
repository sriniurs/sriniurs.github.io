EKAVĪRIKĀ

REṆUKĀ OF MĀHUR · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The mother who came back as a face

FOREWORD

On a forested hill in eastern Maharashtra, pilgrims climb to a Goddess who has no body, only a great face of vermilion: Reṇukā, mother of Paraśurāma, whom the hymn calls Ekavīrikā.

Her son struck off her head at his father’s command, then asked for her life back. Māhur says she rose here, face first, from the earth.

This guide follows her story, the three hills of Māhur, and the question of which shrine is the hymn’s.

Jai Reṇukā Māyī,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: A son who killed his mother? That's a hard story.

PROF. BOLT: Hard, and then healed. The boon he asked for was her life. That's the part Māhur remembers.

ZAPPY: Just a face? No body?

PROF. BOLT: Just a face. And the story of why is one of the strangest in the Purāṇas.

WHERE IT IS

Forest hills of the eastern Deccan

The town. Māhur lies in the far east of Marathwada, a few kilometres inside it from the Pēṅgaṅgā river, which here divides Marathwada from Vidarbha. Telangana is close by.

The hills. Teak forest and three hills, each with its temple; deer, peafowl and bears still live in the woods.

Its partner. The hymn names Māhur right after Kolhapur, at the other end of the state.

HER NAMES

One Goddess, many names
Ekavīrikā / Ekavīrā“The one heroine”, “the sole hero’s mother”: one telling says she bore five sons, and one, Paraśurāma, was the hero
ReṇukāDaughter of King Reṇu, wife of the sage Jamadagni, mother of Paraśurāma
Jagadambā“Mother of the world”: Māhur’s Goddess as Mātāpur-nivāsinī Jagadambā
Mātṛpura“Mother’s city”: Māhur’s name in the Devī Bhāgavata
Yellammā, Yamāī…Reṇukā’s names across the Deccan and South: Yellammā, Yamāī, Ellai Ammaṉ, Padmākṣī

ZAPPY: Ekavīrā and Reṇukā are the same?

PROF. BOLT: In Māhur, mostly yes. But there's a second shrine that says otherwise. We'll get there.

THE PĪṬHA

The hymn says Ekavīrikā

The verse. kolhāpure mahālakṣmī, māhuryām ekavīrikā: the two Maharashtra seats, side by side.

What fell here? The lists disagree: most say Satī’s right hand, some the left hand, the back or the shoulder.

Two stories in one place. Māhur’s own tradition is not about Satī at all, but about another mother, Reṇukā, whose head rests here. The pīṭha and the local legend overlap, as they often do.

EKAVĪRĀ OR REṆUKĀ?

Two shrines, two answers

🛕 Reṇukā is Ekavīrā

Most pilgrims, and Māhur’s own tradition, treat the Reṇukā temple on Māhur hill as the pīṭha: Reṇukā’s other name is Ekavīrā.

🛕 Ekavīrā the elder sister

A small Ekavīrā temple in the forest near Māhur, on the Pēṅgaṅgā, is pointed out as the true “Ekavīrikā”, said to be Reṇukā’s elder sister. Here too only the head is worshipped.

ZAPPY: Which one do pilgrims visit?

PROF. BOLT: Almost everyone climbs to Reṇukā. The Ekavīrā shrine is little known, even in Maharashtra.

THE GODDESS

A face of vermilion

🔴 Only the face

The image in the sanctum is a large face, covered in vermilion. There is no body: the story says only her head came back.

🍃 Tāmbūl

Her special offering is viḍā tāmbūl, a folded betel leaf with nut and spices, given back to devotees as prasāda. She is also offered puraṇpolī every day.

ZAPPY: Betel leaf to a goddess?

PROF. BOLT: She's a mother receiving guests. In Maharashtra you offer a guest tāmbūl.

THE LEGEND · I

The axe and the boon
The doubt. Reṇukā, wife of the sage Jamadagni, went to fetch water and was lost for a moment in watching a prince at play in the river. Jamadagni, sensing her lapse, ordered his sons to kill her.
The obedient son. Four sons refused. The youngest, Paraśurāma, obeyed and struck off her head with his axe.
The boon. Pleased, Jamadagni offered a boon. Paraśurāma asked for his mother and brothers to live again. They were restored.

This is the Mahābhārata and Purāṇic story. Southern tellings add that her head was joined to another woman’s body, giving the Goddesses Yellammā and Māriyammaṉ.

THE LEGEND · II

Māhur’s own story
A second death. In Māhur’s telling, the king Sahasrārjuna attacked the hermitage to seize the wish-giving cow, and Reṇukā was mortally hurt.
The last rites. Paraśurāma, mad with grief, carried his mother away. The elders calmed him and sent him to perform her rites at Māhur, under the guidance of Dattātreya.
The face. He was told his mother would appear again. A popular local telling adds that he was not to look back; he did, and only her face had risen from the earth. That is the face worshipped today.

Local tradition; versions differ in detail.

THE THREE HILLS

Reṇukā, Datta and Anasūyā
Reṇukā’s hill. The Goddess’s temple, reached by a long flight of steps.
Datta Śikhara. The highest of the three: Māhur is honoured as a birthplace of Dattātreya, the teacher in whom Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva are one. Nearby, the Devdeveśvar temple of the Mahānubhāva sect is called Dattātreya’s place of rest.
Anasūyā’s hill. For Datta’s mother, Anasūyā, wife of the sage Atri. The three gods came to her as beggars to test her purity; by its power she turned them into babies, and they were born as her son.

ZAPPY: A Goddess, a guru and a mother on three hills?

PROF. BOLT: Two mothers and the son of one of them. Māhur is a town of mothers.

IN THE TEXTS

Mātṛpura in the Devī Gītā
Devī BhāgavataIn the Devī Gītā, the Goddess lists her holy places; second among them is Mātṛpura in the Sahyādri, “where Devī Reṇukā dwells”
MahābhārataThe story of Reṇukā, Jamadagni and Paraśurāma (in the Vana Parva)
The 18 hymnMāhuryām ekavīrikā: the line that names her among the eighteen
PoetsThe Marathi saint-poet Viṣṇudās and others sang her āratīs, still sung at Māhur

ZAPPY: The Goddess herself names Māhur?

PROF. BOLT: In the Devī Gītā she does, second on her own list. Not many places can say that.

HISTORY

The temple and the fort
Old Rock-cut caves near the town, the Pāṇḍav Leṇī, show the hills were sacred long before the present temple.
12th–13th c. The Reṇukā temple is said to be the work of a Yādava king of Devagiri.
12th c. Māhurgaḍ fort is built on another hill; Yādavas and the Gond kings of Chandrapur are named as builders.
1358–1527 The fort passes to the Bahmanīs and their successors; a Gond chief holds it for a time (1398).
1527–1724 Ahmadnagar, the Mughals (from 1617), the Marathas (1670–89), the Mughals again.
17th c. The local jāgīrdār Udārām Deśmukh (d. 1632) and his wife Sāvitrībāī, “Rāi Bāgan”, the royal tigress, hold Māhur.
1724–1948 Under the Nizām of Hyderabad, until Māhur joins India.

THE PĀṆḌAV LEṆĪ

Caves in the hill

The caves. Near Māhur town are rock-cut caves known as the Pāṇḍav Leṇī, “caves of the Pāṇḍavas”, a name given to many old caves in Maharashtra.

Whose? Sources disagree: some call them Buddhist monks’ caves, others date them to the Vākāṭaka or Rāṣṭrakūṭa age and compare them to Elephanta. What is clear is that the hills of Māhur were sacred long before the present temple.

ZAPPY: So which Ekavīrā is the hymn's?

PROF. BOLT: Māhur's, by the hymn's own words: māhuryām. The others share her name and her story.
