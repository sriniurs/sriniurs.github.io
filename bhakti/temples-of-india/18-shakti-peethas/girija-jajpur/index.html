GIRIJĀ

BIRAJĀ OF JĀJPUR · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The Goddess born from Brahmā’s fire

FOREWORD

On the Vaitaraṇī, a river named after the stream that the dead must cross, stands the shrine of Birajā, called Girijā in the Śakti Pīṭha hymn. Here, tradition says, Satī’s navel fell, and here Brahmā lit a sacrificial fire from which the Goddess rose.

She has only two arms, rare for Durgā, and one of them drives a spear into the buffalo-demon. In her courtyard is a well where people feed their ancestors, at the navel of a giant whose body spans India. Once a year she rides out in her own chariot.

This guide tells her legends, her festivals and the long history of Jājpur.

Jai Mā Birajā,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why is she called Girijā in the hymn but Birajā in Jājpur?

PROF. BOLT: Girijā is Pārvatī, the mountain's daughter. Birajā, 'the stainless', is what her own people have always called her.

ZAPPY: Two navels in one place?

PROF. BOLT: Satī's, and a giant's. Jājpur is where both stories meet.

WHERE IT IS

On the Vaitaraṇī

Odisha’s north. Jājpur lies in the coastal plain north of Cuttack, on the Vaitaraṇī, the river named after the stream every soul must cross to reach Yama’s land.

Two Pīṭhas. Odisha has Birajā here and Vimalā at Purī, both linked to the navel.

HER NAMES

Girijā, Birajā, Oḍḍiyāna
Girijā“Daughter of the mountain”: Pārvatī; the name in the Aṣṭādaśa Śakti Pīṭha hymn
Virajā · Birajā“The stainless one”, free of rajas; Odia pronounces V as B
Oḍḍiyāna PīṭhaTantric name: oḍḍiyāna is a woman’s ornament worn at the navel
Birajā KṣetraThe old name of the whole town and region
JājpurFrom Yajñapura, “city of the sacrifice”, or from King Yayāti

ZAPPY: The hymn's 'Oḍhyāṇa' is a piece of jewellery?

PROF. BOLT: A navel ornament. The place is named after where it would sit: Satī's navel.

THE PĪṬHA

Where the navel fell
The story. When Satī gave up her body at Dakṣa’s sacrifice, Śiva carried it across the world in grief; Viṣṇu’s discus cut it, and where each part fell, a Pīṭha arose.
Here. Temple tradition says Satī’s navel fell at Jājpur.
A shared claim. The Tantra Cūḍāmaṇi puts the navel in Utkala (Odisha), with Vimalā as Goddess and Jagannātha as Bhairava; many lists read that as Purī. Others place the navel here. Both are honoured.

LEGEND · BRAHMĀ’S FIRE

The Goddess from the sacrifice
The sacrifice. The Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa tells that Brahmā performed a great sacrifice on the bank of the Vaitaraṇī.
The Goddess. From the gārhapatya fire rose Pārvatī, and told Brahmā to call her Virajā.
She stays. Brahmā asked her to remain here as Śiva’s consort. She agreed, and around her arose the nine Durgās, the sixty-four Yoginīs and the Mātṛkās.
Yajñapura. So the town became the “city of the sacrifice”: Jājpur.

THE GODDESS

Two arms, one spear
FormDvibhujā: two-armed Mahiṣamardinī, very rare among Durgā images
Right handDrives a spear into the chest of Mahiṣāsura
Left handPulls the buffalo-demon’s tail
FeetOne on the lion, one on the buffalo
AgeScholars date the image well before the present temple; some say the Gupta era
Beside herA shrine of Bagalāmukhī, one of the ten Mahāvidyās, rare in temples

NĀBHI GAYĀ

The giant’s navel
GayāsuraA giant so holy that all who saw him were freed. The gods asked for his body as an altar; Viṣṇu pressed him down
His wishThat the gods stay where his body lay, and that rites for the dead done there free the ancestors
Three GayāsŚiro Gayā (head) in Bihar, Nābhi Gayā (navel) here, Pāda Gayā (feet) at Pīṭhāpuram
The wellNābhi Gayā is a circular well in the temple grounds; piṇḍas for ancestors are dropped into it
BeliefTradition says the offerings vanish by the next morning

ZAPPY: Pīṭhāpuram has the feet, Jājpur the navel?

PROF. BOLT: Two Śakti Pīṭhas, two Gayās. The Goddess and the giant keep meeting.

THE VAITARAṆĪ

The river at the gate of death
In mythThe Vaitaraṇī is the terrible river every soul must cross on the way to Yama; a cow given in charity helps the soul across
On earthJājpur’s river bears the name: to bathe here is to cross it in life
MahābhārataThe Pāṇḍavas bathe in the Vaitaraṇī on their pilgrimage, in the Vana Parva
Daśāśvamedha GhāṭWhere kings are said to have done horse sacrifices; the Somavaṃśī king Yayāti among them
Pitṛ ritesWith Nābhi Gayā, the river makes Jājpur a place for the dead as much as the living

ZAPPY: A real river named after the river of the dead?

PROF. BOLT: Bathe in it now, the belief goes, and the other one will be easy.

BIRAJĀ KṢETRA

A town of shrines
A crore of liṅgasTradition says Jājpur holds about a crore (ten million) Śiva-liṅgas
VarāhaVarāhanātha, Viṣṇu as boar, is worshipped as a guardian of the Kṣetra
TrilocaneśvaraA Śiva temple with fine carving on its hall
MātṛkāsLarge old images of the Seven Mothers, among Odisha’s finest sculpture
Four KṣetrasOdisha counts four holy regions: Purī (Viṣṇu), Bhubaneswar (Śiva), Koṇārka (Sūrya) and Jājpur (the Goddess)

THROUGH THE YEAR

A chariot for the Goddess
Durgā PūjāThe great festival: a sixteen-day worship (Ṣoḍaśa Dinātmikā), from the dark-fortnight aṣṭamī before Mahālayā to Āśvina Navamī
SiṃhadhvajaHer chariot, with a lion on its flag: the only chariot festival for a goddess in Odisha, by most accounts
SuniāThe chariot’s building begins on Suniā, the Gajapati kings’ new year day
NavarātriAlso kept as Aparājitā Pūjā

ZAPPY: Jagannātha has his chariots; she has hers?

PROF. BOLT: One, with a lion flag. And unlike his, it doesn't head to an aunt's house; it circles her own town.

HISTORY

Capital of kings
Purāṇas The Mahābhārata and Purāṇas praise the Vaitaraṇī and Virajā.
Early centuries CE The two-armed Mahiṣamardinī is carved and worshipped.
8th–10th c. The Bhauma-Karas rule from Virajā/Guhadevapāṭaka; Jājpur is their capital.
10th–11th c. The Somavaṃśī king Yayāti Keśarī, tradition’s founder of the temple, holds horse sacrifices here.
13th c. The present temple, in Kaliṅga style, is usually dated to this time.
Later Damage in the wars of the 16th century; the shrine restored and worship continued.

Dates don’t agree. Tradition credits Yayāti Keśarī, who ruled around the 10th–11th century, but the present building is usually dated to the 13th. He may have built an earlier temple on the site.
