KĀMĀKṢĪ

KĀÑCĪPURAM · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The Goddess whose eyes are love

FOREWORD

Kāñcīpuram is a city of Śiva temples, and of one Goddess. In the city’s great Śiva temples there is no separate shrine for his consort, because she lives in one place: Kāmākṣī, seated in yoga at the centre of the city.

She is Lalitā, who rose from the earth to destroy Bhaṇḍāsura; Pārvatī, who held a sand liṅga through a flood; the Goddess of the Śrī Cakra, of a mute poet’s five hundred verses, and of a golden image that travelled to Thanjavur.

Jai Kāmākṣī,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Is Kāmākṣī the same Goddess as Lalitā?

PROF. BOLT: The same. Kāñcī is where Lalitā sits as Kāmākṣī, and the Śrī Cakra in front of her says so.

ZAPPY: Kāmākṣī: 'eyes of love'?

PROF. BOLT: That's one reading. Another says her two eyes are Sarasvatī and Lakṣmī. Either way, one look from her is enough.

WHERE IT IS

Kāñcī, between the hills and the sea

The city. Kāñcīpuram lies on the Vegavatī and Pālār rivers, inland from Chennai. It was the capital of the Pallavas and is famous for its temples and its silk.

The yellow dots. Tiruvārūr and Thanjavur are where the golden image of Kāmākṣī went in the 18th century (see “The Golden Kāmākṣī”).

A holy city. Kāñcī is one of the seven mokṣapurīs, cities said to grant liberation, with Ayodhyā, Mathurā, Haridvār, Kāśī, Ujjain and Dvārakā.

HER NAMES

Love, eyes and ten million
Kāmākṣī“She whose eyes are love”; or “she whose eyes are ka and ma”, Sarasvatī and Lakṣmī
Kāmakoṭi“Ten million desires”, or the summit of desire: the name of her seat, the Kāmakoṭi Pīṭha
Kāmakoṭi Nāyaki“Lady of the Kāmakoṭi”: the temple’s Tamil name, Kāmakōṭṭam
Lalitā TripurasundarīThe supreme Goddess of Śrīvidyā, “beautiful in the three worlds”
Para Bhaṭṭārikā“The supreme Lady”: the main image
Śrī Mātā“The holy Mother”, the first name of the Lalitā Sahasranāma

ZAPPY: Why so many names about love and desire?

PROF. BOLT: In Śrīvidyā, desire isn't the enemy. The Goddess is the power that moves everything, and Kāmākṣī holds its bow.

THE PĪṬHA

The navel of the earth

The verse. kāmākṣī kāñcikāpure: the second name of the hymn, straight after Laṅkā.

What fell here. The usual South Indian list says Satī’s navel, which fits a local idea: Kāñcī as the nābhi, the navel or centre, of the earth. Other lists name her back.

A different emphasis. At Kāñcī the Satī story is not the main story. Temple tradition stresses Kāmākṣī as Lalitā, who rose to kill Bhaṇḍāsura, and as Pārvatī, who did penance here and won Śiva back.

THE GODDESS

Seated in the lotus posture

🪷 The pose

Kāmākṣī sits in padmāsana, the lotus posture of a yogin, facing south-east. She is a Goddess in meditation, not in battle.

🏹 The four

Noose (attachment held in check), goad (anger turned to good), sugarcane bow (the mind) and five flower arrows (the senses): the classic weapons of Lalitā.

Temple tradition says the one image holds three forms at once: gross (sthūla), subtle (sūkṣma) and causal (kāraṇa).

FIVE FORMS OF KĀMĀKṢĪ

One Goddess, five shrines
Para BhaṭṭārikāThe main image in the sanctum, in padmāsana
Tapa KāmākṣīKāmākṣī doing penance, to the right of the main image, near the Bila gate
Añjana KāmākṣīTo the left: linked with Lakṣmī, who did penance here to win back her beauty; also called Arūpa Lakṣmī. Devotees are given kuṅkuma here
Bangāru KāmākṣīThe golden Kāmākṣī: the original is in Thanjavur; Kāñcī keeps a five-metal image in her place
Utsava KāmākṣīThe festival image that goes out in procession

Lakṣmī’s story. Cursed to lose her beauty, Lakṣmī came to Kāmākṣī and did penance until it returned. That is why kuṅkuma is first offered at Añjana Kāmākṣī’s shrine and only then given to devotees.

ZAPPY: Why five Kāmākṣīs?

PROF. BOLT: Five ways to meet her: as queen, as yogini, as giver of beauty, as gold, and as the one who walks the streets with you.

THE ŚRĪ CAKRA

The diagram before the Goddess
The diagram. A Śrī Cakra (Śrī Yantra) is Lalitā’s own form drawn in geometry: nine interlocking triangles round a central point, ringed by lotus petals and a square with four gates.
Two founders. Tradition says the sage Durvāsas, a form of Śiva, first set a Śrī Cakra before the Goddess; the present one is credited to Ādi Śaṅkara.
The Gāyatrī Maṇḍapa. The sanctum hall has 24 pillars, for the 24 syllables of the Gāyatrī mantra; its four walls are said to stand for the four Vedas.

THE LEGEND · BHAṆḌĀSURA

The gods as parrots
The demon. Bhaṇḍāsura, born from the ashes of Kāma, the god of love, after Śiva burned him, grew so strong that he drove the gods from heaven.
Hiding as parrots. The gods fled to Kāñcī and did penance in the form of parrots, perched in the campaka trees.
Through the Bila. The Goddess rose through an opening in the earth, the Bila, and destroyed Bhaṇḍāsura. The Bila gate beside Tapa Kāmākṣī marks the spot, and leads to the sacred tree called the Bilākāśa.

ZAPPY: Is that why she's shown with a parrot?

PROF. BOLT: The parrot is the gods' own disguise. And in Kāñcī's songs, it's the bird that carries your prayer to her.

THE LEGEND · THE MANGO TREE

Pārvatī wins Śiva back
A playful mistake. Pārvatī once covered Śiva’s eyes in play, and the worlds went dark. To atone, she came down to earth and settled at Kāñcī.
The sand liṅga. Under a mango tree by the Vegavatī she made a liṅga of earth and worshipped it. Śiva tested her with a flood; she held the liṅga in her arms to save it.
The wedding. Pleased, Śiva appeared and married her. The liṅga is worshipped as Ekāmbareśvara, “Lord of the single mango tree”, the earth liṅga among Śiva’s five element temples.

This is why Kāmākṣī is called the consort of every Śiva in Kāñcī, and why the city’s Śiva temples have no Goddess shrine of their own.

HISTORY

From the Pallavas to today
5th–8th c. The temple may have been founded by the Pallava kings of Kāñcī.
9th c. The Āḻvārs’ hymns in the Divya Prabandham praise the Viṣṇu shrine now inside the temple, Tirukkaḷvaṉūr.
14th c. Major rebuilding; one historian credits it to the Cholas. The temple is modified again in the 17th century.
After 1565 With the fall of Vijayanagara, the temple’s attendants carry the golden image away for safety.
1739–1781 The golden Kāmākṣī rests in the Tyāgarāja temple at Tiruvārūr.
Late 18th c. She is taken to Thanjavur, under the Maratha kings, and stays there.
Today About 5 acres; administered by the state’s endowments department, with the head of the Kāñci Kāmakoṭi Pīṭham as hereditary trustee.

Dates for the early temple are uncertain; one legend even gives 1783 for its construction. The 14th-century Chola date comes from a single recent study.

THE GOLDEN KĀMĀKṢĪ

From Kāñcī to Thanjavur
Why she left. In the troubled years after Vijayanagara fell, the priests feared for the temple’s treasures. The golden image was carried south; tradition names a devotee called Kāmākṣī Dāsar.
A long road. She rested for decades in Tiruvārūr, then came to Thanjavur, where the Maratha kings gave her a temple of her own: Bangāru Kāmākṣī (Telugu bangāru, “gold”). Her face is dark; her body, gold.
A composer’s goddess. Śyāmā Śāstri, one of the Trinity of Carnatic music, was born into her priestly family and served her as priest. His songs to Kāmākṣī are among the loveliest in the music.

THE KĀMAKOṬI PĪṬHAM

The maṭha and Ādi Śaṅkara

🙏 The maṭha’s view

The Kāñci Kāmakoṭi Pīṭham holds that Ādi Śaṅkara himself founded it at Kāñcī, consecrated the Śrī Cakra, and ended his life here. The maṭha is closely tied to the temple; its head is hereditary trustee.

📚 The other view

The Śṛṅgeri maṭha and many historians do not accept that Śaṅkara founded a maṭha at Kāñcī; they place his life in the 8th century and his death in the Himalaya. One view sees the Kumbakonam maṭha as a branch that grew independent.

What both share. Whatever its founding, the temple keeps a shrine to Śaṅkara, and the hymns and rites of Śrīvidyā linked with his name are central to worship here.

IN THE HYMNS

Poets, a mute boy and a composer
Mūka Pañcaśatī500 verses in five centuries (Āryā, Pādāravinda, Stuti, Kaṭākṣa, Mandasmita), by Mūka Kavi, a mute boy who, the legend says, found his voice by her grace. The Kāmakoṭi maṭha counts him as one of its early heads
Lalitā SahasranāmaTemple tradition says Hayagrīva taught Lalitā’s thousand names to the sage Agastya here; a Hayagrīva shrine marks the spot
Śyāmā ŚāstriMany kṛtis, most of them addressed to Kāmākṣī, sung to the golden image he served
The 18 hymnKāmākṣī kāñcikāpure: the line that names her among the eighteen

ZAPPY: Elephants in the dawn worship?

PROF. BOLT: A cow and an elephant are honoured before the doors open. Kāñcī starts its day with the Goddess's own animals.

Māsi Pūram, Navarātri and her Fridays: the Goddess’s calendar.
