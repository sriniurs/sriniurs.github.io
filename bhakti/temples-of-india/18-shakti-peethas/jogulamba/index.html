JOGULĀMBĀ

ALAMPUR · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The fierce Mother of yoga

FOREWORD

On the bank of the Tuṅgabhadrā, among nine Chalukya temples thirteen centuries old, sits a Goddess with bared teeth and wild hair full of creatures: Jogulāmbā.

Her temple was destroyed in 1390. Her image waited in her husband’s shrine for six hundred years, until a new temple was built on the old site in 2005.

This guide tells her story, and the story of Alampur’s temples: one moved stone by stone to escape a dam.

Jai Jogulāmbā,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why does her temple look so new next to such old ones?

WHERE IT IS

Where two rivers meet

The town. Alampur stands on the left bank of the Tuṅgabhadrā, close to where it flows into the Kṛṣṇā, across the river from Kurnool.

The confluence. The meeting of the rivers, the Saṅgam, was holy ground; much of it now lies under the waters of the Śrīśailam dam.

Śrīśailam’s gate. Alampur is called the western gateway to Śrīśailam, the next pīṭha in the hymn, downstream on the Kṛṣṇā.

THE PĪṬHA

Where Satī’s upper teeth fell

The verse. alampure jogulāmbā, śrīśaile bhramarāmbikā: Alampur and Śrīśailam are named in the same breath, and stand on the same river system.

What fell here. Satī’s upper teeth (ūrdhva danta). A fierce Goddess with bared teeth and outstretched tongue keeps the memory.

A Yoginī seat. Among the eighteen, Alampur is the Yoginī Pīṭha: the seat of the Goddess as mistress of yoga and tantric power.

THE GODDESS

Ugra: the fierce form

🔥 The image

She sits in an ugra (fierce) form on a corpse, tongue out, her great mass of hair holding a scorpion, a frog, a lizard and a skull; some describe a bat or an owl as well.

🦂 What they mean

Devotees read them as her mastery over poison (scorpion), disease (frog) and hidden harm (lizard), and over death itself (corpse and skull).

With her in the sanctum: her two attendant powers Caṇḍī and Muṇḍī, the Saptamātṛkās, Gaṇeśa and Vīrabhadra with a vīṇā. The new temple is ringed by a pool of water, said to cool her fiery form.

THE LEGENDS

Brahmā’s penance and the alchemist
Brahmā’s penance. Local tradition says Brahmā did penance here to Śiva, who appeared to him. That is why the nine Śiva temples are all called “Brahma”: Bāla Brahma, Svarga Brahma and the rest.
The alchemist. Another story tells of Rasa Siddha, a master of alchemy who could turn base metal to gold, said to have begun the temples at Śiva’s bidding. Some even say the nine names were the names of his medicinal herbs.
The Goddess. In the Śakti story, she is here because Satī’s teeth fell here; in the local one, because Śiva and Brahmā’s holy ground needed a guardian.

These are local traditions; the inscriptions name Chalukya kings and queens as the builders.

HISTORY

Thirteen centuries at Alampur
Before 610s The Saṅgameśvara temple at the confluence: a label inscription places it before Pulakeśin II, making it the oldest Chalukya temple here.
640–753 The Badami Chalukyas build the nine Navabrahma temples.
681–696 Svarga Brahma is built by Lokāditya Ela Arasa in honour of King Vinayāditya’s queen.
702 Bāla Brahma begins its unbroken worship.
9th–11th c. Rāṣṭrakūṭas and later Chalukyas build the Papanāśi temples.
1390 The Jogulāmbā temple is destroyed by the Bahmani army; the image is taken to Bāla Brahma.
After 1390 Vijayanagara’s Harihara II is said to have sent troops and fortified the temple complex.
1979 Saṅgameśvara is moved, stone by stone, before the Śrīśailam dam floods its site.
2005 A new Jogulāmbā temple rises on the old site, 615 years later, and the image returns.
2016 Telangana names a new district Jogulāmbā Gadwal.

Why they matter. Eight of the nine have curved northern (Nāgara) towers, unusual this far south; their friezes even show Pañcatantra fables. Historians see them as a link to later Kākatīya temple art.

SAṄGAMEŚVARA

The temple that moved
At the confluence. The Saṅgameśvara temple stood at Kuḍavelli, where the Tuṅgabhadrā meets the Kṛṣṇā, about 20 km from Alampur.
The dam. The Śrīśailam reservoir was going to drown it. The Archaeological Survey took it apart block by block.
Rebuilt. It was put back together near Alampur in 1979, after years of work. The Navabrahma temples too were protected from the rising water.

PAPANĀŚI AND THE MUSEUM

More temples, more stones
PapanāśiA group of 23 temples, 9th–11th c., about 2.5 km south-west of Alampur, by the Rāṣṭrakūṭas and later Chalukyas
The museumAt the entrance of the Bāla Brahma complex: sculptures and inscriptions from the Navabrahma temples and the drowned villages
InscriptionsOld Kannada and Telugu records of kings, queens and gifts, from the 7th century onward

ZAPPY: So Alampur is really a museum town?

PROF. BOLT: A living one. Worship never stopped at Bāla Brahma, even when the Goddess had no home.
