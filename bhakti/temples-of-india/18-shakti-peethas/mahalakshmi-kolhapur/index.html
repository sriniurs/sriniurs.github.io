MAHĀLAKṢMĪ

AMBĀBĀĪ OF KOLHAPUR · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The Goddess who faces the sunset

FOREWORD

Kolhapur calls her Ambābāī, Mother. The hymn calls her Mahālakṣmī. Her dark stone image holds a citron and a mace, wears a serpent and a liṅga on her crown, and faces west, so that twice a year the setting sun walks up to her.

She killed the demon who gave the city its name. In another story, she left Viṣṇu’s heaven in a temper and Viṣṇu followed her to Tirumala.

This guide follows her legends, her temple, her centuries in hiding, and her return.

Jai Ambābāī,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why does the sun only reach her twice a year?

PROF. BOLT: The sun sets in a different place every day. Only twice a year does it line up with her west door.

WHERE IT IS

Kolhapur and Maharashtra’s Goddesses

The city. Kolhapur lies on the Pañcagaṅgā, “five Gaṅgās”, below the Sahyādri hills. Its old name, Karavīra, is still used for the temple’s Goddess: Karavīra-nivāsinī, “she who dwells in Karavīra”.

Dakṣiṇa Kāśī. Like several holy towns of the South, Kolhapur calls itself the “Kāśī of the South”, mainly because of this temple.

The yellow dots are Maharashtra’s other great Goddess seats, including Māhur, the next pīṭha in the hymn.

HER NAMES

Ambā, Lakṣmī, Karavīravāsinī
Ambābāī“Mother”: the name Kolhapur uses for her, as the city’s guardian
Mahālakṣmī“The great Lakṣmī”: the hymn’s name, and the temple’s formal one
Karavīra-nivāsinī“She who dwells in Karavīra”: a name in the Lakṣmī Sahasranāma
Siṃhavāhinī“Who rides the lion”: so an inscription of 1049 calls her
KolhāpuraThe city named after the demon she defeated, Kolhāsura

ZAPPY: Another city named after a demon? Like Mysuru!

PROF. BOLT: Same pattern: the Goddess wins, the demon asks to be remembered, the city keeps his name.

THE PĪṬHA

Where Satī’s eyes fell

The verse. kolhāpure mahālakṣmī, māhuryām ekavīrikā: Kolhapur and Māhur, the two Maharashtra seats, named together.

What fell here. Satī’s eyes, by the usual list. Some temple accounts add that the Goddess’s gaze watches over the city.

Older than the list. Kolhapur’s own text, the Karavīra Māhātmya, tells a different story of why she is here: the slaying of Kolhāsura.

THE GODDESS

Citron, mace, shield and cup

🖐 Four hands

A mātuliṅga (citron), a gadā (mace), a kheṭa (shield) and a pānapātra (drinking cup). The image is of dark stone, about three feet high, on a stone platform.

🐍 On her head

A five-hooded serpent shades her crown, and a small liṅga sits in it. Behind her stands her mount, a stone lion. A Śrī Yantra is carved on the wall.

ZAPPY: A citron? Not a lotus?

PROF. BOLT: Not a lotus in sight. This is not the Lakṣmī of the calendars. Turn the page.

LAKṢMĪ OR AMBĀ?

The supreme Mahālakṣmī

The puzzle. Mahālakṣmī with a mace and shield, a serpent and a liṅga on her head, riding a lion? That is not Viṣṇu’s gentle consort.

The answer. An appendix to the Devī Māhātmya, the Prādhānika Rahasya, describes the supreme Mahālakṣmī, the first Goddess, from whom Mahākālī and Mahāsarasvatī come: she holds a citron, mace, shield and cup, and bears a serpent, a liṅga and a yoni on her head. Kolhapur’s image matches it closely.

Both at once. So Ambābāī is the great Goddess of the Devī Māhātmya, honoured by Śaivas, Śāktas and Vaiṣṇavas alike. An inscription of 1049 calls her both lion-rider and Śiva’s consort; the Tirupati story makes her Viṣṇu’s.

Read the trinity of Mahākālī, Mahālakṣmī and Mahāsarasvatī in our Devī Māhātmya series.

THE LEGEND · KOLHĀSURA

How the city got its name
The demon. The Karavīra Māhātmya tells of Kolhāsura, who tormented gods and people in the land of Karavīra.
The Goddess. Mahālakṣmī came to protect the people and killed him in battle.
The boon. Dying, the demon asked that the place bear his name. She agreed: Kolhāpura. The spot where he fell became a tīrtha, and she stayed to guard the city.

The Karavīra Māhātmya is a local sacred text, said to belong to the Skanda Purāṇa; one study dates the Kolhāpur Māhātmya to about 1730.

THE LEGEND · TIRUPATI

Why Lakṣmī left Vaikuṇṭha
The kick. The sage Bhṛgu, testing the gods, came to Viṣṇu and, ignored, kicked him on the chest, where Lakṣmī lives. Viṣṇu only asked if the sage’s foot was hurt.
Lakṣmī leaves. Angry that her husband had not defended her place, Lakṣmī left Vaikuṇṭha and came to Kolhapur.
Viṣṇu follows. Viṣṇu came down to earth searching for her and settled on the Tirumala hill as Śrīnivāsa, Lord Veṅkaṭeśvara.

ZAPPY: So Tirupati and Kolhapur are a married couple who quarrelled?

PROF. BOLT: In the story, yes. Many pilgrims visit both, to see them both.

THE KIRAṆOTSAV

When the sun bows to her
WhenTwice a year, around 31 January – 2 February and 9 – 11 November, about three days each
HowThe setting sun, low in the west, shines straight through the great west gate and the halls into the sanctum
The orderDay one, her feet; day two, her chest; day three, her whole form
MeaningDevotees say the Sun god comes to pay homage to the Goddess
TodayNew buildings and changes along the line have shifted or shortened it in some years

ZAPPY: The temple was built to catch the sunset?

PROF. BOLT: She faces west for a reason. Twice a year, the light walks up to her.

HISTORY · I

Chalukyas and Śilāhāras
Legend A Konkan king, Karṇadeva, is said to have cleared the forest and found the shrine (one tradition says 109 CE).
7th c. The first temple, often credited to the Chalukyas; one account gives 634 CE.
940–1212 Kolhapur is the seat of the Śilāhāra kings; their age gives the temple much of its present core.
1049 An inscription of a devotee of “Mahālakṣmī of Kolhapur” calls her lion-rider and Śiva’s consort.
11th c. Gaṇḍarāditya of the Śilāhāras completes the temple and builds the path around the sanctum.
Later The Yādavas of Devagiri and others patronise the shrine.

Dates for the earliest stages are traditions, not inscriptions. Jain Tīrthaṅkara figures carved on the sanctum walls reflect the Śilāhāra age, when Jainism flourished in Kolhapur.

HISTORY · II

Hidden, then home
14th–17th c. In the Sultanate and Ādilshāhī years the image is hidden in a priest’s house for safety.
Early 1700s Tārābāī makes Kolhapur a Maratha capital. A priest dreams of where the Goddess is hidden; Sambhājī of Kolhapur has her found.
c. 1715 Sidhojī Hindurāv Ghorpaḍe reinstalls the image on Vijayādaśamī. (Sources give 1712, 1715 or 1722.)
1838–43 The Garuḍa Maṇḍapa is added under Dājī Paṇḍit.

THE TEMPLE

Black stone, west door
StyleHemāḍpanti-style dark stone, richly carved below, with a plainer later spire, which suggests rebuilding after the image returned
FacingWest, toward the setting sun
ShrinesMahākālī and Mahāsarasvatī, the other two of the Devī Māhātmya trinity; Gaṇapati in an upper shrine; and many more
Garuḍa MaṇḍapaA 19th-century hall facing the sanctum
Maṇikarṇikā KuṇḍThe old temple tank, recently restored

ZAPPY: Why does the bottom look older than the top?

PROF. BOLT: Because it is. The carved base is Śilāhāra; the spire came later, after the troubles.

SAVING THE IMAGE

A thousand years of abhiṣeka
The problem. Centuries of bathing the image with milk, water and other offerings had worn and cracked the stone.
The treatments. Conservation was done in 1955; the Archaeological Survey treated it again in 2015 with a coating called vajralepa (darśan stopped for two weeks), and again in April 2024.
The debate. Devotees and experts have argued over methods: some praise the care taken; others say chemical work did harm, and there has even been talk of replacing the image. It remains a sensitive subject in Kolhapur.

THREE AND A HALF PĪṬHAS

Maharashtra’s Goddess circuit
KolhapurMahālakṣmī: a full pīṭha
TuljāpurTulajā Bhavānī, the Bhosales’ family Goddess: a full pīṭha
MāhurReṇukā (Ekavīrā): a full pīṭha, and the next in our hymn
VaṇīSaptaśṛṅgī on her seven peaks: the “half” pīṭha

A Marathi tradition. The “three and a half Śakti Pīṭhas” (sāḍe tīn śaktipīṭhe) are a much-loved pilgrimage of Maharashtra, separate from the 51 or 18 lists. Two of them, Kolhapur and Māhur, are also in the eighteen.

ZAPPY: Why only half for Saptaśṛṅgī?

PROF. BOLT: One explanation: the three and a half stand for the syllable Om, A-U-M and the half-syllable after. Saptaśṛṅgī is the half.
