FOREWORD

"Annapūrṇe sadāpūrṇe…" Many of us have heard these words said quietly before a meal, without knowing they are the closing lines of a much longer hymn. The Śrī Annapūrṇā Stotram is Śaṅkara's song to the Mother who feeds the world, and ten times over it ends with the same humble request: bhikṣāṃ dehi, "Mother, give me alms."

This small book opens up all twelve verses in simple English: what each one says, what its key words mean, and the teaching inside. It is for anyone who has chanted the hymn, or just its last two lines, for years without knowing their meaning, and for anyone meeting it for the first time.

May every reader come away grateful for every meal, generous with every plate, and hungry, as Śaṅkara was, for the food that truly fills: knowledge and a free heart. Śrī Annapūrṇeśvaryai namaḥ.

THE STORY · ŚIVA'S BEGGING BOWL

How the Goddess taught the world that food is sacred
The Kāśī legend in 5 frames
"It's all māyā." Śiva tells Pārvatī that the world is illusion, and food is part of that illusion.
The Mother withdraws. To show what food really means, Pārvatī disappears, and with Her, nourishment vanishes from the world. Famine spreads.
One kitchen left. Śiva and his followers learn that food can still be found in only one place: a kitchen in Kāśī.
The bowl. Śiva comes to Kāśī with a begging bowl. Pārvatī, as Annapūrṇā, feeds him with Her own hands.
She stays. Annapūrṇā remains in Kāśī, beside Viśvanātha, so that no one in the city goes hungry.

The lesson

The material world isn't to be dismissed: food is sacred.

The image

Śiva holding out a bowl to Her: even the Lord receives from the Mother.

The hymn

Śaṅkara stands where Śiva stood, bowl in hand: bhikṣāṃ dehi.

ZAPPY: Śiva had to beg? He's the greatest of gods!

PROF. BOLT: That's the whole point of the story. If even He needs Her food, who are we to waste it?

Food is not "just food". In this story it's the Mother's own hand feeding the world.

ABOUT THE HYMN

Kāśī, Horanadu, and twelve verses of one request

✍️ Author and place

Traditionally Ādi Śaṅkarācārya, composed, tradition says, at the Annapūrṇā temple in Kāśī, a few steps from Kāśī Viśvanātha. Also called the Annapūrṇāṣṭakam, though it has 12 verses.

📍 Close to home: Horanadu

The Annapoorneshwari temple at Horanadu, on the Bhadra in Chikkamagaluru district, is said to have been consecrated by Sage Agastya. It serves three free meals a day to every visitor.

One refrain, ten times. Each of the first ten verses ends with the same line: bhikṣāṃ dehi kṛpāvalambana-karī mātānnapūrṇeśvarī, "Mother Annapūrṇeśvarī, giver of compassion's support, give me alms." The verses before it describe Her from every angle: queen, warrior, the Earth, the alphabet, the cosmic pot, the nursing mother.

The reveal. Only in verse 11 do we learn what Śaṅkara is really begging for: jñāna and vairāgya, knowledge and detachment. Verse 12 then ends with one of the most quoted lines in Sanskrit devotion: Pārvatī is my mother, Śiva my father, devotees my family, the three worlds my home.

How people use it. Many families chant the whole hymn on Fridays and festival days. Its last two verses are said before meals in countless homes, and the hymn is sung at the āratī in Varanasi.

VERSE 1 · GOING DEEPER

GIVER OF JOY, QUEEN OF KĀŚĪ

Who is Annapūrṇā? Anna means food and pūrṇā means full: "She who is full of food", the form of Pārvatī who feeds the world. In pictures She holds a jewelled bowl of rice and a golden ladle. Her great temple stands in Kāśī (Varanasi), a few steps from Kāśī Viśvanātha, and tradition says Śaṅkara composed this hymn there.

The opening titles. She is nityānanda-karī, giver of lasting joy, and varābhaya-karī: one hand grants boons, the other says "do not fear". She is saundarya-ratnākarī, an ocean of beauty, and pratyakṣa-māheśvarī, the Great Goddess present before our eyes, not a distant idea. Prāleyācala-vaṃśa-pāvana-karī: She blessed the family of the snowy mountain, Himavān, by being born as his daughter Pārvatī.

The refrain. Then comes the line that ends each of the first ten verses: bhikṣāṃ dehi kṛpāvalambana-karī mātānnapūrṇeśvarī, "O Mother Annapūrṇeśvarī, who gives the support of compassion, give me alms." A sannyāsī lives on bhikṣā, food given by householders. Here Śaṅkara stands at the Mother's own door with his bowl.

💡 Queen of Kāśī

In Varanasi, Annapūrṇā is honoured as the Queen of Kāśī beside Viśvanātha, its King. A popular belief says no one in Kāśī goes to sleep hungry, by Her grace.

💡 Ratnākara

"Mine of jewels" is a poetic name for the ocean. Calling Her an ocean of beauty borrows that image.

Asking is not weakness. The hymn's great teacher calls himself a beggar at Her door. Whatever we have, food, learning or success, came through someone's giving. Remembering that keeps the heart humble and grateful.

ZAPPY: Śaṅkara, the great teacher, begging for food?

PROF. BOLT: A monk lives on alms. But wait for verse 11 to find out what food he really means.

Every one of the first ten verses ends with bhikṣāṃ dehi, "give me alms, Mother". The greatest teacher asks like a child.

VERSE 2 · GOING DEEPER

ADORNED IN GOLD AND PEARLS

A royal portrait. The second verse paints Her as a queen. Nānā-ratna-vicitra-bhūṣaṇa: ornaments of many coloured jewels. Hemāmbarāḍambarī: splendid in golden robes. A pearl necklace (muktā-hāra) hangs and shines upon Her.

Fragrance. Kāśmīra is saffron (named after Kashmir, where it grows) and aguru is aloe-wood, or agarwood, a costly incense. She is kāśmīrāguru-vāsitā, scented with both, and ruci-karī, radiant and delightful. Indian poetry often describes the divine through fragrance: felt before it is seen.

Why all this splendour? Annapūrṇā is the Mother of plenty. Her ornaments say that abundance itself is sacred: a home with enough food, enough comfort and enough to share is a home where the Goddess lives. And again, the same request: bhikṣāṃ dehi, give me alms.

💡 Kāśmīra = saffron

Sanskrit calls saffron kāśmīra or kuṅkuma. Kashmir has been famous for it for centuries.

💡 Agarwood

Aguru is a resin-rich wood prized across Asia for incense and perfume: a sign of royal luxury in old poetry.

Plenty is nothing to be ashamed of when it's held as Her gift. What matters is what we do with it. Splendour that is shared becomes worship; splendour that is hoarded becomes a burden.

ZAPPY: Why so much jewellery for the goddess of food?

PROF. BOLT: A full kitchen is a rich house. Her splendour is what plenty looks like.

She is abundance made visible: gold, pearls, fragrance, and a full pot.

VERSE 3 · GOING DEEPER

JOY OF YOGA, GUARDIAN OF THREE WORLDS

Inner and outer. Yogānanda-karī: She gives the joy of yoga, the calm of a gathered mind. Ripu-kṣaya-karī: She destroys enemies. Tradition reads that two ways: outer threats, and the six inner enemies (desire, anger, greed, delusion, pride and envy).

Dharma and artha. Dharmārtha-niṣṭhā-karī: She makes us niṣṭha, firmly established, in dharma (right conduct) and artha (wealth and livelihood). Indian thought names four aims of life: dharma, artha, kāma and mokṣa. Here two are joined: wealth that stands on the ground of right conduct.

Light and protection. Her radiance ripples (laharī) like the moon, sun and fire, the three great lights. She is trailokya-rakṣā-karī, guardian of the three worlds, and sarvaiśvarya-samasta-vāñchita-karī, giver of every prosperity and every wish.

💡 Six inner enemies

Kāma (desire), krodha (anger), lobha (greed), moha (delusion), mada (pride) and mātsarya (envy): the ṣaḍ-ripu, often meant when hymns speak of "enemies".

💡 Four aims of life

The puruṣārthas: dharma, artha, kāma and mokṣa. Right living, wealth, enjoyment and liberation.

Annapūrṇā's food feeds the body, but this verse reminds us She also feeds the mind with yoga's calm. A full stomach with a restless mind is only half a meal.

ZAPPY: Dharma and artha together?

PROF. BOLT: Right living and wealth, side by side. Earn, but earn rightly.

She gives inner joy and outer safety together: yoga's calm and the world's protection.

VERSE 4 · GOING DEEPER

GAURĪ OF KAILĀSA, OPENER OF FREEDOM'S DOOR

Her home and Her names. Kailāsācala-kandarālaya-karī: She makes Her home in the caves of Mount Kailāsa, Śiva's mountain. Then a string of names: Gaurī (the fair one), Umā, Śaṅkarī (Śaṅkara's consort) and Kaumārī (the eternal maiden). The Goddess of Kāśī is the same Pārvatī of the Himālaya.

Knowledge. Nigamārtha-gocara-karī: She brings the meaning (artha) of the Vedas (nigama) within our reach. Oṅkāra-bījākṣarī: Her seed-syllable is Om. The giver of food is also the giver of understanding.

Freedom. The strongest phrase in the verse: mokṣa-dvāra-kapāṭa-pāṭana-karī, She who breaks open (pāṭana) the panels (kapāṭa) of the door of liberation. Not a polite opening: She bursts them. For a seeker, that is the greatest alms of all.

💡 Kāśī and mokṣa

Kāśī has long been believed to grant liberation to those who die there. "Opener of mokṣa's doors" fits Her role as Queen of that city.

💡 Bīja

A bīja-akṣara, seed syllable, is a one-syllable mantra that holds a deity's power the way a seed holds a tree.

Hunger comes in layers: for food, for understanding, for freedom. The same Mother answers each in turn. Being fed is the beginning of the journey, not its end.

ZAPPY: The goddess of food opens the door to liberation?

PROF. BOLT: First She feeds you. Then She shows you the way out. Good mothers do both.

She feeds the body, explains the Vedas and bursts open the door to freedom.

VERSE 5 · GOING DEEPER

HOLDER OF THE COSMIC POT

The cosmic pot. Brahmāṇḍa-bhāṇḍodarī: brahmāṇḍa is "Brahmā's egg", the universe; bhāṇḍa is a pot; udara is the belly. She holds the whole universe within Her as a pot holds food. It's a perfect image for the Goddess of food: Her rice-pot and the cosmos are the same idea.

Seen and unseen. Dṛśyādṛśya-vibhūti-vāhana-karī: She carries all glory, visible and invisible. Līlā-nāṭaka-sūtra-bhedana-karī: She cuts the strings (sūtra) of the drama of divine play, the strings that make the puppet-show of illusion move. When She cuts them, the seeker is free.

Lamp and delight. Vijñāna-dīpāṅkurī: She is the flame (aṅkura, literally "sprout") of the lamp of true knowledge. Śrī-viśveśa-manaḥ-prasādana-karī: She brings joy to the mind of Viśveśvara, Śiva as Lord of Kāśī.

💡 Brahmāṇḍa

"Brahmā's egg": the old Indian word for the universe, imagined as a vast egg. One of the eighteen great Purāṇas is named after it.

💡 Viśveśvara

"Lord of the universe": Śiva as worshipped at Kāśī Viśvanātha, Annapūrṇā's neighbour in the old city.

Life can feel like a play we're caught in, pulled by strings of habit and fear. Wisdom is the flame She lights and the scissors She holds. Prayer is asking Her to use both.

ZAPPY: She holds the universe like a pot holds rice?

PROF. BOLT: That's exactly the picture. The Mother of food carries the whole world in Her pot.

The pot of rice in Her hand is a picture of the universe She holds.

VERSE 6 · GOING DEEPER

THE EARTH ITSELF, GIVER OF DAILY FOOD

Urvī. The verse opens with one bold word: urvī, the Earth. Annapūrṇā isn't only a goddess who hands out food; She is the ground from which all food grows. Every grain of rice, every vegetable, every fruit begins in Her.

Queen of everyone. Sarva-janeśvarī: queen of all people, not only devotees. Hunger doesn't check caste, creed or merit, and neither does She. Her hair is veṇī-nīla, dark like a blue-black braid: a homely, human detail in a cosmic hymn.

Every day. Nityānna-dāneśvarī: the Goddess who gives food nitya, every day. Not once, as a miracle, but daily and quietly, through farmers, cooks and mothers. Sarvānanda-karī, sadā-śubha-karī: She brings all joy and always does good.

💡 Horanadu, Karnataka

At the Annapoorneshwari temple at Horanadu on the Bhadra river in Chikkamagaluru district, all three meals are served free to every visitor, whatever their religion, caste or creed.

💡 Annadāna

Giving food, annadāna, is praised in Indian tradition as one of the highest gifts, because it sustains life itself.

The next time you eat, think of the long chain behind the plate: earth, rain, farmer, trader, cook. The hymn calls the start of that chain the Mother. A moment of thanks before eating is a small way of saying so.

ZAPPY: She is the Earth?

PROF. BOLT: Where does every grain of rice come from? Exactly.

Every meal begins in the soil, and the hymn calls that soil the Mother Herself.

VERSE 7 · GOING DEEPER

EVERY LETTER, EVERY CYCLE

The alphabet. Ādi-kṣānta-samasta-varṇana-karī: She forms all the letters from a (the first) to kṣa (the last in the traditional list). In the Goddess traditions, the letters of the alphabet are Her body. Every word, prayer and poem is made of Her.

Three acts. Śambhos tri-bhāvā-karī: She carries out Śambhu's (Śiva's) three acts, usually read as creation, preservation and dissolution. Śiva is the still ground; She is the power that does the work.

Sprouting and night. Nityāṅkurā: ever-sprouting, like seeds that keep coming up, just right for a goddess of food. Śarvarī: night, the rest after the day's work. Kāmākāṅkṣa-karī: She fulfils wishes. Janodaya-karī: She raises people up.

💡 Mātṛkā

The Sanskrit letters are called mātṛkā, "little mothers". Many hymns describe the Goddess as made of the fifty letters from a to kṣa.

💡 Aṅkura

"Sprout": the same word used in verse 5 for the flame of knowledge. Food and wisdom both "sprout" from Her.

Our words, like our food, come from Her. Speaking kindly is a way of giving back what we were given. A harsh word wastes Her letters as surely as thrown-away food wastes Her grain.

ZAPPY: She's the alphabet too?

PROF. BOLT: Every word you speak is built from Her letters. Food for the body, letters for the mind.

She is every letter we speak and every cycle the world goes through.

VERSE 8 · GOING DEEPER

DAKṢA'S DAUGHTER, THE NURSING MOTHER

Dākṣāyaṇī. She is Satī, daughter of Dakṣa and Śiva's first wife, who was born again as Pārvatī. The hymn quietly ties every form together: Satī, Pārvatī, Gaurī, Annapūrṇā, all one Mother.

The nursing Mother. Svādu-payodharā: She whose breasts give sweet milk. Before rice or bread, every human being's first food is a mother's milk. Calling Annapūrṇā this is the most natural thing in the world: She is our first nourishment.

Good fortune. Vāmā, the lovely one (also "She on the left", Śiva's side); priya-karī, kind and dear; saubhāgya-māheśvarī, Great Goddess of good fortune. And, as always, She fulfils devotees' wishes and does good.

💡 Vāmā

Means both "left" and "lovely". In images of Śiva and Pārvatī together, the Goddess sits on Śiva's left.

💡 Satī and the Śakti Pīṭhas

After Satī gave up her body at Dakṣa's sacrifice, her body's parts are said to have fallen at the Śakti Pīṭhas across the land. Verse 10 recalls that sacrifice.

The first gift any of us received was food from a mother who asked nothing in return. Annapūrṇā worship is, in a way, remembering that first unpaid kindness, and passing it on.

ZAPPY: Sweet milk? That's very motherly.

PROF. BOLT: That's the point. Before any rice, a baby's first food is its mother's milk. She's that Mother.

Before any other food there is a mother's milk. Annapūrṇā is that first nourishment.

VERSE 9 · GOING DEEPER

BRIGHT AS COUNTLESS SUNS

Blinding light. Candrārkānala-koṭi-koṭi-sadṛśā: like crores upon crores of moons, suns and fires. Śaṅkara piles up numbers to say Her light is beyond measure. Even Her earrings shine like the three great lights.

Four things in Her hands. Mālā (rosary or garland), pustaka (book), pāśa (noose) and aṅkuśa (goad). The rosary is prayer and remembrance; the book is knowledge; the noose restrains what runs wild; the goad steers and urges forward.

More than food. Bowl and ladle are Annapūrṇā's usual emblems, but this verse shows Her supreme form, holding the tools of the inner life. The Goddess who fills the plate also trains the mind.

💡 Koṭi

Koṭi means ten million, a crore. "Koṭi-koṭi" is crores upon crores: the poet's way of saying "beyond counting".

💡 Shared emblems

The noose and goad are also Lalitā's weapons, another reminder that the Goddess traditions see one Mother in many forms.

Remembrance, learning, restraint and drive: a simple daily checklist hidden in a hymn. Feed the mind with all four, and the body's food is put to good use.

ZAPPY: Book and rosary in the hands of the food goddess?

PROF. BOLT: Prayer and knowledge in two hands, restraint and direction in the other two. She feeds more than stomachs.

Her four hands hold prayer, knowledge, restraint and direction.

VERSE 10 · GOING DEEPER

PROTECTOR, OCEAN OF COMPASSION, HEALER

Protector of protectors. Kṣatra-trāṇa-karī: kṣatra is the warrior's power, the duty to protect others, and She protects even them. Mahābhaya-karī (read as mahā-abhaya-karī): She gives great fearlessness. Then three tender names: mātā (Mother), kṛpā-sāgarī (ocean of compassion), sākṣān-mokṣa-karī (giver of liberation, directly).

One Mother again. Sadā śiva-karī: always auspicious. Viśveśvarī: Queen of the universe, consort of Viśveśvara. Śrī-dharī: She who bears Śrī, Lakṣmī's form. The Goddess of food is also the Goddess of wealth.

Dakṣa and health. Dakṣākranda-karī: She who made Dakṣa weep. Dakṣa held a great sacrifice, insulted Śiva and humiliated his daughter Satī; the sacrifice was destroyed and his pride broken. Nirāmaya-karī: She frees from disease (āmaya). Compassion and correction, healing and humbling: all one Mother.

💡 Dakṣa's sacrifice

A famous Purāṇic story: Dakṣa shut Śiva out of his yajña, Satī gave up her body in protest, and Śiva's fury, through Vīrabhadra, wrecked the sacrifice.

💡 Food and health

Nirāmaya-karī fits the Goddess of food. Indian medicine has always treated the right food as the foundation of good health.

Compassion isn't the same as softness. A good mother feeds, comforts and heals, and also says no to cruelty and pride. Annapūrṇā's kindness has a backbone.

ZAPPY: Why would a compassionate mother make someone weep?

PROF. BOLT: Dakṣa insulted Śiva and humiliated Satī. Even an ocean of compassion has a shore.

She protects, frees and heals, and arrogance doesn't go unanswered.

VERSES 11–12 · GOING DEEPER

ANNAPŪRṆE SADĀPŪRṆE: THE FAMOUS CLOSING

The most famous lines. Verse 11, annapūrṇe sadāpūrṇe, is chanted in countless homes before meals, often on its own. Annapūrṇā is sadā-pūrṇā, ever full: Her bowl never runs out. She is śaṅkara-prāṇa-vallabhā, as dear to Śiva as life itself.

The twist. After ten verses of "give me alms", Śaṅkara finally says what he's asking for: jñāna-vairāgya-siddhyartham, for the attainment of knowledge and detachment. The food he begged for all along was wisdom. A monk at the Mother's door asks for the one thing that truly fills.

A new family. Verse 12 is the closing blessing: my mother is Pārvatī, my father is Maheśvara, my relatives are Śiva's devotees, and my homeland is the three worlds. For a sannyāsī who has left his home, it's a beautiful statement. He hasn't lost a family; he has gained the whole universe as one.

💡 Before meals

In many homes, especially in South India, "annapūrṇe sadāpūrṇe…" is said before eating, turning an ordinary meal into an offering.

💡 Annapūrṇā Jayantī

Her festival falls on the full moon of Mārgaśīrṣa: in 2026, on 23 December. Food donation is the heart of the day.

The world is our home and everyone in it is family: one of the oldest ideas in Indian thought, in four short lines. Feed people as if they were your relatives, because the hymn says they are.

ZAPPY: So the alms he wanted all along was… knowledge?

PROF. BOLT: Knowledge and detachment. Ten times he asked for rice, and only now you learn what the rice was.

The real alms: jñāna and vairāgya. The real family: everyone, everywhere.
