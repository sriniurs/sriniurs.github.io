GAJA LAKṢMĪ

AṢṬA LAKṢMĪ · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Lakṣmī with the elephants

FOREWORD

Gaja Lakṣmī is the Goddess on a lotus while two elephants pour water over her from pots held in their trunks. It is the oldest surviving image of Lakṣmī, carved more than two thousand years ago, and still the one above countless temple doors.

This guide follows the image through Vedic poetry, Buddhist and Jain stūpas, ancient coins and Karnataka’s temple doorways, and asks what the elephants mean.

AT A GLANCE

Gaja Lakṣmī on one page
NameGaja Lakṣmī, “Lakṣmī with elephants”; also Abhiṣeka Lakṣmī
GiftRoyal power, rain and abundance
FormSeated or standing on a lotus, holding lotuses; two (sometimes four) elephants pour water on her
AgeAmong the oldest Indian goddess images, from the 2nd century BCE
SeenAbove temple doors, on coins, on stūpa gateways

ZAPPY: Two elephants giving a bath?

PROF. BOLT: A royal bath. It's how kings were crowned.

Gaja Lakṣmī may be the single most widespread image in Indian art. It appears on Buddhist railings, Jain caves, Hindu temples, coins, seals, textiles, wedding cards and house doors. Its meaning was clear to all: abundance, rain and royal good fortune.

Because the image is so old and so shared, Gaja Lakṣmī is also a reminder that much of Indian religious imagery grew from a common stock before the traditions separated.

IN THE ŚRĪ SŪKTA

Delighting in elephants
Hastināda-pramodinīShe “delights in the trumpeting of elephants”
Aśvapūrvām rathamadhyām“With horses before her, in the middle of chariots”: a royal procession
Padme sthitām“Standing on a lotus”
RootThe hymn already contains the Gaja Lakṣmī picture

aśvapūrvāṃ rathamadhyāṃ hastinādapramodinīm
śriyaṃ devīm upahvaye śrīr mā devī juṣatām

I call on Śrī: horses before her, chariots around her, delighting in the elephants’ call. May the Goddess Śrī be pleased with me.

The Śrī Sūkta’s third verse, given here, sets the scene of a royal procession: horses in front, chariots in the middle, elephants trumpeting. The Goddess is called to come with all this splendour.

Elsewhere the hymn places her on a lotus, lotus-coloured and lotus-scented. The elephant-bath picture combines these elements.

THE OCEAN’S BATH

From the churning
RisingWhen Lakṣmī rose from the ocean of milk, the elephants of the directions bathed her
Golden potsThey poured the waters of sacred rivers from golden jars
ThenShe garlanded Viṣṇu
SourceViṣṇu Purāṇa 1.9 and the Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.8

In the Bhāgavata Purāṇa’s telling, the sages perform Lakṣmī’s abhiṣeka as she rises, the rivers bring their waters, and the elephants of the quarters pour them from golden pots. It is a coronation scene, with the Goddess as queen of the universe.

Gaja Lakṣmī thus also commemorates Lakṣmī’s return to the world after Durvāsā’s curse.

UdayagiriOdisha’s Jain caves (Rānī Gumphā) show her too

ZAPPY: On a Buddhist stūpa?

PROF. BOLT: Good luck belongs to everyone. Buddhists and Jains used her too.

At Bharhut and Sāñcī, Gaja Lakṣmī appears among the carved medallions and on the architraves of the gateways. Scholars debate whether Buddhists saw her as a goddess or as a symbol, perhaps even of the Buddha’s mother Māyā; either way she meant good fortune.

In Jain tradition, the mothers of the Tīrthaṅkaras see fourteen (or sixteen) auspicious dreams before the birth; one of them is Śrī, anointed by elephants. Jain manuscripts painted this scene for centuries.

ON ANCIENT COINS

Fortune in the purse
Kauśāmbī and UjjainEarly punch-marked and cast coins of north India show her
AzilisesIndo-Scythian king of the 1st century BCE: Gaja Lakṣmī on his coins
MeaningA king’s fortune and the land’s prosperity on money itself
ContinuityLakṣmī on coins continued under the Guptas and later dynasties

Coins of Kauśāmbī and Ujjain, among the earliest Indian coinages, show a goddess with elephants. Azilises, an Indo-Scythian ruler of the 1st century BCE, put Gaja Lakṣmī on some of his coins, showing how foreign rulers adopted Indian symbols of royal fortune.

Lakṣmī remained a coin image for centuries, most famously on Gupta gold coins, where she sits on a lotus or a throne.

ABOVE THE DOOR

Lalāṭa-bimba
Lalāṭa-bimbaThe small image on the centre of a temple door lintel
Gaja LakṣmīVery often she is the one, blessing all who enter
KarnatakaCommon on Hoysaḷa and Vijayanagara doorways, and on house doors
ElloraA great Gaja Lakṣmī panel at the Kailāsa temple, 8th century

ZAPPY: Why on the door?

PROF. BOLT: So that everyone who walks in passes under her blessing.

The lalāṭa-bimba, “forehead image”, is the small figure at the centre of a door lintel. In Śiva temples it may be Gaṇeśa, in Viṣṇu temples Garuḍa or Viṣṇu, but Gaja Lakṣmī appears across all kinds of temples and in houses.

In Karnataka, house doors carved with Gaja Lakṣmī are still made for new homes, especially in older towns and villages.

THE ELEPHANTS

Rain, kingship, water
Rain-cloudsElephants are linked with clouds; their bath is rain on the fields
KingsElephants were royal animals; the abhiṣeka bath crowns a king
DirectionsThe eight diggajas hold up the world
WaterFortune comes as a flow, not a hoard

The elephants carry layered meanings. In Indian poetry they are compared to rain-clouds, dark and heavy, so their bath is the monsoon on the fields. They were also the mounts of kings, and the abhiṣeka, a sacred bath, is the core of a royal consecration.

The eight elephants of the directions, the diggajas, support the earth in Purāṇic cosmology. When they bathe Lakṣmī, the whole world honours her.

HER VERSE

In the Aṣṭalakṣmī Stotram
ThemeHer verse pictures her with elephants, horses and chariots, as in the Śrī Sūkta
RoyalIt calls her the giver of a kingdom’s splendour
RefrainIt asks her to protect the singer always
UseSung at house-warmings and door rituals

Her verse draws directly on the Śrī Sūkta’s picture of elephants, horses and chariots. It is a royal verse, about splendour and dignity as much as money.

We describe rather than reproduce it.

HOW SHE IS WORSHIPPED

In home and temple
DoorwaysMany homes carve or paint her above the main door
AbhiṣekaTemple bathing rites echo her elephant bath
VaralakṣmīHer image is common on the kalaśa decorations
ElephantsTemple elephants bless devotees with their trunks

Gaja Lakṣmī is worshipped less as a separate deity than as a blessing image. Painting her over the door at a gṛhapraveśa, house-warming, is a common custom.

In temples with elephants, the elephant’s trunk on a devotee’s head is itself felt as Gaja Lakṣmī’s blessing.

WHAT SHE TEACHES

Fortune flows
Abundance is sharedWater poured on her flows to everyone
Power with graceElephant strength bowing to the lotus
Welcome at the doorBegin every entry with blessing
Ancient and commonOne image for Hindus, Buddhists and Jains alike

The image teaches that fortune is a flow, like water poured from a height, not a heap to be guarded. The water runs off the Goddess and onto the ground, for all.

Its long history across faiths also shows that a good symbol needs no gatekeeper.
