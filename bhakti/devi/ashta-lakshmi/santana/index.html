SANTĀNA LAKṢMĪ

AṢṬA LAKṢMĪ · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Lakṣmī of children and family

FOREWORD

Santāna means offspring, continuity, the line that carries on. Santāna Lakṣmī holds a child on her lap: she is Lakṣmī as the blessing of children, of a healthy family and of generations that continue.

This guide traces her from a Vedic verse about Śrī’s son Kardama to the Varalakṣmī Vratam that women keep across the South, and asks what “family wealth” really means.

AT A GLANCE

Santāna Lakṣmī on one page
NameSantāna Lakṣmī, “Lakṣmī of offspring”
GiftChildren, their health, and a family that continues
FormSix arms: two pots (kalaśa), a sword and a shield; one hand holding a child on her lap, one in fear-not
FeelThe most motherly of the eight
Prayed toBy couples hoping for children, and parents for their children’s welfare

ZAPPY: Why a sword and shield, with a baby?

PROF. BOLT: A mother protects. That's the most fierce love there is.

Santāna Lakṣmī is the form most often prayed to quietly, by couples longing for a child or parents anxious for a sick one. Her image combines tenderness and protection: a baby on her lap, a sword and shield in her hands.

Her pots, kalaśas, are full: fertility, the womb, abundance. Many homes keep her picture in the room of an expectant mother.

KARDAMA, ŚRĪ’S SON

In the Śrī Sūkta
The verse“By Kardama, Śrī has offspring; dwell in me, Kardama”
KardamaCalled Śrī’s son, “mud”, the fertile earth from which life grows
CiklītaAnother son of Śrī in the hymn, asked to bring her home
MeaningEven the Vedic Śrī is a mother

kardamena prajā bhūtā mayi sambhava kardama
śriyaṃ vāsaya me kule mātaraṃ padmamālinīm

Through Kardama she has offspring; Kardama, dwell in me; make Śrī, the Mother wearing a lotus garland, live in my family.

The later verses of the Śrī Sūkta speak to Śrī’s children, Kardama and Ciklīta, asking them to bring their mother to live in the singer’s family. Kardama literally means mud or clay: the fertile wet earth from which crops and life spring.

The Purāṇas also know a sage Kardama, father of nine daughters and of the philosopher Kapila. Whatever the link, the hymn makes Śrī a mother whose presence in a family is its fortune.

VARALAKṢMĪ VRATAM

The family vow
WhenThe Friday before the full moon of Śrāvaṇa (July–August)
WhoMainly married women, for husband, children and home
KalaśaA pot dressed as the Goddess, with a coconut face, sari and jewels
ThreadA sacred yellow thread (dāra, toraṇa) is tied on the wrist
WhereKarnataka, Andhra, Telangana, Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra

ZAPPY: Is that Santāna Lakṣmī?

PROF. BOLT: It's Lakṣmī as Vara, the boon-giver. The boon most asked is the family's welfare.

Varalakṣmī Vratam is one of the most important domestic festivals of the South. Women prepare for days: the house is cleaned, the kalaśa is decorated with a coconut, mango leaves, turmeric and a silver or painted face of the Goddess, and the vrata story is read.

The sacred thread tied on the wrist (in Kannada often called dāra) is a vow and a protection. Neighbours are invited and given turmeric, kumkum and small gifts, a sign of shared good fortune.

CĀRUMATĪ’S STORY

The vrata legend
Śiva to PārvatīThe vrata story is told by Śiva to Pārvatī, in a text ascribed to the Skanda Purāṇa
CārumatīA devoted wife in the town of Kuṇḍina
The dreamLakṣmī appeared and taught her the vrata
BlessingShe and the women who kept it with her were blessed with wealth and family

In the vrata story, Śiva tells Pārvatī of Cārumatī, a woman of Kuṇḍina devoted to her husband and in-laws. Lakṣmī, pleased, appears in a dream and teaches her the vrata. Cārumatī performs it with her neighbours, and all are blessed.

The story makes devotion to family, not wealth itself, the reason for Lakṣmī’s blessing. That is the heart of Santāna Lakṣmī.

SANTĀNA GOPĀLA

Kṛṣṇa as the child-giver
MantraA well-known mantra to Kṛṣṇa as Santāna Gopāla, said by couples wishing for a child
ImageBaby Kṛṣṇa on Yaśodā’s lap: the matching picture
ṢaṣṭhīThe goddess Ṣaṣṭhī and Subrahmaṇya are also prayed to for children
TogetherLakṣmī and Viṣṇu as parents of the world
See: Subrahmaṇya Ṣaṣṭhī

Santāna Gopāla, Kṛṣṇa as giver of children, is a widely used devotional mantra. Couples also pray to Ṣaṣṭhī, the goddess of the sixth day, protector of newborns, and to Subrahmaṇya, especially in Karnataka at Kukke and Ghāṭi.

The image of baby Kṛṣṇa on Yaśodā’s lap matches Santāna Lakṣmī’s: the divine child and the loving mother.

HER VERSE

In the Aṣṭalakṣmī Stotram
ThemeHer verse calls her the mother of the world, giver of children
FeelGentle, a lullaby beside the war-cry of Dhairya Lakṣmī
RefrainIt asks her to protect the singer always
UseSung by expectant parents and at naming ceremonies

Her verse in the Stotram has a soft, rocking rhythm. It is often sung by grandmothers to newborns, and at nāmakaraṇa, naming ceremonies.

We describe it rather than reproduce it.

FAMILY AS WEALTH

What santāna means
ContinuitySantāna also means an unbroken line: the family’s values passed on
HealthChildren’s wellbeing, not only their birth
AdoptionTradition honours adopted and foster children as fully one’s own: Kṛṣṇa raised by Yaśodā
StudentsIn some traditions, disciples are called spiritual offspring

ZAPPY: What about families without children?

PROF. BOLT: Santāna is more than birth. Nephews, students, the young you raise: all are family.

Santāna means more than offspring. It is the continuity of a family: its name, values, skills and memory. A family that passes on good habits, the tradition says, keeps Santāna Lakṣmī.

Indian epics honour adoptive and foster parents warmly: Kṛṣṇa raised by Yaśodā and Nanda, Karṇa by Adhiratha and Rādhā, Sītā found in a furrow by Janaka.

A NOTE ON CARE

Prayer and medicine
FertilityMany couples pray and also seek medical help; the two go together
PregnancyPrayers for a safe birth sit beside good antenatal care
CompassionNo one should be blamed for not having children
Her waySantāna Lakṣmī is a mother, not a judge

Prayers for children have always sat beside practical care. Today that means medical help for infertility and safe pregnancy. Faith and medicine are not rivals here.

A compassionate reading of Santāna Lakṣmī also rejects blaming women, or anyone, for not having children. She is a mother, and mothers comfort.

WHAT SHE TEACHES

The treasure at home
Protect the youngHer sword and shield
Raise wellA family is a line of values
Every childBorn or adopted, a gift
GratitudeCount the people around your table

Santāna Lakṣmī asks us to treat family as wealth: to protect the young fiercely, to raise them with values, and to see every child, born or adopted, as a gift.

The simplest practice is gratitude: to look around the dinner table and count the blessings there.
