AṢṬA LAKṢMĪ · A REFERENCE GUIDE
Eight forms of the Goddess of fortune

FOREWORD

Lakṣmī is the Goddess of fortune, and almost every Indian home has her picture. But fortune has many faces. Grain in the granary, courage in hard times, children in the house, success in work, knowledge in the mind and money in the purse: tradition calls each of these a Lakṣmī.

The Aṣṭa Lakṣmī, “eight Lakṣmīs”, gather them into one set of eight forms. This overview explains who Lakṣmī is, where the eight come from and how they are worshipped. Then eight short guides take each form in turn.

ZAPPY: Eight Lakṣmīs? Isn't there just one?

PROF. BOLT: One Goddess, eight gifts. Each form is one kind of wealth.

The Aṣṭa Lakṣmī are not eight separate goddesses but eight faces of one, each named for a kind of blessing. A family that prays to them is asking not just for money but for food, courage, children, success, learning and the dignity of a good life.

This series takes each in turn: her name, her form and what she holds, where her idea comes from in older texts, her stories, how she is worshipped and what she teaches. Each article stands alone, but together they make one picture of Lakṣmī.

WHO IS LAKṢMĪ?

Śrī, the Goddess of fortune
NamesLakṣmī, Śrī, Kamalā, Padmā, Ramā, Mahālakṣmī, Indirā, Bhārgavī
ConsortOf Viṣṇu in every avatāra: Sītā with Rāma, Rukmiṇī with Kṛṣṇa, Padmāvatī with Veṅkaṭeśvara
FormGolden, on a lotus, holding lotuses, pouring coins, often with elephants
GiftsProsperity, beauty, auspiciousness, royal power and grace
OppositeAlakṣmī or Jyeṣṭhā: misfortune, poverty and quarrel

Lakṣmī is one of the most widely worshipped deities in India: in shops, homes and banks, on wedding invitations and account books. Śrīvaiṣṇava theology gives her a central place as the mediator, puruṣakāra, who pleads with Viṣṇu on behalf of the devotee.

The Viṣṇu Purāṇa says she is as inseparable from Viṣṇu as speech from meaning or light from the sun: wherever he goes, she goes. In each of his avatāras she takes a matching form.

IN THE VEDAS

Śrī before Lakṣmī
EarlyIn the Ṛgveda “śrī” and “lakṣmī” are qualities: splendour and a good sign
Śrī SūktaA hymn attached to the Ṛgveda (a khila) praising Śrī as a golden goddess: the oldest hymn to her
Śatapatha BrāhmaṇaA story of Śrī born from Prajāpati’s tapas, from whom the gods took her gifts
MergingBy the epics, Śrī and Lakṣmī are one goddess, wife of Viṣṇu

In the earliest Vedic verses, lakṣmī means a mark or sign, good or bad, and śrī means radiance, beauty or prosperity. A king was said to have śrī; a field could have it. Only gradually did these become a single goddess.

The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa story is striking: Śrī emerges from Prajāpati, so beautiful that the gods want to kill her and seize her gifts. Prajāpati tells them to take the gifts without harming her, and each god takes one: food, kingship, fame and more. This list of separate gifts is a distant ancestor of the idea of many Lakṣmīs.

FROM THE OCEAN OF MILK

How she came back to the world
The curseThe sage Durvāsā gave Indra a garland; Indra let his elephant trample it, and Durvāsā cursed the gods to lose their fortune
Śrī leavesLakṣmī withdrew; the three worlds grew poor and weak
The churningGods and asuras churned the ocean of milk with Mount Mandara, Vāsuki as the rope, Viṣṇu as the tortoise beneath
She risesLakṣmī emerged on a lotus; the elephants of the quarters bathed her; she chose Viṣṇu
SourceViṣṇu Purāṇa 1.9 and many other Purāṇas

ZAPPY: So she left, and had to be churned back?

PROF. BOLT: Fortune leaves the careless. It took gods and demons together to bring her back.

The churning of the ocean (samudra manthana) is told in the Mahābhārata and many Purāṇas. Fourteen treasures rose from it, including the moon, the wish-cow Kāmadhenu, the horse Uccaiḥśravas, the elephant Airāvata, the poison Hālāhala that Śiva drank, and the nectar of immortality.

Lakṣmī’s emergence is the climax. Seated on a lotus, holding a lotus, she is bathed by the elephants of the directions with water from golden pots: the Gaja Lakṣmī scene. Then she garlands Viṣṇu and takes her place on his chest. The Viṣṇu Purāṇa follows with Indra’s long hymn to Śrī, asking her never to leave again.

WHY EIGHT?

A modern grouping of old ideas
Old giftsEach of the eight gifts is praised in older texts, especially the Śrī Sūkta and the Purāṇas
New setThe set of exactly eight, with names and images, is recent: it spread in the 20th century
The StotramThe Aṣṭalakṣmī Stotram, one verse per form, made the eight popular
TemplesAṣṭalakṣmī temples appeared from the 1970s: Chennai, Hyderabad and elsewhere
VariantsSome lists swap in Aiśvarya, Saubhāgya, Rājya or Vara Lakṣmī

ZAPPY: So it's new?

PROF. BOLT: The gifts are ancient. The neat set of eight is modern. Both are true.

Scholars note that while each of the eight gifts can be found in old texts, the exact set of eight with these names appears to be modern. Its spread is closely linked with the Aṣṭalakṣmī Stotram, now printed in countless prayer books and played in shops each morning.

Silverware, pooja items and decorative kalaśas carrying images of all eight became popular wedding gifts in South India. The grouping thus spread through homes as much as through temples.

THE LISTS DIFFER

Which eight?
Common listĀdi, Dhānya, Dhairya (Vīra), Gaja, Santāna, Vijaya, Vidyā, Dhana
HyderabadIts temple includes Aiśvarya Lakṣmī among the eight
Other formsSaubhāgya (good fortune), Rājya (kingship), Vara (boons), Mahālakṣmī herself
This seriesFollows the common list, as in the Stotram and the Chennai temple

Variant lists show how flexible the idea is. Aiśvarya Lakṣmī, lordship and luxury; Saubhāgya Lakṣmī, marital good fortune; Rājya Lakṣmī, kingship; and Vara Lakṣmī, the giver of boons, all appear in some sets.

The flexibility is old: Indian religion often groups a deity’s forms into neat numbers, the eight Bhairavas, nine Durgās, ten Mahāvidyās, while the members vary by region and school.

THE AṢṬALAKṢMĪ STOTRAM

The hymn behind the eight
FormEight verses in Sanskrit, one for each form, each ending with a refrain asking her to protect “me, always”
OpeningThe first verse addresses Ādi Lakṣmī as worshipped by sages and sweet as Mādhava’s beloved
UseSung in homes on Fridays, at Varalakṣmī Vratam and at Dīpāvalī
AgeA modern composition; its author is not firmly known
Our approachWe describe each verse in its own article; for older hymns to Lakṣmī see the Kanakadhārā Stotram and Mahālakṣmī Aṣṭakam
Older hymns: Kanakadhārā Stotram · Mahālakṣmī Aṣṭakam

Each verse of the Stotram addresses one form, lists her attributes, and asks for protection with a refrain. Its rhythm and easy Sanskrit made it a favourite for children and family singing.

Because it is a recent composition whose rights are unclear, we describe its verses rather than printing them. The far older Kanakadhārā Stotram by Ādi Śaṅkara and the Mahālakṣmī Aṣṭakam from the Padma Purāṇa tradition are both on this site, verse by verse.

THE ŚRĪ SŪKTA

The oldest hymn to her
WhereAn appendix (khila) to the Ṛgveda, 15 verses plus later additions
GoldenIt opens: hiraṇyavarṇāṃ hariṇīṃ, “golden-hued, the doe”
ElephantsIt praises her as delighting in the trumpeting of elephants: the root of Gaja Lakṣmī
HarvestIt calls her rich in cattle and ever-nourished: the root of Dhānya Lakṣmī
AlakṣmīIt asks her to drive away hunger, thirst and misfortune

hiraṇyavarṇāṃ hariṇīṃ suvarṇarajatasrajām
candrāṃ hiraṇmayīṃ lakṣmīṃ jātavedo ma āvaha

Bring to me, O Fire, Lakṣmī: golden, the doe, wearing gold and silver garlands, moon-bright, made of gold.

The Śrī Sūkta is among the most recited Vedic hymns, used in temple worship of Lakṣmī and at the start of many pūjās. Its imagery, gold, lotuses, elephants, cows, fragrance and the lamp, became the standard picture of the Goddess.

Its second half asks for children, cattle, horses and servants, and for Alakṣmī, “hunger, thirst and stain”, to be driven away. In that sense the Śrī Sūkta already contains several of the eight Lakṣmīs, five centuries or more before anyone counted them.

HER SYMBOLS

Lotus, elephants, gold and owl
LotusGrows from mud yet stays clean: wealth without stain
ElephantsRain-clouds and royal power; they bathe her
Gold coinsPouring from her hand: generosity
OwlHer vehicle in Bengal and the north (ulūka): watchful in darkness
KalaśaThe full pot: abundance; worshipped as her form at Varalakṣmī Vratam

The lotus is Lakṣmī’s chief emblem: she is Padmā and Kamalā, “of the lotus”, and is usually seated or standing on one, holding two more. Lotuses grow in muddy water, but their petals stay clean: the ideal of wealth used without corruption.

Her owl vehicle is a northern and eastern idea; in the south she rarely has one. A popular saying makes a moral of it: the owl, who sees in the dark, is also proverbially foolish, a warning that wealth can blind.

HER DAYS

Through the year
FridaysHer weekly day
Varalakṣmī VratamThe Friday before the Śrāvaṇa full moon (July–August); great in Karnataka, Andhra, Telangana and Tamil Nadu
DhanterasTwo days before Dīpāvalī: new gold and vessels are bought
DīpāvalīLakṣmī Pūjā on the new moon of Kārttika (Āśvina in some calendars)
Kojāgarī PūrṇimāBengal and Odisha: the full moon when she asks “who is awake?”

Varalakṣmī Vratam, the great Lakṣmī festival of the South, is kept mainly by married women for the welfare of the family. A kalaśa is decorated as the Goddess with a face, sari and jewels, and the story of Cārumatī, a devoted wife blessed by Lakṣmī in a dream, is read.

In Bengal and Odisha, Lakṣmī Pūjā falls on Kojāgarī Pūrṇimā, the full moon after Durgā Pūjā, when she is said to roam asking “ko jāgarti?”, “who is awake?”, and to bless those who keep vigil.

WHERE THE EIGHT ARE WORSHIPPED

Aṣṭalakṣmī temples
ChennaiThe Aṣṭalakṣmī Kovil at Besant Nagar, by the sea: foundation 1974, completed 1976, conceived by the Kanchi Paramācārya
LayoutShrines on several levels; Dhana Lakṣmī at the top under the śikhara
HyderabadThe Aṣṭalakṣmī temple at Vāsavī Colony, consecrated in April 1996 under the Kanchi Kāmakoṭi Pīṭha
Older Lakṣmī shrinesMahālakṣmī at Kolhāpur, Mumbai’s Mahālakṣmī, Padmāvatī at Tiruchānūr, and the Hoysaḷa Lakṣmīdevī at Doḍḍagaddavalli
HomesThe eight are most often worshipped in a framed picture at home
See also: Mahālakṣmī, Kolhāpur · Kamalā

The Besant Nagar temple, on the beach, is laid out on ascending levels so devotees climb past the eight forms, with a shrine of Mahālakṣmī and Viṣṇu together. Its sea setting fits a goddess born from the ocean.

Karnataka has some of the oldest Lakṣmī temples: the Lakṣmīdevī temple at Doḍḍagaddavalli near Hassan, built in 1113 CE in the early Hoysaḷa style, and the much-visited Mahālakṣmī temple at Goravanahalli near Tumakuru.

WHAT THE EIGHT TEACH

Wealth is more than money
Wealth is pluralFood, courage, children, success, learning and money are all Lakṣmī
BalanceA rich person without courage or knowledge is still poor
She stays whereTradition: Lakṣmī stays with the clean, hard-working, truthful and generous
She leavesWhere there is laziness, quarrel and dirt, Alakṣmī enters

The Aṣṭa Lakṣmī idea is a quiet correction to a narrow view of wealth. Money alone, Dhana Lakṣmī, is one form in eight. The others are harvests, courage, dignity, family, success, learning and the original grace behind them all.

A traditional teaching lists where Lakṣmī lives: in a clean home, among people who work hard, speak truthfully, honour elders and give freely. That is a list anyone can practise.
