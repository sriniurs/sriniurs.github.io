TĀRĀ

DAŚA MAHĀVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The blue star who carries you across

FOREWORD

She looks like Kālī’s younger sister: blue instead of black, short and full-bellied, in a tiger skin, a knife in one hand and a skull-cup in the other, standing on a cremation ground. Her name means “star”, and “she who carries across”.

This is Tārā, the second Great Wisdom. When Śiva drank the world’s poison, she nursed him like a baby. A sage learned her worship from the Buddha. And in a Bengal village by a cremation ground, a “mad saint” called her mother and ate from her plate.

This guide tells her stories and visits her great seat, Tārāpīṭh.

Jai Tārā Mā,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Is she the same as the Buddhist Tārā?

PROF. BOLT: Related, not identical. This guide is about the Hindu Mahāvidyā, with a page on how the two meet.

Part of Daśa Mahāvidyā, the ten Great Wisdoms. Previous: Kālī.

AT A GLANCE

The essential facts on one page
NameTārā: “star”, and “she who carries across” (from tṝ, to cross)
PlaceSecond of the ten Mahāvidyās, closest in look to Kālī
LookBlue; short and big-bellied; tiger skin; knife and skull-cup, sword and lotus; one matted braid; on a cremation ground
FormsUgratārā, Ekajaṭā, Nīlasarasvatī
BhairavaAkṣobhya: Śiva who drank the poison unshaken
Great seatTārāpīṭh, Birbhum, West Bengal, on the Dvārakā river
SaintBāmākhepā (1837–1911), the “mad saint” of Tārāpīṭh
Shared nameTārā is also a great saviour goddess of Buddhism; the two traditions overlap

ZAPPY: A star and a ferry-woman?

PROF. BOLT: Both guide you across the dark. Sailors steered by stars; Tārā steers souls across the ocean of life.

HER NAMES

Star, saviour, blue speech
Tārā“Star”; and tāriṇī, “she who carries across”
Ugratārā“Fierce Tārā”: she saves from terrible (ugra) dangers
Ekajaṭā“One braid”: her hair in a single matted lock
Nīlasarasvatī“Blue Sarasvatī”: giver of speech and learning
Tāriṇī“Saviour”: the name in Bengali devotion
AtharvavedaOne text calls her the form of the Atharva Veda

ZAPPY: Blue Sarasvatī? Isn't Sarasvatī white?

PROF. BOLT: Tārā gives speech too, but the speech that comes out of the dark, out of a crisis. Hence blue.

HER FORM

Like Kālī, but not Kālī
ColourBlue, like the night sky or deep water
BuildShort, with a full belly: she holds the whole world
HandsScissors or knife (kartṛ), skull-cup, sword, blue lotus
DressTiger skin, a garland of heads, snakes as ornaments
HairOne tall matted braid; a small image of Akṣobhya in it
GroundStanding on Śiva on a cremation ground, often with a funeral pyre

ZAPPY: How do I tell her from Kālī?

PROF. BOLT: Blue not black, tiger skin not bare, scissors not sword, short not tall, and that single braid.

AKṢOBHYA AND THE POISON

The mother of Śiva
The poison. When the gods and demons churned the ocean, the deadly hālāhala poison rose first. Śiva drank it to save the worlds, and his throat turned blue.
Unshaken. He swallowed it without being disturbed: so in Tārā’s worship he is Akṣobhya, “the unshaken”, her Bhairava.
The Mother’s milk. Bengal tradition adds that the poison still burned in him, until Tārā took him on her lap like a baby and nursed him, and the burning stopped.
At Tārāpīṭh. The ancient stone image there shows exactly this: Tārā suckling Śiva.

ZAPPY: The Goddess is Śiva's mother here?

PROF. BOLT: At Tārāpīṭh, yes. The fiercest Mahāvidyā turns out to be the gentlest mother.

VASIṢṬHA AND THE BUDDHA

A sage learns from China
No success. The Rudrayāmala tells that the sage Vasiṣṭha worshipped Tārā for ages without result, and grew angry.
Go to Mahācīna. A voice told him to learn the right way from the Buddha (taken in some Hindu schools as an avatāra of Viṣṇu) in “Mahācīna”, a land north of the Himalaya.
Cīnācāra. The Buddha taught him cīnācāra, the “Chinese way” of left-hand (vāmācāra) worship, and pointed him to Tārāpīṭh.
The vision. There Vasiṣṭha worshipped on the cremation ground; Tārā appeared to him nursing Śiva, then became the stone image still worshipped.

ZAPPY: A Hindu sage taught by the Buddha?

PROF. BOLT: That's the legend, and it shows how close the Hindu and Buddhist Tārās once were.

HINDU AND BUDDHIST TĀRĀ

Two rivers, one source?
Buddhist TārāA compassionate saviour, the “mother of all Buddhas”, beloved in Tibet and Nepal: Green Tārā, White Tārā and twenty-one forms
Hindu TārāThe second Mahāvidyā: fiercer, closer to Kālī, a Tantric Goddess of Bengal and Assam
OverlapShared name, shared idea of rescue; Ekajaṭā and Ugratārā appear in both systems
BorrowingScholars think the two grew together in the Buddhist-Śaiva world of eastern India, Oḍḍiyāna and the Himalaya
MahācīnaThe Tantras locate her worship partly in a northern land beyond the mountains

ZAPPY: Same Goddess or different?

PROF. BOLT: Two traditions shaped her side by side. Each sees her its own way; both call her the one who saves.

TĀRĀPĪṬH

A village on the Dvārakā
WhereTārāpīṭh village, near Rampurhat, Birbhum district, West Bengal, on the north-flowing Dvārakā river
NameBengali tārā also means the pupil of the eye: tradition says Satī’s third eye fell here, and the village, once Candīpur, was renamed
Pīṭha statusTemple tradition counts it among the 51; many standard lists of the 51 do not, and call it a Siddha Pīṭha
Holy trioWith Kālīghāṭ and Navadvīp, one of the great tīrthas of Bengali Hindus
AdministrationToday managed under the Tarapith Rampurhat Development Authority
See also: The 51 Śakti Pīṭhas

THE TEMPLE

Two images in one sanctum
Outer imageA large metal image: the fierce face, silver crown, flowing hair, smeared with red sindūr; this is what most devotees see
Inner imageBehind it, the ancient stone image of Tārā nursing Śiva, said to be the one Vasiṣṭha saw
BuildingBengal style with curved roofs; local accounts credit Jagannāth Rāy of Mallārpur; the often-quoted year “1225” is most likely in the Bengali era, not 1225 CE
GateA carving of Durgā and her family over the main gate

THE CREMATION GROUND

Where Tārā lives
MahāśmaśānaThe great cremation ground by the Dvārakā, beside the temple, is part of the shrine
SādhanāFor centuries tāntrikas have meditated here among the pyres
Why thereWhere bodies burn, the illusion that we are only bodies burns too
PañcamuṇḍīSeats of five skulls on which Tantric adepts are said to sit in practice
VisitorsPilgrims see the pyres and the ascetics; most worship at the temple only

ZAPPY: Is it frightening?

PROF. BOLT: For a visitor, maybe. For the tāntrika, it's the most honest place on earth.

BĀMĀKHEPĀ

The mad saint of Tārāpīṭh
BornBāmācaraṇ Caṭṭopādhyāy, 22 February 1837, in a village near Tārāpīṭh
LifeLived on the cremation ground, worshipping Tārā as his mother; ate with dogs, cared nothing for rules
Khepā“Mad”: he would speak to the Goddess aloud, scold her, eat her offerings before she did, and the temple came to accept it
Died18 July 1911; his shrine and āśrama stand by the Dvārakā, near the temple
LegacyHe brought the secret Tārā worship into open, loving devotion

ZAPPY: He ate the Goddess's food first?

PROF. BOLT: The legend says she told the priests: 'he's my son; let him eat'. That's Tārāpīṭh.

WHAT SHE TEACHES

The wisdom of Tārā
Crossing overIn every crisis, a guiding star: call her and she carries you across
SoundAs Nīlasarasvatī, she is the first word that breaks the silence
Fierce motherShe is terrible to danger and tender to her child, even to Śiva
Beyond sectsHindu and Buddhist alike reach for her in trouble
Second wisdomAfter Kālī’s dissolution, Tārā’s rescue: the first light in the dark
