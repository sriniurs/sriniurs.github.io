KĀLĪ

DAŚA MAHĀVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The dark Mother of Time

FOREWORD

She is black as the night sky, her hair loose, her tongue out, a garland of heads round her neck, a sword in her hand, and her foot on Śiva. And millions of people call her, simply, Mā.

Kālī is the first of the ten Great Wisdoms: the Goddess of Time, who ends all things and frees her devotees from fear. She leapt from Durgā’s frown to destroy demons no one else could stop; in Bengal she is worshipped on the darkest night of the year.

This guide tells her stories, reads her image piece by piece, and visits her two great temples in Kolkata.

Jai Mā Kālī,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: How can something so frightening be loved?

PROF. BOLT: Ask any child who's been scolded and then hugged. Her fierceness is for what harms you, never for you.

AT A GLANCE

The essential facts on one page
NameKālī: “the dark one”, and “she who is Time” (kāla)
PlaceFirst of the ten Mahāvidyās; head of the Kālīkula
LookBlack or dark blue; wild hair; tongue out; garland of heads; skirt of arms; sword and severed head; standing on Śiva
First great storyThe Devī Māhātmya (c. 6th c.): born from Durgā’s brow; kills Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa, drinks Raktabīja’s blood
NamesKālikā, Śyāmā, Dakṣiṇa Kālī, Bhadrakālī, Cāmuṇḍā, Bhavatāriṇī
TemplesKālīghāṭ (a Śakti Pīṭha) and Dakṣiṇeśvar, Kolkata; Bhadrakālī shrines in Kerala
FestivalKālī Pūjā, on the new moon of Kārtika, the night of Dīpāvalī in Bengal
Poets and saintsRāmprasād Sen; Śrī Rāmakṛṣṇa

ZAPPY: Why is the first Mahāvidyā the scariest?

PROF. BOLT: She's first because she's the ground: Time eats everything, and the Mother of Time is the start of all ten.

HER NAMES

Time, darkness, mercy
KālīFeminine of kāla: “time”, “death”, and “black”
Mahākālī“Great Kālī”, Goddess of the first chapter of the Devī Māhātmya; consort of Mahākāla
CāmuṇḍāSlayer of Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa
Dakṣiṇa KālīThe Bengal form: “the gracious” or “right-footed” Kālī
Śyāmā“The dark one”: the name in Bengali songs
Bhadrakālī“Auspicious Kālī”: the Kerala form
Bhavatāriṇī“She who carries across the ocean of existence”: Dakṣiṇeśvar’s Kālī

ZAPPY: A goddess called 'auspicious' and 'death' at once?

PROF. BOLT: That's Kālī. What ends one thing makes room for the next.

EARLY ROOTS

Before the Purāṇas
Agni’s tongueThe Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad names Kālī as the first of the seven flickering tongues of fire
Village goddessMany scholars think she began as a local goddess of the hills and forests, later joined to Pārvatī
Battle goddessBy the 6th century she is the wrath of the Goddess in war
TantraLater Tantras make her the supreme Goddess, beyond even Śiva

DEVĪ MĀHĀTMYA · CAṆḌA AND MUṆḌA

Born from a frown
The frown. When the demons Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa came to seize the Goddess Ambikā, her face darkened with anger.
Kālī leaps out. From her furrowed brow sprang Kālī: gaunt, dark, with a garland of skulls and a tiger skin, mouth gaping, tongue lolling.
Cāmuṇḍā. She destroyed the armies, cut off both demon heads and brought them to Ambikā, who named her Cāmuṇḍā.
Read the text: The Devī Māhātmya series

DEVĪ MĀHĀTMYA · RAKTABĪJA

The demon who multiplied
The boon. Raktabīja, “blood-seed”, had a boon: every drop of his blood that touched the ground became a new Raktabīja.
An army of copies. The more the Goddesses wounded him, the more demons filled the field.
Kālī’s answer. Kālī spread her tongue across the battlefield and drank every drop before it fell, swallowing the copies. Bloodless, Raktabīja fell.

ZAPPY: Why does she drink blood?

PROF. BOLT: To stop evil from multiplying. Some troubles grow every time you strike at them; Kālī swallows them whole.

WHY SHE STANDS ON ŚIVA

The tongue between her teeth
The popular story. After the battle, Kālī danced on, drunk with victory, and the earth began to shake. Śiva lay down in her path. When her foot touched him she stopped, and put out her tongue in shame.
The Tantric reading. Śiva below is śava, still consciousness; Kālī above is śakti, the power that moves it. Without her, Śiva is a corpse.
Both are taught. Bengal tells the first; Tantra the second. The image holds both.

READING THE IMAGE

What each sign means
BlackAll colours vanish into black: everything dissolves into her
Garland of headsOften fifty: the fifty letters of Sanskrit, the sound from which the world is made
Skirt of armsThe deeds (karma) she cuts away from her devotee
SwordThe knowledge that cuts the ego
Severed headThe ego, already cut
Fear-not and boon handsIn Dakṣiṇa Kālī her right hands bless and say “do not fear”
NakednessDigambarī, “clothed in the sky”: nothing covers or limits her
Cremation groundWhere all pretence ends; her home

ZAPPY: So the scary parts are actually blessings?

PROF. BOLT: For the devotee, yes. She cuts off exactly what was holding you down.

HER FORMS

Many Kālīs
Dakṣiṇa KālīFour-armed; right foot forward on Śiva; the gracious form of Bengal homes and temples
Śmaśāna KālīThe cremation-ground Kālī of Tantric practice, left foot forward
BhadrakālīIn Kerala the fierce, protective Goddess of the Kāvu groves, slayer of Dārika
MahākālīIn the Devī Māhātmya’s first chapter, Yoganidrā who wakes Viṣṇu; ten-faced, ten-armed in some images
Ādyā Kālī“Primordial Kālī”, source of all
Rakṣā KālīWorshipped to ward off epidemic and disaster
See also: Mahākālī of Ujjain (Śakti Pīṭha 9)

KĀLĪGHĀṬ

Kolkata’s oldest Kālī
PīṭhaOne of the 51 Śakti Pīṭhas: the toes of Satī’s right foot; among the four Ādi Pīṭhas in another list (as the face)
ImageBlack touchstone; three huge eyes; a long golden tongue; four hands of gold
TemplePresent building completed in 1809 under the Sābarṇa Rāy Caudhurī family; older shrines on the site since at least the 15th century
RiverIt stood on the Ādi Gaṅgā, an old channel of the Hooghly, now almost dry
ArtIts bazaar gave birth to Kalighat paintings, bold 19th-century pictures
CityOne theory derives “Kolkata” from Kālīkṣetra, Kālī’s field

DAKṢIṆEŚVAR

Bhavatāriṇī and Rāmakṛṣṇa
FounderRāṇī Rāsmaṇi, a wealthy widow of Kolkata, after a dream of the Goddess on the eve of a pilgrimage to Kāśī
Consecrated31 May 1855, on the east bank of the Hooghly
GoddessBhavatāriṇī, Kālī who carries devotees across saṃsāra
StyleBengal navaratna, “nine jewels”: three storeys and nine spires; twelve Śiva shrines along the river
RāmakṛṣṇaŚrī Rāmakṛṣṇa served here as priest; his visions of the Mother made Dakṣiṇeśvar world-famous, with Śāradā Devī and Vivekānanda

RĀMPRASĀD AND ŚYĀMĀ SAṄGĪT

Songs to a dark mother
Rāmprasād Sen18th-century poet of Bengal, who sang to Kālī as a child sings to his mother, sometimes scolding her
Śyāmā saṅgīt“Songs to Śyāmā”: the Bengali devotional song tradition he shaped
KamalākāntaAnother great Śākta poet in the same tradition
The toneIntimate, playful, defiant: the devotee demands the Mother’s attention
RāmakṛṣṇaLoved and sang Rāmprasād’s songs

ZAPPY: People scold Kālī in their songs?

PROF. BOLT: Like a child tugging at his mother's sari. The fiercer the Goddess, the closer her children get.

KĀLĪ PŪJĀ

The darkest night
WhenThe new moon (amāvāsyā) of Kārtika: the night of Dīpāvalī in Bengal, Assam and Odisha
WorshipThrough the night: clay images in homes and paṇḍāls, hibiscus, lamps, sweets
LampsRows of lamps in the darkest night of the year
Other daysPhalahāriṇī Kālī Pūjā in Jyeṣṭha; Raṭanti Kālī Pūjā in Māgha
HibiscusRed jabā flowers are her favourite offering

ZAPPY: Lakṣmī on Dīpāvalī elsewhere, Kālī in Bengal?

PROF. BOLT: Same night, two Goddesses: plenty and freedom. Bengal chose freedom.

WHAT SHE TEACHES

The wisdom of Kālī
TimeEverything that is born is eaten by time; she is that time, and beyond it
FearlessnessFace death and loss, and fear loses its grip
LoveBehind the terror, a mother: devotees call her “Mā” first
FreedomBhavatāriṇī: she frees from the cycle of birth and death
First wisdomThe first Mahāvidyā: dissolve, and only then begin
