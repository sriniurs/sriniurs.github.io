DHŪMĀVATĪ

DAŚA MAHĀVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE
The smoky widow Goddess

FOREWORD

Every other Goddess in this series is young, beautiful or terrible in her power. The seventh Great Wisdom, Dhūmāvatī, is old. She is a grey, trembling widow on a horseless chariot, a crow on her banner, a winnowing basket in her hand, forever hungry.

The Tantra says she became a widow by swallowing Śiva himself. She is kin to the old goddesses of misfortune, and householders keep their distance. Yet in Kāśī she is a protecting grandmother, and at Datiā she shares a shrine with Bagalāmukhī.

This guide tells her stories, her meaning and her temples.

Jai Dhūmāvatī,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Is she unlucky to read about?

PROF. BOLT: No. She is the Goddess who sits with you when things go wrong.

Part of Daśa Mahāvidyā, the ten Great Wisdoms. Previous: Chinnamastā.

AT A GLANCE

The essential facts on one page
NameDhūmāvatī: “the smoky one”
PlaceSeventh of the ten Mahāvidyās
She isThe Goddess as loss, old age and emptiness: what is left when everything else is gone
LookAn old, grey widow in white or soiled clothes; trembling hands; a winnowing basket; a boon-giving gesture
VehicleA horseless chariot with a crow on its banner, or the crow itself
ConsortNone: she is the only widow among the Mahāvidyās
KinLinked with the older goddesses Nirṛti, Jyeṣṭhā and Alakṣmī
TemplesKāśī; the Pītāmbarā Pīṭha at Datiā; Kāmākhyā

ZAPPY: Why would anyone worship misfortune?

PROF. BOLT: You cannot wish it away. Better to know her face, and ask for her blessing too.

HER NAMES

Smoke, widow, elder
DhūmāvatīFrom dhūma, smoke: “she who is made of smoke”
Dhūmrā“The smoke-coloured”
VidhavāThe widow
Jyeṣṭhā“The elder”: a commentator on the Śāradātilaka Tantra identifies her with Jyeṣṭhā
Dhūmāvatī MāHer name at Kāśī, as a protective mother

ZAPPY: A goddess made of smoke?

PROF. BOLT: Smoke is what remains after the fire. She comes after everything has burned.

HER FORM

The old widow
AgeOld, thin, wrinkled, with grey dishevelled hair
ColourSmoky or dark, “like black clouds at the time of dissolution”
HandsTrembling: one holds a winnowing basket, one gives boons
DressWhite or dirty clothes, the dress of a widow
CrowOn her banner, or as her mount: a carrion bird, a sign of death
MoodAlways hungry, always thirsty, never satisfied

THE GODDESS WHO ATE ŚIVA

The Prāṇatoṣiṇī Tantra story
Hunger. Satī, living with Śiva in the Himālaya, grew terribly hungry and asked him for food. He did not give it.
The swallow. “Then I will eat you,” she said, and swallowed him. Smoke poured from her body.
The curse. Śiva asked to be let out, and she released him. He then rejected her and said she would take the form of a widow: Dhūmāvatī.

ZAPPY: She made herself a widow?

PROF. BOLT: By her own act. Some read it as anger; others as a woman who needs no one.

OTHER TELLINGS

Sources differ
Satī’s pyreSome say she rose from the smoke of Satī’s body burning in Dakṣa’s fire
Durgā’s helperAn oral legend says Durgā created her in the battle with Śumbha and Niśumbha
Kālī’s versionAnother telling has Kālī, not Satī, swallow Śiva
Ten formsWith the other Mahāvidyās, she appeared when Satī blocked Śiva’s path; she stands in the south-east
Late arrivalShe has no known history before the Mahāvidyā lists, which Kinsley places no earlier than the 12th century

HER OLDER SISTERS

Nirṛti, Jyeṣṭhā, Alakṣmī
NirṛtiVedic goddess of decay, need and bad luck; hymns ask her to stay away
Jyeṣṭhā“The elder”, sister of Lakṣmī: dark, ugly, with a crow banner; once widely worshipped in South India
Alakṣmī“Not-Lakṣmī”, the opposite of good fortune
The differenceJyeṣṭhā is married; Dhūmāvatī is always a widow
AlsoLinked with the four rainy months (Cāturmāsya), the inauspicious season

ZAPPY: So Lakṣmī has an ugly elder sister?

PROF. BOLT: In the old stories, yes. In Bengal, Alakṣmī is even sent out of the house before Lakṣmī is welcomed.

THE BASKET AND THE CROW

Reading her symbols
Winnowing basketSeparates grain from husk: the wisdom to tell the real from the passing
CrowA bird that eats the dead: the end that comes to all
Horseless chariotShe goes nowhere, yet she is everywhere
WidowhoodNo partner, no home: the state of the renouncer who has let go
HungerThe emptiness that no worldly thing can fill

ZAPPY: A basket is wisdom?

PROF. BOLT: Shake life hard enough and the husk blows away. What stays is the grain.

WHO WORSHIPS HER

For those outside the household
Ideal devoteesBachelors, widows, renouncers and Tantric practitioners
HouseholdersTraditionally keep their distance; her worship is quiet and private
PlacesCremation grounds, forests and deserts are her haunts
Seventh wisdomAfter Chinnamastā gives herself away, Dhūmāvatī is what is left: emptiness

DHŪMĀVATĪ AT KĀŚĪ

The protective grandmother
KāśīHer temple in Vārāṇasī is among the very few dedicated to her alone
A changeHere she sheds her bad name and becomes a local protector
Who comesEven married couples worship her here, unlike elsewhere

ZAPPY: The scary widow becomes a grandmother?

PROF. BOLT: In Kāśī, yes. Nearness makes even the fierce ones family.

More Kāśī: Tripurabhairavī

THE PĪTĀMBARĀ PĪṬHA, DATIĀ

Two Mahāvidyās under one roof
WhereDatiā, Madhya Pradesh, on a low hill at the edge of the town
FounderA Swāmījī who settled in Datiā in the 1920s and set up the Pītāmbarā Pīṭha of Bagalāmukhī
DhūmāvatīHe also founded a temple of Dhūmāvatī inside the same āśrama
Rare pairTwo Mahāvidyās, Bagalāmukhī and Dhūmāvatī, worshipped in one complex
AlsoThe Vanakhaṇḍeśvara Śiva temple, said to date from the Mahābhārata age

WHAT SHE TEACHES

The wisdom of emptiness
Face lossOld age, grief and want come to everyone; she teaches us to look at them
Let goThe widow and the renouncer have nothing left to lose
DiscernmentWinnow: keep the grain, let the husk fly
Hidden graceHer other hand still gives boons
