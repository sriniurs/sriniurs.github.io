LALITĀ TRIŚATĪ

ŚRĪ LALITĀ TRIŚATĪ STOTRAM · BRAHMĀṆḌA PURĀṆA
Three hundred names of the Goddess · every śloka explained
HOW THIS WORKS: The whole Triśatī in one go for chanting, then every śloka on two pages: first the chant, a picture, the meaning and every name in the key-word table; then a deep dive into the story and ideas behind the names.

WHERE WE ARE

The Lalitā Triśatī, “three hundred names of Lalitā”, is part of the Lalitopākhyāna in the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa. The sage Agastya had already learned the Lalitā Sahasranāma from Hayagrīva and still felt something lacking; Hayagrīva, at the Goddess’s own bidding, then taught him these 300 names.

The names are arranged in fifteen groups of twenty, each group beginning with one syllable of the root mantra of Śrīvidyā. That mantra is given only by a teacher at initiation, and the hymn itself asks that it be kept from the faithless; so we explain every name but do not assemble or explain the mantra. The names themselves are recited openly in temples such as Kāmākṣī at Kāñcī.

There are 59 ślokas, about five names each. A famous commentary on the Triśatī is traditionally ascribed to Ādi Śaṅkara.

ŚLOKA 1 · DEEP DIVE

THE AUSPICIOUS ONE

🔎 In context

The Triśatī belongs to the Lalitopākhyāna, the story of Lalitā in the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, told as a dialogue between Hayagrīva, Viṣṇu’s horse-headed teacher form, and the sage Agastya. Agastya had already received the Lalitā Sahasranāma and still felt something missing; these 300 names are the answer. They fall in fifteen groups of twenty names, each group opening with one syllable of a mantra given at initiation in the Śrīvidyā tradition. We do not assemble or explain that mantra here.

📜 Looking deeper

The first name makes the method plain: the Goddess is the sound itself, not merely praised by it. Kalyāṇa, auspicious, repeats three times, as if the hymn needed to settle the mind in goodness before anything else. The “auspicious mountain” is read as Meru, on whose summit the Śrīpura, Lalitā’s city, stands, or as the Śrīcakra’s raised form. Kalāvatī joins the arts to her: the sixty-four arts and the sixteen phases (kalā) of the moon.

🌱 For today

Begin anything important by fixing the mind on what is good. The rest follows more easily.

ŚLOKA 2 · DEEP DIVE

THE LOTUS-EYED

🔎 In context

The kadamba forest (kadambavana) is a fixed feature of Lalitā’s world. In the Lalitopākhyāna her city sits within rings of walls and groves, and the innermost grove is of kadamba trees. The Madurai Mīnākṣī temple, by tradition, stands where a kadamba forest once grew, and Mīnākṣī is often identified with Lalitā.

📜 Looking deeper

Two names here describe what she does and three describe where and how she is. The sequence is gentle: her eyes, then her mercy, then her home. Kalmaṣa means stain, sin or dullness; kalmaṣaghnī, she who kills it, appears at the very start so the reader is reassured before going deeper. Commentators often note that compassion is called nectar, amṛta, because it heals and does not run out.

🌱 For today

Mercy that does not run out is a good thing to keep in mind when dealing with others too.

↑ ŚLOKA 2 · An ocean of compassion.

ŚLOKA 3 · DEEP DIVE

THE SCIENCE OF KĀMA

🔎 In context

Kandarpa is Kāma, the god of love. In the Lalitopākhyāna, Kāma was burned to ash by Śiva’s third eye; from the ash rose the demon Bhaṇḍa, and the gods prayed to the Goddess. Lalitā appeared from the fire of their sacrifice, destroyed Bhaṇḍa, and with a glance brought Kāma back to life. That is the story behind this name.

📜 Looking deeper

Kandarpavidyā is a technical name: the Śrīvidyā mantra is said to have been first worshipped by Kāma, and one of its traditional forms is called the Kādi vidyā after him. So the Goddess is both the one who revives love and the knowledge love itself worships. The camphor-betel name is pure delight: a queenly detail of scent, as if her presence could be smelled in every direction.

🌱 For today

Love that was destroyed can be restored by grace. Do not give up on it too soon.

↑ ŚLOKA 3 · Her glance revived love.

ŚLOKA 4 · DEEP DIVE

WITNESS OF ACTION

🔎 In context

These names close the first group of twenty. They move from beauty to karma: she watches every act, moves the actor, and returns the result. The ideas echo the Gītā, where the Lord is the witness (upadraṣṭā) and the giver of results, and Kṛṣṇa says he sits in every heart, moving all beings.

📜 Looking deeper

Kali, the present age of the world, is marked by strife and confusion; a deity who removes its faults is especially sought in it. The three karma names together describe the whole cycle: sākṣiṇī, the witness who sees; kārayitrī, the inner mover; and karmaphalapradā, the dispenser of results. Some commentators read “karmādi” as “action, knowledge and devotion”, all of which she witnesses.

🌱 For today

Act as though you are seen, because, the texts say, you are.

ŚLOKA 5 · DEEP DIVE

BEYOND “THIS” AND “THAT”

🔎 In context

A new group of twenty begins, and its names almost all start with eka, “one”: a deliberate play on the syllable that opens the group. The theme moves from her beauty to her nature as the one reality.

📜 Looking deeper

Ekākṣarī, the single syllable, is often read as Om; the “one and many syllables” are all of language, which comes from it. “Not this, not that” echoes the Upaniṣadic neti neti: the highest cannot be pointed at like an object. Ekānandacidākṛti, one bliss and consciousness, is the Vedāntic description of Brahman. Śrīvidyā holds that Lalitā is that Brahman with form, personal and beyond form at once.

🌱 For today

Some things can only be known by becoming quiet, not by pointing.

↑ ŚLOKA 5 · One syllable, all of language.

ŚLOKA 6 · DEEP DIVE

KNOWN BY ONE-POINTED DEVOTION

🔎 In context

This verse gives a short course in how she is reached. Scripture alone cannot capture her; only devotion, concentration and freedom from craving can.

📜 Looking deeper

Āgama means the scriptures, especially the Tantras. To say she cannot be known by them “thus” is not to reject them, but to say that words point and experience arrives. The eṣaṇās, “cravings”, are classically three: for wealth, for children and for fame, named in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad as what renunciants give up. Ekāgra, one-pointed, is Patañjali’s word for the concentrated mind.

🌱 For today

Focus is a form of worship. A scattered mind can read everything and see nothing.

Nandana is Indra’s pleasure garden in heaven, where the wish-granting trees grow. A fresh kalpaka creeper there is the newest, most abundant giver of wishes. The verse brings Vedic ritual, Upaniṣadic wisdom and heavenly abundance together in the syllable.

🌱 For today

A practice is complete only when it gives something back.

ŚLOKA 59 · DEEP DIVE

THE GAṄGĀ AND THE KAUSTUBHA

🔎 In context

The 300th name ends the hymn with bliss. The Gaṅgā flows from the Himalaya, and Pārvatī, the Himalaya’s daughter, is Gaṅgā’s sister; the Kaustubha is the jewel that rose from the churned ocean and is worn by Viṣṇu. Śiva and Viṣṇu images meet in the last verse.

📜 Looking deeper

After the hymn, the uttarapīṭhikā, the closing section, has Hayagrīva explain the worth of the names, link them to the Śrīcakra, and praise their recitation above other hymns. It also says the hymn should not be taught to the faithless. We describe this section but do not reproduce it. The last name, giver of supreme bliss, is the hymn’s promise in one word.

🌱 For today

Let the last word of anything you do be joy.
