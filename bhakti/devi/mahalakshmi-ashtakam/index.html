FOREWORD

"Namaste'stu mahāmāye…" For many of us, these words are among the first Sanskrit we ever learnt, chanted on Fridays, in Navarātri and at Dīpāvalī's Lakṣmī Pūjā. The Mahālakṣmī Aṣṭakam is short, musical and easy to remember. Yet in just eight verses, it travels from Lakṣmī on Her throne to the demon She defeated at Kolhapur, and all the way to the claim that She is the Supreme Reality itself.

This small book opens up each of those verses in simple English: what it says, what its key words mean, and the teaching inside. It is for anyone who has chanted the Aṣṭakam for years without knowing its meaning, and for anyone meeting it for the first time.

May every reader come to see Mahālakṣmī not only as the giver of wealth, but as the Mother who protects, the power who sustains, and the grace that turns plenty into generosity. Śrī Mahālakṣmyai namaḥ.

THE STORY · INDRA'S PRAYER

Why the king of the gods sings to Lakṣmī
The story in 5 frames
The hymn is traditionally said to come from the Padma Purāṇa, and it opens with indra uvāca: "Indra said".
Indra, king of the gods, knows loss well. In the famous story of sage Durvāsa's curse, Lakṣmī withdraws from heaven and the gods lose their glory.
Prosperity returns only when Lakṣmī rises again at the churning of the Ocean of Milk.
Verse 2 names another threat: the demon Kolāsura, whom Mahālakṣmī defeated. Many retellings say Indra sang this hymn in gratitude for that victory.
Ever since, the Aṣṭakam has been a householder's daily prayer for protection, plenty and peace.

The singer

A king who has lost everything before, and knows what grace is worth.

The Goddess

Not only gentle: warrior, protector, and the Supreme Reality itself.

The refrain

Mahālakṣmi namo'stu te: the same bow, eight times.

ZAPPY: Why would the king of heaven need to pray for wealth?

PROF. BOLT: Because he's the one who knows it can vanish overnight. Nobody prays harder than someone who's lost it once.

Indra and Lakṣmī's departure from heaven is a well-known Purāṇic story. The link between this particular hymn and the Kolāsura victory is a popular retelling, not something the hymn itself states.
Prosperity is never permanent property. Even the king of the gods has to bow for it.

ABOUT THE HYMN

Where it comes from, the Kolhapur connection, and how it's used

📜 Source and singer

Traditionally attributed to the Padma Purāṇa, one of the eighteen great Purāṇas, and spoken by Indra. Its simple metre and repeated refrain make it one of the most widely chanted Lakṣmī hymns in India.

📍 The Kolhapur connection

The Karavīra Māhātmya, Kolhapur's sacred history, tells of Mahālakṣmī defeating the demon Kolhāsura there. The city's name is said to come from him. Her temple, Ambābāī, is one of the great Śakti seats of India.

What makes this hymn different. Many Lakṣmī hymns dwell on Her beauty and Her gift of wealth. The Aṣṭakam does that too, but it also calls Her Mahāmāyā, Ādya-śakti, Mahā-raudrā and Parabrahma-svarūpiṇī. It presents Lakṣmī as the Supreme Goddess: the fierce protector, the primal power, and the ultimate Reality itself.

Compared with Kanakadhārā. Śaṅkara's Kanakadhārā Stotram is a poet's meditation on one tender image, Lakṣmī's compassionate glance. Indra's Aṣṭakam is a king's salute, a list of Her powers and names, each ending in the same bow. One is intimate, the other majestic. Together they show both faces of the Mother.

How people use it. Families recite it on Fridays, through Navarātri, during Varamahālakṣmī Vrata and at the Lakṣmī Pūjā of Dīpāvalī. The phalaśruti encourages chanting it once, twice or three times a day, always with devotion.

VERSE 1 · GOING DEEPER

SALUTATIONS, GREAT MĀYĀ

Who is speaking. Most printings open with indra uvāca, "Indra said". The king of the gods is the singer, and that matters. In the Purāṇas Indra loses and regains his heaven again and again. This is the prayer of someone who knows exactly how quickly prosperity can come and go.

Mahāmāyā. Māyā is often translated as "illusion", but here it means the Goddess's creative power: the force through which the one Reality appears as this vast, varied world. Calling Lakṣmī Mahāmāyā lifts Her far above a giver of gold. Śrīpīṭhe adds that She is seated on the throne of Śrī: auspiciousness, beauty, prosperity and grace.

Viṣṇu's weapons in Her hands. The conch (śaṅkha), discus (cakra) and mace (gadā) are Viṣṇu's emblems, yet here She holds them. In this way of seeing, Mahālakṣmī is not standing behind Viṣṇu's power; She is that power. Devotees often read the conch as primal sound, the discus as the wheel of time and order, and the mace as strength. Then comes the half-line repeated in every verse: mahālakṣmi namo'stu te, O Mahālakṣmī, salutations to You.

💡 Why "Aṣṭakam"?

Aṣṭa means eight. An aṣṭakam is a hymn of eight verses, often with a few closing verses on the benefits of reciting it. This one has 8 + 3.

💡 Śrīpīṭha

Literally "the seat of Śrī". Many devotees also hear in it the Goddess's great shrines, above all Kolhapur, revered as one of Her principal seats.

Before asking for anything, Indra names who She really is. Wealth that comes from Her is part of a far larger power, the one that holds the world together. Bowing to Her as Mahāmāyā is a reminder that what we have is held in trust, not owned.

ZAPPY: Conch, discus and mace? Aren't those Viṣṇu's?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. She's holding them. In this hymn She isn't only beside the power; She is the power.

The first verse sets the tone: Lakṣmī here is not just the goddess of money but Mahāmāyā, the great power behind the whole world.

VERSE 2 · GOING DEEPER

RIDER ON GARUḌA, TERROR OF KOLĀSURA

On Garuḍa. Garuḍa, king of birds, is Viṣṇu's carrier: a symbol of speed, strength and fearlessness. Seeing Lakṣmī ride Garuḍa repeats the point of verse 1. She moves with the full power of Viṣṇu, not as a passenger beside Him.

Kolāsura and Kolhapur. The Karavīra Māhātmya, the sacred history of Kolhapur (ancient Karavīra), tells how the demon Kolhāsura grew powerful, tormented gods and people, and was finally defeated there by Mahālakṣmī. Local tradition says his dying wish was that the place should carry his name: Kolhā-pur. Her shrine, the Ambābāī temple, became one of India's great centres of Goddess worship. Many retellings link this verse, and the hymn itself, to the gods' gratitude after that victory.

Remover of sin. The second line turns from the outer demon to the inner ones: sarva-pāpa-harā, She who takes away all sin. As She frees a city from a tormentor, She frees a person from the weight of wrongdoing. In this hymn prosperity always comes paired with purification.

💡 Kiraṇotsav

The Kolhapur temple faces west. On 31 Jan–2 Feb and 9–11 Nov each year, the setting sun's rays travel in and fall on the Goddess: the feet on day one, the chest on day two, the whole image on day three.

💡 Close to Karnataka

Kolhapur lies just across the border from Belagavi, and Ambābāī is deeply revered by devotees from north Karnataka.

Grace has two faces: the soft hand that gives and the strong hand that protects. When something harmful takes hold of a life, a home or a mind, praying to Mahālakṣmī is also asking for the courage and strength to face it.

ZAPPY: Lakṣmī fighting a demon? I thought She just sat on a lotus.

PROF. BOLT: Go to Kolhapur and say that. The whole city is named after the demon She defeated.

Lakṣmī is not only gentle. Grace also protects, and the wicked fear it.

VERSE 3 · GOING DEEPER

ALL-KNOWING, ALL-GIVING

One word, four times. The verse is built on sarva, "all". The repetition is the message. Nothing lies outside Her knowledge, nothing beyond Her giving, no wickedness She cannot stop, no sorrow She cannot reach.

Knowing comes first. Sarvajñā, all-knowing, opens the list. A giver who doesn't understand what you truly need can give the wrong thing. Because She knows everything, Her gifts fit: sometimes what we ask for, sometimes what we actually need.

Protection and healing. Sarva-duṣṭa-bhayaṅkarī picks up the fierce note of verse 2: the wicked fear Her. Sarva-duḥkha-harā ends with comfort: She removes every sorrow. Fear for those who harm, relief for those who suffer: one power, pointed in two directions.

💡 Varadā

Varada means boon-giver. In pictures, Lakṣmī's lowered open palm, the varada mudrā, often has gold coins pouring from it: the boon-giving hand made visible.

💡 Easy to chant

Every verse ends with the same half-line, and most first halves end with an "O You who…" address. That steady pattern is why children learn this hymn so easily.

We tend to pray only for what we think we need. Trusting Her as sarvajñā, the one who knows, frees us from the anxiety of getting every request exactly right. Ask, but leave room for Her wisdom in the answer.

ZAPPY: 'Sarva' four times in two lines?

PROF. BOLT: All-knowing, all-giving, all-guarding, all-healing. Indra isn't leaving any gaps.

Four "alls": She knows everything, gives everything, guards against everything, heals everything.

VERSE 4 · GOING DEEPER

SUCCESS, WISDOM, JOY AND FREEDOM

Two pairs. Siddhi and buddhi: accomplishment, and the understanding to use it well. Success without wisdom can ruin a person; wisdom without success can feel powerless. She gives both together.

Bhukti and mukti. This is the boldest line. Many spiritual paths set enjoyment and liberation against each other. Here they sit side by side: She is bhukti-mukti-pradāyinī, the giver of both. A comfortable home, food on the table and the everyday joys of family, and at the end, freedom from the cycle of birth and death. Her grace covers the whole journey.

The mantra is Her body. Mantra-mūrti means She whose form is the mantra. In the Goddess traditions, the deity is present in Her sacred sound. When you chant this very hymn, the tradition says, you are not just talking about Her; you are touching Her form. Sadā, always, adds that this presence never leaves.

💡 Bhukti-mukti

The pairing is common in Goddess hymns, and it's a big reason Lakṣmī worship is so loved by householders: it blesses ordinary life instead of asking you to leave it.

💡 Siddhi and Buddhi

In Gaṇeśa tradition, Siddhi and Buddhi are also named as Gaṇeśa's consorts. Here the words are used in their plain sense: success and intelligence.

You don't have to choose between living well and growing spiritually. A life lived with gratitude, generosity and clarity can be both comfortable and free. Lakṣmī's grace is meant for the whole of it.

ZAPPY: Enjoyment AND liberation? Don't spiritual people give up enjoyment?

PROF. BOLT: Not in this verse. She gives both: a good life now and freedom at the end.

She gives both: bhukti (a good life) and mukti (freedom). No either-or.

VERSE 5 · GOING DEEPER

WITHOUT BEGINNING OR END

Eternal. Ādyanta-rahitā: free of beginning and end. Stories show Lakṣmī rising from the churning of the ocean, but this verse treats that as an appearance, not a birth. Like the sun at dawn, She becomes visible; She does not begin.

The first power. Ādya-śakti is the primordial energy from which all creation flows, the same idea the Devī Māhātmya expresses when it calls the Goddess the source of everything. Maheśvarī, great sovereign, says She also rules what She creates. Lakṣmī is spoken of here in the highest terms used for the Supreme Goddess.

Yoga. Yogajñā: She knows yoga, the discipline that unites the individual with the divine. Yoga-sambhūtā: She arises from yoga. Her grace meets those who practise steadiness, concentration and inner discipline, and She is the fruit of that practice.

💡 Rising from the ocean

The famous story of Lakṣmī emerging at the churning of the Ocean of Milk is read, in the light of this verse, as the eternal Goddess appearing, not being born.

💡 Two readings

Some printings have yogaje ("born of yoga") in place of yogajñe ("knower of yoga"). Both are common, and both fit.

Wealth comes and goes; its source does not. Remembering that She is beginningless helps us hold our own gains and losses lightly. What truly sustains us was here before them and will be here after.

ZAPPY: No beginning? Then where did She come from?

PROF. BOLT: That's the point. Everything else comes from somewhere. She is where coming-from starts.

Lakṣmī is Ādya Śakti, the first power: eternal, not a goddess who arrived later.

VERSE 6 · GOING DEEPER

SEEN AND UNSEEN, FIERCE AND VAST

Gross and subtle. Sthūla is the physical world: bodies, earth, gold, grain. Sūkṣma is the subtle: thought, feeling, breath, life-force. She is both. Lakṣmī is present not only in money and harvests but in a calm mind and a kind heart.

Mahā-raudrā. The word comes from Rudra, the fierce form of Śiva. Lakṣmī is called supremely fierce: the third time in six verses the hymn reminds us She is not merely gentle. Her ferocity is aimed at whatever harms Her creation.

The vast womb. Mahodarā literally means "great-bellied", an ancient image of the Mother who carries the whole universe inside Her. With mahā-pāpa-harā, remover of great sins, the verse ends on comfort: no wrong is too heavy for the one who carries everything.

💡 Mahā × 5

"Mahā" (great) appears five times in this verse: mahā-raudrā, mahā-śakti, mahodarā, mahā-pāpa and Mahālakṣmī. The sound itself builds a feeling of vastness.

💡 Raudra

From the same root as Rudra. Calling Lakṣmī raudrā links Her to the fierce protective power usually associated with Śiva and Durgā.

Prosperity is not only what you can count. A peaceful mind, good relationships and clear thinking are the subtle wealth this verse points to. Ask Her for both kinds.

ZAPPY: Fierce Lakṣmī again?

PROF. BOLT: Like a mother: soft at home, terrifying to anyone who threatens her child.

She is in everything you can see and everything you can't, and nothing is too heavy for Her to carry.

VERSE 7 · GOING DEEPER

ON THE LOTUS, THE SUPREME ITSELF

The familiar picture. Padmāsana-sthitā: seated on the lotus. This is the image most people know, Lakṣmī on a pink lotus. The lotus grows in mud yet stays clean, the classic symbol of purity in the middle of the world and of a spirit unfolding.

The boldest claim. Parabrahma-svarūpiṇī: She whose own form, whose essence, is Para-brahman, the Supreme Reality the Upaniṣads speak of. Indra is not saying She is close to the Ultimate or serves it. He says She is it. This is the hymn's peak.

Then straight back to tenderness. Right after that grand statement come two warm words: parameśī, supreme Lady, and jaganmātā, Mother of the world. The Ultimate is not cold or distant; it is a Mother. That turn, from the highest philosophy to a child's word for mother, is the heart of devotion.

💡 Brahman

In Vedānta, Brahman is the one unchanging reality behind everything. Para-brahman means the supreme, highest Brahman.

💡 Lotus names

Padmā, Kamalā, Padmāsanā, Padmapriyā: many of Lakṣmī's names mean lotus or lotus-seated. The lotus is Her most constant symbol.

You can reach the highest truth through love, not only through philosophy. The Mother on the lotus is the same reality the sages sought in meditation, and a simple prayer to Her leads to the same place.

ZAPPY: Brahman? That's the big philosophy word.

PROF. BOLT: The biggest. Indra is saying Lakṣmī isn't a part of the Ultimate. She is it.

The peak of the hymn: Lakṣmī is the Supreme Reality itself, and still our Mother.

VERSE 8 · GOING DEEPER

IN WHITE, ADORNED, MOTHER IN THE WORLD

White and jewels. Śvetāmbara-dharā: She wears white, the colour of purity, peace and calm. Nānālaṅkāra-bhūṣitā: She is adorned with many ornaments. Together they say something lovely: purity doesn't mean poverty, and abundance doesn't have to mean greed. In Her the two live in harmony.

In the world. Jagat-sthitā means She abides in, pervades, the world. Having called Her the Supreme Brahman in verse 7, Indra brings Her straight back into creation. She is not only beyond the world but in it: in every home, field and life.

The last word. The final address is again jaganmātā, Mother of the world, the same word that closed verse 7. The eight verses have travelled from Mahāmāyā on Her throne, through warrior, all-giver, eternal power and Supreme Reality, and come home to the simplest name of all: Mother.

💡 Eight verses, one journey

1 power · 2 protector · 3 all-giver · 4 joy and freedom · 5 eternal · 6 seen and unseen · 7 Supreme Reality · 8 Mother in the world.

💡 White for Lakṣmī?

Lakṣmī is usually pictured in red or gold. Picturing Her in white points to Her calm, serene aspect.

However high the hymn climbs, it ends at home. The Goddess of the universe is present in our own world, our family, our work. Seeing Her there turns ordinary life into a place of worship.

ZAPPY: White clothes but lots of jewellery?

PROF. BOLT: Purity and abundance together. Simple at heart, generous in what She gives.

The hymn ends where we live: She is in this world, as its Mother.

PHALAŚRUTI · GOING DEEPER

THE FRUIT OF CHANTING

What a phalaśruti is. Many hymns end with verses describing the phala, the fruit, of reciting them. They are not part of the praise itself but a kind of encouragement, telling the listener why the practice is worth keeping up.

Once, twice, three times. The verses climb like steps. Once a day: mahā-pāpa-vināśana, great sins destroyed. Twice: dhana-dhānya, wealth and grain, a home that does not lack. Three times: mahā-śatru-vināśana, great enemies removed. Traditional teachers often add that the greatest enemies are the inner ones: anger, greed, fear and envy.

The real condition. The very first line sets the terms: yaḥ paṭhed bhaktimān naraḥ, the one who recites with devotion. And the last line names the true reward: that Mahālakṣmī becomes nityaṃ prasannā, ever pleased. Regular recitation is a discipline; the grace itself comes from the relationship.

💡 When families chant it

Fridays, Navarātri and the Lakṣmī Pūjā of Dīpāvalī are favourite times. "Once, twice, three times" is often taken as morning, noon and evening.

💡 The closing line

Most printings end: itīndrakṛtaṃ mahālakṣmyaṣṭakaṃ sampūrṇam, "thus ends the Mahālakṣmī Aṣṭakam composed by Indra."

Kingdoms, wealth and victory are the language of a king like Indra. For most of us the "kingdom" is a steady home, the "grain" is enough to share, and the "enemies" are our own bad habits. Chant daily, and let it change those.

ZAPPY: So three times a day is the premium plan?

PROF. BOLT: Ha. Read the first line again: bhaktimān, with devotion. Without that, counting won't help.

The promise rests on one condition: bhakti. Regularity helps; sincerity matters most.

THE WHOLE HYMN ON ONE PAGE

Eight bows, eight faces of the same Mother
1 · Mahāmāyā. On the throne of Śrī, holding Viṣṇu's conch, discus and mace.
2 · Protector. Rider of Garuḍa, terror of Kolāsura, remover of sin.
3 · All-giver. Knows all, gives all, guards against all, heals all.
4 · Joy and freedom. Success and wisdom, bhukti and mukti; Her body is the mantra.
5 · Eternal. No beginning, no end: the first power, born of yoga.
6 · Seen and unseen. Gross and subtle, fierce, holding the universe within.
7 · The Supreme. On the lotus, Para-brahman itself, and still Mother.
8 · Mother in the world. In white, adorned, present in every home.

🪷 Lotus

Purity in the middle of the world.

🦅 Garuḍa

She moves with Viṣṇu's full power.

⚖️ Bhukti + mukti

A good life and freedom, both.

🙏 Bhakti

The one condition for every fruit.
