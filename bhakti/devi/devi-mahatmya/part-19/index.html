WHERE WE ARE

In Part 18, the king and the merchant worshipped a clay image on a riverbank for three years. She appeared: one received his kingdom, the other knowledge. The 700 verses were complete.

This last part gathers what is read after the text: the Vedic Devī Sūkta the merchant chanted, and the three rahasyas (“secrets”) that Medhas tells the king about Mahākālī, Mahālakṣmī and Mahāsarasvatī.

THE DEVĪ SŪKTA

Ṛgveda 10.125 · the hymn the merchant chanted
The story
The oldest voice. The Devī Sūkta is the 125th hymn of the 10th maṇḍala of the Ṛgveda, eight verses. Tradition names its seer Vāk, daughter of the sage Ambhṛṇa.
“I”. Unusually for the Veda, the hymn is in the first person: the divine power describes herself.
In the story. Chapter 13 says the merchant Samādhi chanted “the supreme Devī Sūkta” on the riverbank.
In practice. Many recite it after the 13 chapters; some also before.
Here. All eight verses for chanting, with four of them explained.

ZAPPY: A Vedic hymn in a Purāṇa series?

PROF. BOLT: The Devī Māhātmya roots itself in the Veda by naming this hymn. Read them together and you see the same Goddess, thousands of years apart: 'I alone', and 'I move with all the gods'.

The Devī Māhātmya’s Vedic root.

VERSE 1 · DEVĪ SŪKTA

“I MOVE WITH THE RUDRAS”
ahaṃ rudrebhirvasubhiścarāmyahamādityairuta viśvadevaiḥ |ahaṃ mitrāvaruṇobhā bibharmyahamindrāgnī ahamaśvinobhā ‖1‖
I move with the Rudras and the Vasus, with the Ādityas and all the gods. I uphold both Mitra and Varuṇa; I uphold Indra and Agni, and both the Aśvins.
WordMeans
ahamI
carāmiI move
bibharmiI uphold, carry
ubhāboth
The oldest hymn of the Goddess. This is Ṛgveda 10.125, the hymn of Vāk, the seer-daughter of the sage Ambhṛṇa. It is the Devī Sūkta the merchant chanted on the riverbank (Part 18), and many recite it after the Devī Māhātmya. Every verse begins with “I”: the Goddess speaks of herself.
The Goddess speaks: “I move with all the gods.”

VERSE 3 · DEVĪ SŪKTA

“I AM THE QUEEN”
ahaṃ rāṣṭrī saṃgamanī vasūnāmcikituṣī prathamā yajñiyānām |tāṃ mā devā vyadadhuḥ purutrābhūristhātrāṃ bhūryāveśayantīm ‖3‖
I am the queen, the gatherer of treasures, the knowing one, first among those worthy of worship. The gods have set me in many places, I who abide in many places and enter into many things.
WordMeans
rāṣṭrīqueen, sovereign
saṃgamanī vasūnāmgatherer of treasures
cikituṣīthe knowing one
purutrāin many places
bhūri-āveśayantīmentering many things
Many places, one queen. “The gods set me in many places”: the seed of the whole Devī Māhātmya idea that she takes many forms, and of “yā devī sarvabhūteṣu”, the Goddess in all beings.
She abides in many places, one queen in all.

VERSE 5 · DEVĪ SŪKTA

“WHOM I LOVE, I MAKE MIGHTY”
ahameva svayamidam vadāmijuṣṭam devebhiruta mānuṣebhiḥ |yaṃ kāmaye taṃ tamugraṃ kṛṇomitaṃ brahmāṇaṃ tamṛṣiṃ taṃ sumedhām ‖5‖
I myself declare this, welcomed by gods and by men: whom I love, I make mighty; him I make a brahmā (a priest), him a seer, him wise.
WordMeans
svayam vadāmiI myself declare
juṣṭamwelcomed, cherished
yaṃ kāmayewhom I love
ugrammighty
sumedhāmwise
Grace. Strength, priesthood, vision and wisdom are her gifts to whomever she loves. Compare the boons of chapter 13: she gave the king his power and the merchant his wisdom.
Strength and wisdom are her gifts.

VERSE 8 · DEVĪ SŪKTA

“I BLOW LIKE THE WIND”
ahameva vāta iva pra vāmyārabhamāṇā bhuvanāni viśvā |paro divā para enā pṛthivyaitāvatī mahinā saṃ babhūva ‖8‖
I blow forth like the wind, laying hold of all the worlds. Beyond the sky, beyond this earth: so great have I become in my greatness.
WordMeans
vāta ivalike the wind
ārabhamāṇātaking hold of
bhuvanāni viśvāall the worlds
paraḥ divābeyond the sky
mahināby greatness
Beyond everything. The hymn ends where the Devī Māhātmya ends: she is in all the worlds, and beyond them. This verse is the Vedic root of “ekaivāhaṃ”, “I alone” (Part 15).
In all the worlds, and beyond them.

SECRET 1 · PRĀDHĀNIKA RAHASYA

The primary secret: where the gods and goddesses come from
The story
Mahālakṣmī first. King Suratha asks Medhas for the “secret”. Medhas: Mahālakṣmī, made of all three guṇas, is first of all, golden, filling the void.
Mahākālī. Seeing the void, she takes a form of pure tamas: Mahākālī, dark, sword and head in hand. Her names include Mahāmāyā, Kālarātri, hunger, thirst and sleep.
Mahāsarasvatī. Then a form of pure sattva, moon-bright, with book and vīṇā: Mahāsarasvatī, Vāk, Bhāratī.
Three pairs. Mahālakṣmī brings forth Brahmā and Lakṣmī; Mahākālī, Rudra (Śiva) and Sarasvatī; Mahāsarasvatī, Viṣṇu and Gaurī.
Three marriages. She gives Sarasvatī to Brahmā, Gaurī to Rudra and Lakṣmī to Viṣṇu. Brahmā creates, Viṣṇu sustains, Rudra dissolves.

ZAPPY: So Lakṣmī is Śiva's sister?

PROF. BOLT: In this secret teaching, yes: each god is born beside another god's consort. It's a puzzle on purpose. The point is that all of them come from the one Mahālakṣmī.

All gods and goddesses come from the one Mahālakṣmī.

SECRET 2 · VAIKṚTIKA RAHASYA

The secret of the forms: how to picture the three
The story
One Goddess, three ways. She is named Śarvā, Caṇḍikā, Durgā, Bhadrā and Bhagavatī, and pictured in three forms, one for each carita.
Mahākālī (first carita, Brahmā’s hymn): ten faces, ten arms, ten feet, dark as collyrium, thirty eyes; sword, arrow, mace, conch, discus, sling, club, bow and a head.
Mahālakṣmī (second carita, Mahiṣa): born of all the gods’ light; a thousand arms, worshipped with eighteen, from rosary and lotus to bell and noose.
Mahāsarasvatī (third carita, Śumbha): born from Gaurī’s body, eight-armed, traditionally holding bell, trident, plough, conch, pestle, discus, bow and arrows.
Worship. The rest of this rahasya describes how the three and their attendants are worshipped.

ZAPPY: Why does each carita have its own Goddess?

PROF. BOLT: The Purāṇa tells three stories; this secret gives each a form to meditate on. That's why each part of this series started by naming the carita's deity.

Kālī, Lakṣmī, Sarasvatī: a form for each story.

SECRET 3 · MŪRTI RAHASYA

The secret of the images: the forms she promised in chapter 11
The story
The promised forms. In chapter 11 (Part 16) the Devī promised to return. This rahasya describes how to picture those forms.
Nandā, born to Nanda: golden, on a golden lotus, holding lotus, goad, noose and conch; she is Lakṣmī, Kamalā, Śrī.
Raktadantikā: red garments, red weapons, red eyes and teeth; terrible, yet caring for her devotee like a loving spouse.
Śākambharī: blue, with eyes like blue lotuses, holding greens and fruits; she is also Śatākṣī and Durgā.
Bhīmā and Bhrāmarī follow: the terrible one of the Himālaya and the Goddess of the bees.

ZAPPY: Do people still worship these forms?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. Nandā Devī in the Himalaya, Śākambharī and Banashankari, Bhrāmarī at Srisailam. The promises of chapter 11 live on as temples.

Her promised forms live on as temples.

ZAPPY: Seven days? Not nine?

PROF. BOLT: Many read the text over the nine nights of Navarātri instead, or all at once on Aṣṭamī. The seven-day split is one popular scheme. The Devī herself says even one devoted reading is enough.

PART 19 ON ONE PAGE

The closing in five lines
The Devī Sūkta. Ṛgveda 10.125: “I move with the Rudras… I am the queen… whom I love I make mighty… I blow like the wind, beyond sky and earth.”
Prādhānika rahasya. Mahālakṣmī is first; from her come Mahākālī and Mahāsarasvatī, then Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Rudra with their consorts.
Vaikṛtika rahasya. How to picture the three: ten-faced Mahākālī, eighteen-armed Mahālakṣmī, eight-armed Mahāsarasvatī.
Mūrti rahasya. The forms promised in chapter 11: Nandā, Raktadantikā, Śākambharī, Bhīmā, Bhrāmarī.
The whole. Three limbs, thirteen chapters, the sūkta and the secrets: one Goddess, in every being, who comes whenever she is remembered.

ZAPPY: Is that the end?

PROF. BOLT: Of this series, yes: all 19 parts. Of the Goddess, never. As she says in chapter 11: whenever trouble arises, I will descend. Go back to Part 1 any Navarātri, and begin again.
