CĀMUṆḌĀ

DEVĪ MĀHĀTMYA · PART 12 · CHAPTER 7
Kālī leaps from the brow of the Goddess · explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: The whole of chapter 7 in full for chanting, the story in comic panels, and the key ślokas on their own pages: the chant, a picture, what it says, the key words, one insight and the one thing to remember.

WHERE WE ARE

In Part 11, the Goddess turned Dhūmralocana to ash with one “huṃ”. Śumbha, lip trembling, sent his two great generals, Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa, with a bigger army.

Chapter 7 gives us the birth of Kālī from the Goddess’s own anger, and her famous name, Cāmuṇḍā. For a Mysuru reader this chapter is close to home: Chamundi Hill is named after her.

ZAPPY: Is Kālī a different goddess?

PROF. BOLT: The text says she leaps out of the Goddess's own forehead. Same Goddess, different face: the anger of a mother whose children are threatened.

THE DARK ONE

Ślokas 7.1–27
The story
The army. Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa march with the fourfold army and find her smiling on a golden peak.
Anger. As they draw bows and swords, her face turns black as ink, and from her forehead leaps Kālī, with sword and noose, skull staff and garland of heads.
The feast. Kālī devours elephants, horses, chariots and warriors; catches weapons in her teeth; swallows Muṇḍa’s discuses like a cloud swallowing suns.
The generals. Laughing, she mounts the lion, beheads Caṇḍa, then Muṇḍa. The rest of the army flees.
The name. She brings the two heads to Caṇḍikā: “Two beasts for the sacrifice of battle.” Caṇḍikā names her Cāmuṇḍā.

ZAPPY: Kālī is so scary. Why do people love her?

PROF. BOLT: Because she is scary only to what harms us. To devotees, Kālī is the fiercest Mother: the one who eats our fears whole. Bengal’s great poets call her simply 'Mā'.

The fiercest form is the Mother’s protection.

ŚLOKAS 7.2–4

THE SMILING GODDESS ON THE GOLDEN PEAK
ājñaptāste tato daityāścaṇḍamuṇḍapurogamāḥ |caturaṅgabalopetā yayurabhyudyatāyudhāḥ ‖2‖dadṛśuste tato devīmīṣaddhāsāṃ vyavasthitām |siṃhasyopari śailendraśṛṅge mahati kāñcane ‖3‖te dṛṣṭvā tāṃ samādātumudyamaṃ cakrurudyatāḥ |ākṛṣṭacāpāsidharāstathānye tatsamīpagāḥ ‖4‖
Then, commanded, the daityas, led by Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa, marched with the fourfold army, weapons raised. They saw the Devī, gently smiling, seated on her lion on a great golden peak of the king of mountains. Seeing her, they made ready to seize her; some drew their bows, others held swords, and came near her.
WordMeans
caturaṅga-balathe fourfold army: chariots, elephants, horses, foot
īṣat-hāsāmgently smiling
śaila-indrathe king of mountains
kāñcanagolden
ākṛṣṭa-cāpawith bows drawn
Still smiling. An army comes to drag her away, and she is smiling. The calm before the storm: in two verses her face will turn black as ink.
An army comes; she smiles.

ŚLOKAS 7.5–6

KĀLĪ IS BORN
tataḥ kopaṃ cakāroccairambikā tānarīnprati |kopena cāsyā vadanaṃ maṣīvarṇamabhūttadā ‖5‖bhrukuṭīkuṭilāttasyā lalāṭaphalakāddrutam |kālī karālavadanā viniṣkrāntāsipāśinī ‖6‖
Then Ambikā grew fiercely angry with those enemies, and with her anger her face became black as ink. From her forehead, twisted with frowning brows, swiftly came forth Kālī, of terrible face, armed with sword and noose.
WordMeans
maṣī-varṇamthe colour of ink
bhrukuṭī-kuṭilatwisted with frowns
lalāṭa-phalakathe plane of the forehead
kālīthe dark one; also “Time”
asi-pāśinībearing sword and noose
The birth of Kālī. This is one of the most famous moments in Hindu scripture. Kālī is not a separate goddess called for help: she is the Goddess’s own anger, leaping out of her brow. The fiercest form comes from the one who was smiling a moment ago.
Kālī is the Goddess’s own fury, made visible.

ŚLOKAS 7.7–8

HOW KĀLĪ LOOKS
vicitrakhaṭvāṅgadharā naramālāvibhūṣaṇā |dvīpicarmaparīdhānā śuṣkamāṃsātibhairavā ‖7‖ativistāravadanā jihvālalanabhīṣaṇā |nimagnāraktanayanā nādāpūritadiṅmukhā ‖8‖
She carried a strange skull-topped staff, wore a garland of human heads, was clad in a tiger’s skin, terrible with her withered flesh; her mouth gaping wide, fearsome with her lolling tongue, her eyes sunken and red, she filled the directions with her roar.
WordMeans
khaṭvāṅgaa skull-topped staff
nara-mālāa garland of human heads
dvīpi-carmana tiger’s (leopard’s) skin
śuṣka-māṃsawithered flesh
jihvā-lalanalolling tongue
nimagna-ārakta-nayanāsunken red eyes
The source of every Kālī image. The skull staff, the garland of heads, the lolling tongue: every later painting and idol of Kālī goes back to these two verses. Her look is meant to terrify the ego, not the devotee.
Every image of Kālī comes from these lines.

ŚLOKAS 7.9–11

SHE SWALLOWS ELEPHANTS
sā vegenābhipatitā ghātayantī mahāsurān |sainye tatra surārīṇāmabhakṣayata tadbalam ‖9‖pārṣṇigrāhāṅkuśagrāhayodhaghaṇṭāsamanvitān |samādāyaikahastena mukhe cikṣepa vāraṇān ‖10‖tathaiva yodhaṃ turagai rathaṃ sārathinā saha |nikṣipya vaktre daśanaiścarvayantyatibhairavam ‖11‖
Rushing in with great speed, slaughtering the great asuras, she devoured that army of the gods’ enemies. Seizing elephants in one hand, with their rear-guards, goad-bearers, warriors and bells, she threw them into her mouth. Likewise the warrior with his horses, the chariot with its charioteer, she put into her mouth and chewed them most terribly with her teeth.
WordMeans
abhakṣayatadevoured
vāraṇaelephants
aṅkuśa-grāhagoad-bearers, mahouts
carvayantīchewing
ati-bhairavammost terribly
Kālī eats. Her hunger has no end: elephants, horses, chariots, the whole army. Read symbolically, Kālī is Time (kāla) devouring everything; and the power that devours our pride and illusions whole.
Kālī, as Time, devours everything.

ŚLOKAS 7.12–13

WEAPONS IN HER TEETH
ekaṃ jagrāha keśeṣu grīvāyāmatha cāparam |pādenākramya caivānyamurasānyamapothayat ‖12‖tairmuktāni ca śastrāṇi mahāstrāṇi tathāsuraiḥ |mukhena jagrāha ruṣā daśanairmathitānyapi ‖13‖
One she seized by the hair, another by the neck; one she crushed underfoot, another she smashed with her chest. The weapons and great missiles hurled by the asuras she caught in her mouth in fury and crushed with her teeth.
WordMeans
keśeṣuby the hair
grīvāyāmby the neck
pādena ākramyatrampling with her foot
mahā-astragreat missiles
daśanaiḥ mathitānicrushed by her teeth
Poetic justice. The asuras came to drag the Goddess “by the hair”. Kālī seizes them by the hair. Their weapons do not wound her; she eats them.
They came to drag her by the hair; Kālī drags them.

ŚLOKAS 7.16–18

SUNS INTO A CLOUD
kṣaṇena tadbalaṃ sarvamasurāṇāṃ nipātitam |dṛṣṭvā caṇḍo’bhidudrāva tāṃ kālīmatibhīṣaṇām ‖16‖śaravarṣairmahābhīmairbhīmākṣīṃ tāṃ mahāsuraḥ |chādayāmāsa cakraiśca muṇḍaḥ kṣiptaiḥ sahasraśaḥ ‖17‖tāni cakrāṇyanekāni viśamānāni tanmukham |babhuryathā’rkabimbāni subahūni ghanodaram ‖18‖
Seeing the whole asura army fallen in a moment, Caṇḍa rushed at that most terrible Kālī. The great asura covered the terrible-eyed one with showers of fearsome arrows, and Muṇḍa with discuses hurled by the thousand. Those many discuses, entering her mouth, looked like many suns’ discs entering the belly of a cloud.
WordMeans
abhidudrāvarushed at
bhīma-akṣīterrible-eyed
cakradiscus
viśamānānientering
arka-bimbadisc of the sun
ghana-udaramthe belly of a cloud
A great image. Blazing discuses vanish into Kālī’s mouth “like suns into a dark cloud”. Kālī is the dark cloud that swallows all light and all weapons: nothing aimed at her survives.
Their weapons vanish in her like suns in a cloud.

ŚLOKAS 7.19–21

CAṆḌA AND MUṆḌA FALL
tato jahāsātiruṣā bhīmaṃ bhairavanādinī |kālī karālavadanā durdarśadaśanojjvalā ‖19‖utthāya ca mahāsiṃhaṃ devī caṇḍamadhāvata |gṛhītvā cāsya keśeṣu śirastenāsinācchinat ‖20‖atha muṇḍo’bhyadhāvattāṃ dṛṣṭvā caṇḍaṃ nipātitam |tamapyapātayadbhūmau sā khaḍgābhihataṃ ruṣā ‖21‖
Then Kālī, roaring terribly, with terrible face and gleaming fearsome teeth, laughed a fearful laugh in her fury. Mounting her great lion, the Devī rushed at Caṇḍa, seized him by the hair, and cut off his head with her sword. Then Muṇḍa, seeing Caṇḍa fallen, rushed at her; she struck him down too, in fury, with her sword.
WordMeans
jahāsalaughed
bhairava-nādinīroaring terribly
durdarśa-daśanateeth fearful to see
utthāya mahā-siṃhammounting the great lion
khaḍga-abhihatamstruck by the sword
The laugh again. Like the Goddess in chapter 2, Kālī laughs before she strikes. Note that she rides the lion here: Kālī and the Devī are one, and share everything.
Kālī laughs, and the two generals fall.

ŚLOKAS 7.23–24

“TWO GREAT BEASTS FOR YOUR SACRIFICE”
śiraścaṇḍasya kālī ca gṛhītvā muṇḍameva ca |prāha pracaṇḍāṭṭahāsamiśramabhyetya caṇḍikām ‖23‖mayā tavātropahṛtau caṇḍamuṇḍau mahāpaśū |yuddhayajñe svayaṃ śumbhaṃ niśumbhaṃ ca haniṣyasi ‖24‖
Taking the heads of Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa, Kālī came to Caṇḍikā and said, with a fierce, loud laugh: “Here I bring you Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa, two great beasts. In the sacrifice of battle you yourself will slay Śumbha and Niśumbha.”
WordMeans
upahṛtauoffered, brought
mahā-paśūtwo great beasts, sacrificial animals
yuddha-yajñathe sacrifice of battle
svayamyourself
War as a yajña. Kālī calls the battle a sacrifice and the generals its victims. She leaves the final offering, Śumbha and Niśumbha, for the Goddess herself.
Kālī offers the battle as a sacrifice.

ŚLOKAS 7.26–27

THE NAME CĀMUṆḌĀ
tāvānītau tato dṛṣṭvā caṇḍamuṇḍau mahāsurau |uvāca kālīṃ kalyāṇī lalitaṃ caṇḍikā vacaḥ ‖26‖yasmāccaṇḍaṃ ca muṇḍaṃ ca gṛhītvā tvamupāgatā |cāmuṇḍeti tato loke khyātā devī bhaviṣyasi ‖27‖
Seeing the two great asuras Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa brought before her, the gracious Caṇḍikā spoke to Kālī these gentle words: “Because you have come bringing Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa, you, O Devī, will be famed in the world as Cāmuṇḍā.”
WordMeans
kalyāṇīthe gracious one
lalitam vacaḥgentle, playful words
cāmuṇḍāCaṇḍa + Muṇḍa: the slayer of both
khyātāfamed
A name is given. From this moment Kālī is Cāmuṇḍā, a name honoured in temples across India (Chamundeshwari on Chamundi Hill in Mysuru among them). Note the tone: Caṇḍikā speaks “gently”, like a mother naming a child.
From this day, Kālī is Cāmuṇḍā.

Chapter 7 in five lines
