MAHIṢĀSURA SLAIN

DEVĪ MĀHĀTMYA · PART 8 · CHAPTER 3
The buffalo, the lion, the man and the elephant · explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: The story in comic panels, and the key ślokas of chapter 3 on their own pages: the chant, a picture, what it says, the key words, one insight and the one thing to remember.

WHERE WE ARE

In Part 7, the light of all the gods became the Goddess. Armed with every god’s weapon, she roared, and Mahiṣa’s armies fell like grass before fire.

Chapter 3 is the duel. First the generals, then Mahiṣāsura himself, changing shape again and again. Its climax is the image of Durgā known everywhere: Mahiṣāsuramardinī, “she who crushes the buffalo demon”.

With the blessings of the Devī,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why is the villain a buffalo?

PROF. BOLT: A buffalo is huge, stubborn and blind in its charge. That is tamas, the heavy dullness in all of us. Watch how she ends it.

THE GENERALS FALL

Ślokas 3.1–20
The story
Cikṣura. Mahiṣa’s general rains arrows on her. She cuts down his horses, charioteer, bow and banner, and when his sword shatters on her arm, her spear destroys him.
Cāmara comes on an elephant. Her lion leaps onto the elephant and tears his head off.
The rest. Udagra and Karāla, Uddhata, Bāṣkala, Tāmra and Andhaka, Ugrāsya, Ugravīrya and Mahāhanu, Biḍāla, Durdhara and Durmukha: all are killed.
Three eyes, one trident. “The three-eyed Supreme Goddess” kills several with her trident; others fall to mace, sword and arrows.
Mahiṣa alone. With his generals gone, the buffalo demon himself comes forward.

ZAPPY: So many names! Do they matter?

PROF. BOLT: Some readers see them as faces of the ego: Ugrāsya 'fierce-mouthed', Durmukha 'foul-mouthed', Durdhara 'hard to hold'. Whatever their names, every one falls before Mahiṣa does.

Every general falls before Mahiṣa does.

ŚLOKAS 3.2–3

CIKṢURA ATTACKS
nihanyamānaṃ tatsainyamavalokya mahāsuraḥ |senānīścikṣuraḥ kopādyayau yoddhumathāmbikām ‖2‖sa devīṃ śaravarṣeṇa vavarṣa samare’suraḥ |yathā merugireḥ śṛṅgaṃ toyavarṣeṇa toyadaḥ ‖3‖
Seeing his army being slaughtered, the great asura Cikṣura, the general, went in anger to fight Ambikā. That asura rained arrows on the Devī in battle, as a cloud rains water on the peak of Mount Meru.
WordMeans
nihanyamānambeing slaughtered
senānīgeneral
śara-varṣaa rain of arrows
meru-gireḥ śṛṅgamthe peak of Mount Meru
toyadaa rain cloud, “water-giver”
Rain on a mountain. The simile says it all: a downpour does not hurt a mountain peak. Chapter 3 is the duel with Mahiṣa himself, and it starts with his generals throwing everything at her, uselessly.
Arrows on her are rain on a mountain.

ŚLOKAS 3.8–10

THE SWORD SHATTERS
tasyāḥ khaḍgo bhujaṃ prāpya paphāla nṛpanandana |tato jagrāha śūlaṃ sa kopādaruṇalocanaḥ ‖8‖cikṣepa ca tatastattu bhadrakālyāṃ mahāsuraḥ |jājvalyamānaṃ tejobhī ravibimbamivāmbarāt ‖9‖dṛṣṭvā tadāpatacchūlaṃ devī śūlamamuñcata |tena tacchatadhā nītaṃ śūlaṃ sa ca mahāsuraḥ ‖10‖
His sword, reaching her arm, shattered, O prince! Then he seized a spear, his eyes red with anger, and the great asura hurled it at Bhadrakālī, blazing with radiance like the sun’s disc falling from the sky. Seeing that spear falling, the Devī let fly her own spear, and by it his spear was broken into a hundred pieces, and the great asura too.
WordMeans
paphālashattered
nṛpa-nandanaO prince!: Medhas speaking to King Suratha
aruṇa-locanared-eyed
bhadrakālīthe auspicious Kālī
śatadhāinto a hundred pieces
“O prince!” Remember who is telling this: the sage Medhas, to the king who lost everything (Part 5). He keeps turning to him. These battles are being told to a man who needs to learn what the Goddess can do.
The sword breaks on her arm.

ŚLOKAS 3.14–16

THE LION FIGHTS
tataḥ siṃhaḥ samutpatya gajakumbhāntare sthitaḥ |bāhuyuddhena yuyudhe tenoccaistridaśāriṇā ‖14‖yudhyamānau tatastau tu tasmānnāgānmahīṃ gatau |yuyudhāte’tisaṃrabdhau prahārairatidāruṇaiḥ ‖15‖tato vegāt khamutpatya nipatya ca mṛgāriṇā |karaprahāreṇa śiraścāmarasya pṛthak kṛtam ‖16‖
Then the lion sprang up and, standing between the elephant’s temples, fought hand to hand with that enemy of the gods, Cāmara. Fighting, the two fell from the elephant to the ground and fought on in fury with terrible blows. Then the lion leapt swiftly into the sky, came down, and with a blow of his paw struck Cāmara’s head from his body.
WordMeans
siṃhathe lion
gaja-kumbhathe elephant’s temples
bāhu-yuddhawrestling, arm-to-arm fight
mṛga-arienemy of beasts: the lion
kara-prahāraa blow of the paw
The vāhana is a warrior. Her lion is not decoration. He leaps, wrestles and kills a general by himself. In temple images the lion often bites a demon’s leg: this is where that comes from.
Her lion fights beside her.

THE SHAPE-SHIFTER

Ślokas 3.21–36
The story
The buffalo. Mahiṣa attacks her troops in his buffalo form, then rushes at her lion. The earth breaks, the ocean floods, mountains fall from the sky.
The noose. She binds him. He drops the buffalo form and becomes a lion.
A man, an elephant. She beheads the lion: a man with a sword appears. She cuts down the man: a great elephant appears and drags her lion. She cuts off its trunk.
Buffalo again. He returns to his buffalo body and shakes the three worlds.
The pause. Caṇḍikā drinks again and again, laughs with red eyes, and grinds his hurled mountains to dust with her arrows.

ZAPPY: Why doesn't she just finish him while he's caught?

PROF. BOLT: The story is showing how slippery he is. Then it shows her calm. She is not chasing; she is waiting, cup in hand, for the moment.

He keeps changing; she stays calm.

ŚLOKAS 3.21–23

MAHIṢA ENTERS
evaṃ saṅkṣīyamāṇe tu svasainye mahiṣāsuraḥ |māhiṣeṇa svarūpeṇa trāsayāmāsa tān gaṇān ‖21‖kāṃścittuṇḍaprahāreṇa khurakṣepaistathāparān |lāṅgūlatāḍitāṃścānyān śṛṅgābhyāṃ ca vidāritān ‖22‖vegena kāṃścidaparānnādena bhramaṇena ca |niḥśvāsapavanenānyānpātayāmāsa bhūtale ‖23‖
As his army was being destroyed, Mahiṣāsura, in his own buffalo form, terrified her troops. Some he struck with his muzzle, others with kicks of his hooves, others he lashed with his tail, and others he tore with his horns. Some he felled with his speed, others with his bellowing and wheeling about, and others with the wind of his breath.
WordMeans
māhiṣa svarūpahis own buffalo form
tuṇḍamuzzle
khurahoof
lāṅgūlatail
śṛṅgahorn
niḥśvāsa-pavanathe wind of his breath
The real form. “His own form” (svarūpa) is the buffalo. In Indian thought the buffalo stands for tamas: heavy, dull, blind force. Every part of him becomes a weapon: horn, hoof, tail, even breath.
Mahiṣa is blind brute force.

ŚLOKAS 3.25–27

THE WORLD SHAKES
so’pi kopānmahāvīryaḥ khurakṣuṇṇamahītalaḥ |śṛṅgābhyāṃ parvatānuccāṃścikṣepa ca nanāda ca ‖25‖vegabhramaṇavikṣuṇṇā mahī tasya vyaśīryata |lāṅgūlenāhataścābdhiḥ plāvayāmāsa sarvataḥ ‖26‖dhutaśṛṅgavibhinnāśca khaṇḍaṃ khaṇḍaṃ yayurghanāḥ |śvāsānilāstāḥ śataśo nipeturnabhaso’calāḥ ‖27‖
He too, mighty, in anger, pounding the earth with his hooves, tossed lofty mountains with his horns and bellowed. Crushed by his speed and wheeling, the earth broke apart; struck by his tail, the ocean flooded everywhere. Torn by his tossing horns, the clouds went to pieces; blown by the wind of his breath, mountains fell from the sky in hundreds.
WordMeans
khura-kṣuṇṇapounded by hooves
vyaśīryatabroke apart
abdhithe ocean
ghanāḥclouds
acalāḥmountains, “the unmoving”
Ego out of control. Mahiṣa’s rage damages the whole world: earth, ocean, sky. This is what unchecked tamas does. The verses build tension before the Goddess answers.
His rage wrecks the world.

ŚLOKAS 3.29–30

BUFFALO, LION, MAN
sā kṣiptvā tasya vai pāśaṃ taṃ babandha mahāsuram |tatyāja māhiṣaṃ rūpaṃ so’pi baddho mahāmṛdhe ‖29‖tataḥ siṃho’bhavatsadyo yāvattasyāmbikā śiraḥ |chinatti tāvat puruṣaḥ khaḍgapāṇiradṛśyata ‖30‖
She threw her noose over him and bound the great asura. Bound in the great battle, he gave up his buffalo form. At once he became a lion; and just as Ambikā was cutting off its head, a man appeared with a sword in his hand.
WordMeans
pāśanoose: Varuṇa’s gift
babandhabound
tatyājagave up
puruṣaḥ khaḍga-pāṇiḥa man, sword in hand
The shape-shifter. Tied down in one form, Mahiṣa slips into another. Ego does this: catch it as anger and it becomes pride; catch it as pride and it becomes cleverness. The chase that follows is famous.
Caught in one form, ego slips into another.

ŚLOKAS 3.31–32

MAN, THEN ELEPHANT
tata evāśu puruṣaṃ devī ciccheda sāyakaiḥ |taṃ khaḍgacarmaṇā sārdhaṃ tataḥ so’bhūnmahāgajaḥ ‖31‖kareṇa ca mahāsiṃhaṃ taṃ cakarṣa jagarja ca |karṣatastu karaṃ devī khaḍgena nirakṛntata ‖32‖
At once the Devī cut down the man with her arrows, together with his sword and shield. Then he became a great elephant. With his trunk he dragged her great lion and trumpeted; but as he dragged, the Devī cut off his trunk with her sword.
WordMeans
sāyakaarrow
khaḍga-carmansword and shield
mahā-gajaa great elephant
karatrunk
nirakṛntatacut off
Four forms in four verses. Buffalo, lion, man, elephant. Each time, she meets the new form at once. She does not chase the shapes; she simply answers each one.
Whatever form it takes, she answers it.

ŚLOKAS 3.33–34

SHE DRINKS AND LAUGHS
tato mahāsuro bhūyo māhiṣaṃ vapurāsthitaḥ |tathaiva kṣobhayāmāsa trailokyaṃ sacarācaram ‖33‖tataḥ kruddhā jaganmātā caṇḍikā pānamuttamam |papau punaḥ punaścaiva jahāsāruṇalocanā ‖34‖
Then the great asura took up his buffalo body again and, as before, shook the three worlds with all that moves and does not move. Then Caṇḍikā, Mother of the world, angered, drank the supreme drink again and again, and laughed, her eyes red.
WordMeans
bhūyaḥagain
sa-cara-acaramwith moving and unmoving beings
jagat-mātāMother of the world
pānam uttamamthe supreme drink
jahāsalaughed
Kubera’s cup. The cup “never empty” given in chapter 2 is used now. Commentators read the drink as divine bliss or intoxication with power, not ordinary wine. She is so sure of victory that she pauses to drink.
In the thick of war she pauses, drinks and laughs.

ŚLOKA 3.38

“ROAR, FOOL, ROAR!”
garja garja kṣaṇaṃ mūḍha madhu yāvatpibāmyaham |mayā tvayi hate’traiva garjiṣyantyāśu devatāḥ ‖38‖
The Devī said: “Roar, roar, fool, for a moment, while I drink this honey. When you are slain by me, right here, the gods will soon be roaring.”
WordMeans
garjaroar!
kṣaṇamfor a moment
mūḍhafool, deluded one
madhuhoney, sweet drink
garjiṣyanti devatāḥthe gods will roar
Her only words in the chapter. This is one of the most quoted lines of the whole text. Mahiṣa’s roar is noise that will last “a moment”. Then the roar will change sides: the gods will be the ones shouting, in joy.
“Roar a moment. Then the gods will roar.”

ŚLOKAS 3.40–41

HER FOOT ON HIS NECK
evamuktvā samutpatya sārūḍhā taṃ mahāsuram |pādenākramya kaṇṭhe ca śūlenainamatāḍayat ‖40‖tataḥ so’pi padākrāntastayā nijamukhāttadā |ardhaniṣkrānta evāsīddevyā vīryeṇa saṃvṛtaḥ ‖41‖
Saying this, she leapt up and mounted the great asura, pressed his neck with her foot, and struck him with her trident. Then he, pressed under her foot, came half out of his own mouth, held fast by the Devī’s power.
WordMeans
samutpatyaleaping up
pādena ākramya kaṇṭhepressing his neck with her foot
śūlatrident
ardha-niṣkrāntaḥhalf come out
saṃvṛtaḥheld fast
The famous image. This is Mahiṣāsuramardinī: her foot on the buffalo, trident in its neck, the demon emerging in human form from the carcass. From Māmallapuram to every Durgā Pūjā pandal, this is the moment carved and painted.
This is Mahiṣāsuramardinī.

ŚLOKAS 3.42–44

MAHIṢA FALLS
ardhaniṣkrānta evāsau yudhyamāno mahāsuraḥ |tayā mahāsinā devyā śiraśchittvā nipātitaḥ ‖42‖tato hāhākṛtaṃ sarvaṃ daityasainyaṃ nanāśa tat |praharṣaṃ ca paraṃ jagmuḥ sakalā devatāgaṇāḥ ‖43‖tuṣṭuvustāṃ surā devīṃ sahadivyairmaharṣibhiḥ |jagurgandharvapatayo nanṛtuścāpsarogaṇāḥ ‖44‖
Still fighting, half come out, the great asura was felled by the Devī, who cut off his head with her great sword. Then the whole asura army, crying “alas!”, perished, and all the hosts of gods went into supreme joy. The gods with the heavenly great sages praised the Devī; the lords of the Gandharvas sang and the Apsarases danced.
WordMeans
mahā-asināwith a great sword
hā-hā-kṛtamcrying “alas!”
praharṣagreat joy
gandharvacelestial musicians
apsarascelestial dancers
Vijayā Daśamī. In the popular tradition, this victory is what Navarātri ends on: the tenth day, Vijayā Daśamī, “the victorious tenth”. The asuras cry “hā hā”; heaven sings and dances.
Mahiṣa falls; heaven sings and dances.

PART 8 ON ONE PAGE

Chapter 3 in five lines
The generals (3.1–20). Cikṣura’s arrows are rain on a mountain; Cāmara falls to the lion; the other generals fall one by one.
The buffalo (3.21–27). Mahiṣa scatters her troops; his rage breaks the earth, floods the ocean, tears the clouds.
The shapes (3.28–33). Bound by her noose, he becomes a lion, a man, an elephant, a buffalo again.
The pause (3.34–38). She drinks and laughs: “Roar, fool, roar for a moment, while I drink.”
The end (3.39–44). Her foot on his neck, he comes half out of his mouth; she beheads him. Heaven sings and dances.
