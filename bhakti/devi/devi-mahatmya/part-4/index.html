Why the Saptaśatī is “locked”, and how it opens · explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: All 14 verses for chanting, then the story of the lock and how it opens. Then one page for each verse or pair: the chant, a picture, what it says, the key words, one insight and the one thing to remember.

WHERE WE ARE

The reader has put on the armour (Kavacam) and lifted the bolt (Argalā). One thing still holds the door: the pin.

The Kīlakam is the strangest of the three limbs. It says the Saptaśatī is so powerful that Śiva himself “pinned” it, so that its full fruit comes only to those who approach it the right way. Its 14 verses are short and cryptic, and teachers have explained them in different ways.

After this, the three limbs are complete, and the story begins.

ZAPPY: A book that's locked? Then how does anyone read it?

PROF. BOLT: Anyone can read the words. The Kīlakam is about the fruit: it opens fully to those who give themselves to the Goddess.

HOW THE LOCK OPENS

The pin, and the key
The idea in 5 frames
Three limbs. Armour (Kavacam) to protect, the bolt (Argalā) lifted, the pin (Kīlakam) removed.
Too powerful. The text says the Saptaśatī alone can accomplish anything, even rites meant to harm (verses 3–4).
Śiva pins it. Seeing the world’s concern, Śiva makes it secret and sets a restraint on it (verses 5–6, 8).
Give and receive. On the 8th or 14th of the dark fortnight, with a collected mind, the devotee “gives and receives” (verses 7–8).
Know, then recite. Understand it first, then recite it clearly every day (verses 9–11).

What does “give and receive” mean? The words are cryptic and readings differ. A common one: offer everything you are and have to the Goddess, and receive it back as her grace. Others read it as an exchange with a teacher, or a ritual offering.

VERSE 1

SALUTATIONS TO ŚIVA
viśuddhajñānadehāyatrivedīdivyacakṣuṣe |śreyaḥprāptinimittāyanamaḥ somārdhadhāriṇe ‖1‖
Mārkaṇḍeya said: Salutations to the one whose body is pure knowledge, whose divine eyes are the three Vedas, who is the means of attaining the highest good, and who wears the half-moon.
We bow to Śiva: made of pure knowledge, seeing through the Vedas, the way to our highest good, the moon in his hair.
WordMeans
viśuddha-jñāna-dehawhose body is pure knowledge
trivedī-divya-cakṣuswhose divine eyes are the three Vedas
śreyaḥ-prāpti-nimittathe cause of attaining the highest good
soma-ardha-dhārinwearer of the half-moon
Why start with Śiva? The Kavacam began with Brahmā, the Argalā with the Goddess. The Kīlakam opens with Śiva, because in this hymn it is Śiva who “pins” the Saptaśatī, and whose grace unlocks it.
The lock-keeper is greeted first: Śiva.

VERSE 2

THE PIN OF ALL MANTRAS
sarvam etad vijānīyānmantrāṇām abhikīlakam |so’pi kṣemam avāpnotisatataṃ japyatatparaḥ ‖2‖
Know all this as the kīlaka, the pin, of the mantras. One who is always devoted to reciting it attains well-being.
This text is the key that holds the power of all its mantras. Whoever recites it faithfully is kept safe.
WordMeans
vijānīyātone should know
abhikīlakamthe pin, the fastening
kṣemamwell-being, safety
japya-tatparaḥdevoted to recitation
What is a kīlaka? A kīla is a peg, wedge or pin. A kīlaka verse “fastens” the power of a mantra: it can lock it, and it can secure it in place. The same word appears in many mantras’ opening dedications (seer, metre, deity, seed, power, kīlaka).
The kīlaka is the pin that fastens a mantra’s power.

VERSES 3–4

IT WORKS BY ITSELF
siddhyanty uccāṭanādīnivastūni sakalāny api |etena stuvatāṃ devīstotramātreṇa siddhyati ‖3‖na mantro nauṣadhaṃ tatrana kiṃcid api vidyate |vinā japyena siddhyetasarvam uccāṭanādikam ‖4‖
All undertakings succeed, even rites such as uccāṭana. For those who praise the Devī with this, she is won by the hymn alone. There, no other mantra, no medicine, nothing else is needed: without any other recitation, every rite succeeds.
WordMeans
siddhyantisucceed
uccāṭana-ādīnirites like uccāṭana, driving away
stotra-mātreṇaby the hymn alone
na mantraḥ na auṣadhamno mantra, no medicine
Too powerful? The text says the Saptaśatī alone accomplishes anything, even uccāṭana and similar rites aimed at others. That is the problem the next verses address: a power open to every purpose, good or harmful.
It needs nothing else, and that is why it was locked.

VERSES 5–6

ŚIVA LOCKS IT
samagrāṇy api siddhyantilokaśaṅkām imāṃ haraḥ |kṛtvā nimantrayāmāsasarvam evam idaṃ śubham ‖5‖stotraṃ vai caṇḍikāyās tutac ca guptaṃ cakāra saḥ |samāptir na ca puṇyasyatāṃ yathāvan niyantraṇām ‖6‖
“Everything succeeds by it”: seeing this concern of the world, Hara (Śiva) ordained that all of it should be for good. He made this hymn of Caṇḍikā secret; for its merit has no end. So he set a proper restraint upon it.
WordMeans
loka-śaṅkāthe world’s concern, fear
haraḥHara: Śiva
guptaṃ cakāramade it hidden, secret
niyantraṇāma restraint, a control
The story of the lock. Tradition reads these verses as a story: people feared what such a power could do, so Śiva “pinned” the text, keeping its full fruit for those who come to it the right way. The verses are terse, and editions differ here.
Śiva set a lock, so the power serves only good.
Some editions read guhyaṃ for guptaṃ and samāpnoti sa puṇyena tāṃ yathāvan nimantraṇām in verse 6.

VERSES 7–8

GIVE, AND RECEIVE
so’pi kṣemam avāpnotisarvam eva na saṃśayaḥ |kṛṣṇāyāṃ vā caturdaśyāmaṣṭamyāṃ vā samāhitaḥ ‖7‖dadāti pratigṛhṇātinānyathaiṣā prasīdati |itthaṃrūpeṇa kīlenamahādevena kīlitam ‖8‖
Such a one too attains every well-being, no doubt: one who, with a collected mind, on the fourteenth or the eighth day of the dark fortnight, gives and receives. Otherwise she is not pleased. In this way, with such a pin, it was pinned by Mahādeva.
WordMeans
kṛṣṇāyāṃ caturdaśyāmon the 14th of the dark fortnight
aṣṭamyāmon the 8th
samāhitaḥwith a collected mind
dadāti pratigṛhṇātigives and receives
The key to the lock. “Gives and receives” is famously cryptic. A common reading: the devotee offers everything to the Goddess, and receives it back as her grace. Surrender is the key. See the page “How the Lock Opens”.
The pin comes out when we give ourselves, and receive her grace.

VERSES 9–10

UNPINNED
yo niṣkīlāṃ vidhāyaināṃnityaṃ japati saṃsphuṭam |sa siddhaḥ sa gaṇaḥ so’pigandharvo jāyate naraḥ ‖9‖na caivāpy aṭatas tasyabhayaṃ kvāpīha jāyate |nāpamṛtyuvaśaṃ yātimṛto mokṣam avāpnuyāt ‖10‖
One who unpins it and recites it every day, clearly, becomes a siddha, one of Śiva’s attendants, a gandharva. Wherever he wanders, no fear arises for him here; he does not fall to untimely death, and at death he attains liberation.
WordMeans
niṣkīlām vidhāyahaving unpinned it
saṃsphuṭamclearly, distinctly
siddhaḥ · gaṇaḥa perfected one · Śiva’s attendant
mokṣam avāpnuyātattains liberation
Clearly, every day. Two conditions: the text is first unpinned, approached rightly, and then recited saṃsphuṭam, “distinctly”. Not mumbled, not rushed. The promise reaches from fearless wandering to liberation.
Unpinned and recited clearly, it frees from fear.

VERSE 11

KNOW, THEN BEGIN
jñātvā prārabhya kurvītana kurvāṇo vinaśyati |tato jñātvaiva sampannamidaṃ prārabhyate budhaiḥ ‖11‖
Having understood, one should begin and perform it. One who does it without understanding comes to ruin. Therefore the wise begin it only once they have understood it fully.
Understand it first, then begin. Rushing in without understanding brings nothing. That is why the wise learn it before they start.
WordMeans
jñātvāhaving understood
prārabhya kurvītashould begin and do it
vinaśyaticomes to ruin
budhaiḥby the wise
Why this series exists. The Kīlakam itself says: know first, then recite. Reading the meaning before chanting is not a luxury; the text asks for it.
Understand, then chant: the Kīlakam insists.

VERSES 12–13

EVEN SOFTLY
saubhāgyādi ca yat kiṃciddṛśyate lalanājane |tat sarvaṃ tatprasādenatena japyam idaṃ śubham ‖12‖śanais tu japyamāne’sminstotre sampattir uccakaiḥ |bhavaty eva samagrāpitataḥ prārabhyam eva tat ‖13‖
Whatever good fortune and the like is seen in women, all of it is by her grace; so this auspicious hymn should be recited. Even when recited softly, this hymn brings great and complete prosperity; therefore it should surely be begun.
WordMeans
saubhāgyagood fortune, happiness in marriage
lalanā-janeamong women
tat-prasādenaby her grace
śanaiḥsoftly, slowly
No need to be loud. Verse 13 says even a soft, slow recitation brings fullness. Verse 12 notes that every grace seen in women’s lives is the Goddess’s own; she is honoured in them.
Even recited softly, it brings fullness.

VERSE 14

WHY WOULD WE NOT PRAISE HER?
aiśvaryaṃ yatprasādenasaubhāgyārogyasampadaḥ |śatruhāniḥ paro mokṣaḥstūyate sā na kiṃ janaiḥ ‖14‖
She by whose grace come sovereignty, good fortune, health and wealth, the end of enemies and the highest liberation: why would people not praise her?
Every blessing, from health and wealth to final freedom, comes by her grace. How could we not praise her?
WordMeans
aiśvaryamsovereignty, greatness
ārogyahealth
śatru-hāniḥthe end of enemies
paraḥ mokṣaḥthe highest liberation
na kimwhy not?
A question to end on. The three limbs close with a question that answers itself. The door is unbolted and unpinned; next the reader turns to the 700 verses themselves.
All blessings are hers: how could we not praise her?

PART 4 ON ONE PAGE

The Kīlakam in five lines
Verse 1. Salutations to Śiva, pure knowledge, with the Vedas for eyes and the moon in his hair.
Verses 2–4. The Saptaśatī is the pin of all mantras; by itself it accomplishes everything.
Verses 5–8. So Śiva made it secret and pinned it; it opens to one who, collected, gives and receives.
Verses 9–11. Unpinned and recited clearly, it frees from fear; but understand it first, then begin.
Verses 12–14. Even softly recited it brings fullness; every blessing is hers. Why would we not praise her?
