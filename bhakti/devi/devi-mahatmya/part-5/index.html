DEVĪ MĀHĀTMYA · PART 5 · CHAPTER 1, THE FRAME
Why can’t we let go? · explained and illustrated
HOW THIS WORKS: The Devī Māhātmya is a story, so this part tells it in comic panels, with the key verses in full on their own pages: the chant, a picture, what it says, the key words, one insight and the one thing to remember.

WHERE WE ARE

The three limbs are done: armour on (Kavacam), bolt lifted (Argalā), pin out (Kīlakam). Now the book itself opens. (Many traditions also chant other preparatory hymns here; practice varies.)

It begins not with a goddess but with two broken men: a king whose ministers stole his kingdom, and a merchant whose family stole his wealth. Both have lost everything. Both still cannot stop caring about what they lost.

Their question, “why can’t I let go?”, is the door into the whole Devī Māhātmya.

A STORY INSIDE A STORY

How the Devī Māhātmya is built

The Devī Māhātmya is chapters 81–93 of the Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa. The sage Mārkaṇḍeya tells how a king became the eighth Manu. Inside his story, the sage Medhas tells the king about the Goddess, in three episodes (caritas), each with a different form of the Devī. This part covers the outer frame, chapter 1 up to śloka 66.

Śloka numbers follow the aurobharati edition (104 ślokas in chapter 1). Editions number them slightly differently.

ŚLOKAS 1.2–3 · THE EIGHTH MANU

HOW A KING BECAME A MANU
sāvarṇiḥ sūryatanayoyo manuḥ kathyate’ṣṭamaḥ |niśāmaya tadutpattiṃvistarād gadato mama ‖2‖mahāmāyānubhāvenayathā manvantarādhipaḥ |sa babhūva mahābhāgaḥsāvarṇis tanayo raveḥ ‖3‖
Mārkaṇḍeya said: Sāvarṇi, son of the Sun, who is called the eighth Manu: hear from me in detail how he came to be; how, by the power of Mahāmāyā, that fortunate one, Sāvarṇi, son of Ravi (the Sun), became the lord of a Manu’s age.
WordMeans
sāvarṇiḥ sūrya-tanayaḥSāvarṇi, son of the Sun
manuḥ aṣṭamaḥthe eighth Manu
mahāmāyā-anubhāvenaby the power of Mahāmāyā
manvantara-adhipaḥruler of a Manu’s age
The answer comes first. The book opens by telling us how it ends: a king will become Sāvarṇi, the eighth Manu, the father of mankind in a future age, and all by the grace of the Goddess. How he gets there is the story.
A ruined king will become a Manu, by her power.

A KING LOSES EVERYTHING

Ślokas 1.4–16
The story
A good king. Long ago, in the age of the Manu Svārociṣa, King Suratha of the Caitra line ruled the whole earth and cared for his people like his own children.
Defeat. Enemy kings attacked and defeated him. He retreated to his own city, reduced to ruling just that.
Betrayal. There, his own powerful, corrupt ministers seized his army and his treasury. He had nothing left.
Into the forest. Alone, on the pretext of hunting, the king rode into a deep forest and came to the hermitage of the sage Medhas, where wild animals lived in peace.
Still worrying. Even there, he could not stop thinking: “Is my city safe? Are my elephants fed? Will those ministers waste my treasure?”

ZAPPY: He lost everything and he's still worrying about the elephants?

PROF. BOLT: That's the point! His kingdom is gone, but his mind is still in the palace. Remember that for śloka 42.

He lost his kingdom; his mind did not leave it.

A MERCHANT THROWN OUT

Ślokas 1.17–41
The story
Another exile. In the forest the king meets a man of the vaiśya (merchant) class, named Samādhi.
Thrown out by his own. His wife and sons, greedy for wealth, had taken his money and driven him from his home.
And yet he worries. “Are my wife and sons well? Are they happy, or in trouble?” He cannot stop loving those who threw him out.
Puzzled. “Why does my mind run towards those who have no love for me?” he asks. “I know their faults, and still my heart goes to them.”
Together to the sage. The two exiles go to Medhas and the king puts their shared question to him.

ZAPPY: Why would he still care about them?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly the question! The king clings to a kingdom, the merchant to a family. Two stories, one puzzle.

Wronged by those he loved, he still loves them.

ŚLOKA 1.42 · THE KING’S QUESTION

WHY CAN’T I LET GO?
mamatvaṃ gatarājyasyarājyāṅgeṣv akhileṣv api |jānato’pi yathājñasyakim etan munisattama ‖42‖
The king said: I have lost my kingdom, and yet I still feel “mine” for every part of it. I know better, yet I am like one who does not know. What is this, O best of sages?
I know it’s gone, and I know clinging is foolish. So why does my heart still say “mine”?
WordMeans
mamatvam“mine-ness”, possessiveness
gata-rājyasyaof one whose kingdom is gone
rājya-aṅgeṣuin the parts of the kingdom
jānataḥ api yathā ajñasyaknowing, yet like one who doesn’t
muni-sattamaO best of sages
The question of the whole book. Knowing and letting go are not the same thing. The king knows his attachment is useless, and it still holds him. The merchant feels the same about the family who threw him out. The Devī Māhātmya is the sage’s answer.
Knowing is not the same as letting go. Why?

THE SAGE ANSWERS

Ślokas 1.43–54
The story
Everyone knows things. Medhas says: all creatures have knowledge of the world. Animals know too; some see by day, some by night.
Look at the birds. Though hungry themselves, birds put grain into the beaks of their young, out of pure attachment.
Look at people. Humans long for children, hoping to be cared for in return. Knowing is not enough to free anyone.
The whirlpool. So beings are thrown into the whirlpool of attachment, the pit of delusion, by the power of Mahāmāyā.
Do not be surprised. She is Viṣṇu’s yoganidrā, the power that keeps the world spinning. And then comes the great verse.

ZAPPY: So even animals have this problem?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. That's why Medhas isn't scolding them. Attachment isn't a personal failing; it's built into the world. Now meet the one who built it.

Attachment is not a flaw; it is the power that turns the world.

ŚLOKA 1.55 · EVEN THE WISE

MAHĀMĀYĀ
jñāninām api cetāṃsidevī bhagavatī hi sā |balād ākṛṣya mohāyamahāmāyā prayacchati ‖55‖
For she, the Devī, the Bhagavatī, Mahāmāyā, forcibly draws even the minds of the wise and casts them into delusion.
The great power of illusion pulls even wise minds into confusion. That is who she is.
WordMeans
jñāninām apieven of the wise
cetāṃsiminds
balāt ākṛṣyadragging by force
mohāya prayacchatihands over to delusion
mahā-māyāthe Great Illusion
The most famous verse of chapter 1. Medhas’s answer is startling: the same Goddess they will learn to worship is the one who binds them. Attachment is not a personal failing to be ashamed of; it is a cosmic power. And so only her grace can release it.
The power that binds even the wise is the Goddess herself.

ŚLOKA 1.56 · CREATOR AND LIBERATOR

PLEASED, SHE FREES US
tayā visṛjyate viśvaṃjagad etac carācaram |saiṣā prasannā varadānṛṇāṃ bhavati muktaye ‖56‖
By her this whole universe, moving and unmoving, is created. And she, when pleased, becomes the giver of boons, for the liberation of human beings.
She makes the whole world, living and non-living. And when she is pleased, she grants us freedom.
WordMeans
tayā visṛjyateby her is created
cara-acarammoving and unmoving
prasannāpleased, gracious
varadāgiver of boons
muktayefor liberation
The same hand. The power that creates the world and binds the mind is the one that frees it. That is why the sage tells them to turn to her: the way out of māyā runs through Mahāmāyā herself.
She who binds, when pleased, sets us free.

ŚLOKAS 1.57–58 · BOTH KNOT AND RELEASE

KNOWLEDGE AND BONDAGE
sā vidyā paramā mukterhetubhūtā sanātanī |saṃsārabandhahetuś casaiva sarveśvareśvarī ‖57–58‖
She is the supreme knowledge, eternal, the cause of liberation; and she alone is the cause of the bondage of worldly life. She is the queen of all queens.
She is the highest wisdom that frees us, and also what ties us to the world. She rules over everything.
WordMeans
vidyā paramāthe supreme knowledge
mukteḥ hetu-bhūtāthe cause of liberation
saṃsāra-bandha-hetuḥthe cause of worldly bondage
sarva-īśvara-īśvarīruler of all rulers
Not two powers, one. Many traditions picture bondage and liberation as enemies. Here both are the Goddess: avidyā that ties, vidyā that frees. The difference is whether she is prasannā, pleased.
One Goddess is both the knot and the release.

ŚLOKA 1.60 · WHO IS SHE?

THE KING ASKS
bhagavan kā hi sā devīmahāmāyeti yāṃ bhavān |bravīti katham utpannāsā karmāsyāś ca kiṃ dvija ‖60‖
The king said: Lord, who is this Devī whom you call Mahāmāyā? How did she arise? And what are her deeds, O brahmin?
Who is she? Where did she come from? What has she done?
WordMeans
kā hi sā devīwho is that Goddess?
katham utpannāhow did she arise?
karma asyāḥ kimwhat are her deeds?
dvijaO twice-born: O brahmin
Three questions, three stories. “Who is she, how did she arise, what has she done?” The rest of the Devī Māhātmya answers in three great episodes: Madhu and Kaiṭabha, Mahiṣāsura, and Śumbha and Niśumbha.
The rest of the book answers one question: who is she?

ŚLOKAS 1.64–66 · ETERNAL, YET SHE COMES

SHE APPEARS FOR THE GODS
nityaiva sā jaganmūrtistayā sarvam idaṃ tatam |tathāpi tatsamutpattirbahudhā śrūyatāṃ mama ‖64–65‖devānāṃ kāryasiddhyarthamāvirbhavati sā yadā |utpanneti tadā lokesā nityāpy abhidhīyate ‖66‖
The sage said: She is eternal; the world is her form; all this is pervaded by her. Even so, hear from me how she arises in many ways. Whenever she manifests to accomplish the purpose of the gods, then, though eternal, she is said in the world to be “born”.
WordMeans
nityāeternal
jagat-mūrtiḥwhose form is the world
āvirbhavatishe manifests
deva-kārya-siddhito fulfil the gods’ purpose
utpannā iti abhidhīyateshe is said to be “born”
Never born, always arriving. She was never created; she is the world. But whenever goodness is in trouble she “appears”, and we call that her birth. Like Kṛṣṇa’s promise in the Gītā (4.7–8), she comes when she is needed. The first such coming is Part 6.
Eternal, she still comes whenever she is needed.

PART 5 ON ONE PAGE

Chapter 1, ślokas 1–66, in five lines
The promise. Mārkaṇḍeya will tell how a king became Sāvarṇi, the eighth Manu, by Mahāmāyā’s power.
Two exiles. King Suratha, robbed by his ministers, and the merchant Samādhi, thrown out by his family, meet at the sage Medhas’s hermitage.
The question. “I know it is gone; why do I still feel it is mine?”
The answer. Mahāmāyā drags even the wise into delusion; she creates the world, binds it, and when pleased, frees it.
Who is she? Eternal, the world is her form; yet she appears whenever the gods need her. Here is the first time.
