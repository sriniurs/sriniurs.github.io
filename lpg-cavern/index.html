Why Is India Hiding Cooking Gas Deep Underground?

Below an LPG terminal in Mangaluru sits a giant chamber carved into rock. Its most surprising safety feature is not a steel wall. It is water.

80,000 tonnesMangaluru's invisible storehouseThe water-curtain trick

ABOUT 8 MINUTES

01

The gas store you cannot see

Imagine standing at an LPG terminal near the Mangaluru coast. You see pipes, machinery and ordinary industrial buildings. What you do not see is the extraordinary part: a vast storage space carved into rock below your feet.

On 13 November 2026, India dedicated its largest underground LPG storage cavern to the nation. Built for Hindustan Petroleum Corporation Limited (HPCL) at Mangaluru, it can hold 80,000 metric tonnes of liquefied petroleum gas (LPG). The stated project cost exceeds ₹850 crore. [1] [2]

A small but important detail: this is storage capacity, not a claim that 80,000 tonnes were sitting underground on inauguration day. HPCL had already reported commissioning the cavern in its 2025–26 annual report; November's ceremony was the formal national dedication. [3]

Why undertake all this excavation when we already know how to build tanks? Because, for large volumes of LPG, the rock itself can do part of the job.

02

First, how does a gas become liquid?

A familiar domestic LPG cylinder holds fuel under pressure. The gas we burn is mainly a mixture of propane and butane. Under suitable pressure, these fuels can be stored as liquids, occupying far less space than the equivalent gas at ordinary atmospheric pressure. When released through the regulator, some of the liquid turns into vapour, which reaches the burner. [4]

That same idea works at a much bigger scale at ports and import terminals. The fuel arrives in bulk, is stored under carefully controlled conditions, and later moves to bottling plants or other users. The giant cavern is not a bottomless underground pocket of household cooking gas: it belongs to an industrial supply chain involving pipelines, pumps, valves, monitoring and safety systems.

Why not merely stack more large tanks on the surface? The question is less about whether surface tanks work—they do—and more about how to store an unusually large volume while using less surface land. [5]

03

The mountain becomes the container

Engineers do not simply find a hole and pour LPG into it. They study the rock, groundwater and fractures, choose a suitable depth, then excavate enormous chambers. The rock surrounding those chambers carries the structural loads. Pipes and engineered seals handle the routes through which fuel enters and leaves. [5]

Mangaluru's project is especially clever because its storage caverns were excavated beneath an existing LPG plant. Engineers India Limited described this as an unusual dual use of the same land: working industrial facilities above, valuable storage space below. [6]

This is not an unprotected hole. Excavation must be followed by tests of rock stability, containment, groundwater behaviour and associated equipment. The company behind the construction reported a cavern-acceptance test conducted in 2025. [7]

But the real surprise is that not every cavern needs a giant steel lining to hold its fuel.

04

The invisible wall made of water

Rock looks solid, but it contains tiny cracks and joints. Gas under pressure could try to move outward through them. Here is the engineering trick: keep the pressure of the water in the surrounding rock higher than the pressure tending to push LPG outward. Then the net direction of seepage is towards the cavern, not away from it. [5] [8]

Engineers help maintain this condition with a water curtain: a network of drilled holes and galleries that keeps the relevant rock saturated and its groundwater pressure controlled. Rather than relying on dry rock to be absolutely crack-free, the design uses water pressure to oppose fuel leakage. Water entering the cavern is collected and managed. [8]

It is a pressure barrier, not a literal waterfall wrapped around the LPG. Monitoring matters continuously: pressure, water levels, operational equipment and emergency systems all remain part of the design. [5]

05

From a ship to a rock chamber

The location near New Mangalore Port is no accident. India imports a substantial quantity of LPG, and a coastal terminal is a practical place to receive large shipments. The officially described project includes a 9.4-kilometre LPG pipeline linking New Mangalore Port to the main terminal. [1]

Picture the route: an LPG carrier arrives at the port; its cargo is transferred through controlled facilities and pipelines; the terminal directs fuel into bulk storage; and, when required, pumps and transfer systems send LPG onward into the distribution network. The exact operating sequence depends on the terminal's design and operating conditions, but the logic is straightforward: receive, buffer and release.

The cavern acts as a buffer. Ships do not arrive in the same neat rhythm in which households use cooking gas. A buffer makes it easier to manage irregular arrivals and keep downstream supply moving. It does not remove the need for ships, functioning ports, roads, bottling plants or cylinders.

06

How big is 80,000 tonnes, really?

There is a simple way to picture the scale. A common domestic LPG cylinder in India contains 14.2 kg of fuel. Divide the cavern's rated capacity of 80,000,000 kg by 14.2 kg and you get roughly 56 lakh cylinder-fills. That is our illustrative arithmetic, not a claim about how many filled cylinders the plant can deliver: product mix, usable inventory, handling losses and bottling logistics all matter. [1] [9]

Now look nationally. CRISIL Intelligence reported LPG consumption of 33.2 million metric tonnes in fiscal 2025–26. At that average annual rate, the cavern's full nameplate capacity is equivalent to about 0.88 day of India's total LPG use, or just under 21 hours. Again, this is our comparison of capacity with average national demand, not an estimate of how long supply could actually last in an emergency. [10]

That number is the unexpected lesson: India's largest LPG cavern is huge by the standards of a single storage facility, yet modest beside a country of India's size. Energy security is built by connecting many pieces, not by constructing one heroic underground room.

07

What happens if imports are delayed?

Imagine a ship arriving later than expected. With spare inventory in the right place, the system has more flexibility to continue supplying downstream users while the next shipment is arranged. That is the practical value of extra storage. Inventory buys time; it does not manufacture fuel or guarantee uninterrupted delivery.

Underground caverns can offer land-use and protection advantages for large volumes, but they also demand demanding geology, specialised construction, groundwater management and rigorous inspection. Indian safety regulations call for monitoring of the hydrogeological system, operations and emergency controls. [5]

A further distinction matters in coastal Karnataka. Mangaluru and nearby Padur also have underground facilities for crude oil. Crude oil is the raw material for refineries; LPG is the finished fuel class used in cooking and many industries. Both can be stored underground, but an LPG cavern and a strategic crude-oil reserve should not be counted as interchangeable tanks. [11]

Even the phrase "strategic storage" needs care: this facility is an HPCL LPG asset, not automatically the same thing as the government's Indian Strategic Petroleum Reserves crude-oil programme.

08

The quiet advantage beneath Mangaluru

At first glance the Mangaluru cavern is a headline about size: 80,000 tonnes and more than ₹850 crore. Look closer and the real achievement is more subtle. Engineers turned the rock beneath a working terminal into storage space, then used carefully controlled groundwater pressure to help contain the fuel.

The project also reveals the limits of the word self-reliance. Building storage at home can make the supply chain more resilient, but it does not mean the country no longer needs imports. The fuel still has to arrive, and the network still has to deliver it.

Next time you see an LPG cylinder in a kitchen, remember the unseen journey behind it: a ship, a port, a pipeline, perhaps a rock chamber, a bottling plant and finally a delivery vehicle. The cylinder is the end of a much larger story.
