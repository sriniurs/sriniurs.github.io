ABOUT 7 MINUTES

01

A queue before sunrise

In January 2026, a video from Bengaluru went viral: a long line of women outside a showroom at 4 in the morning.

Some sat on plastic chairs. Some scrolled their phones. They were not waiting for a new iPhone. They were waiting to buy a Mysore silk saree from the government-owned Karnataka Silk Industries Corporation, KSIC. Entry was by token only, and each person could buy just one saree. [1]

The same scene plays out in Mysuru, outside the factory outlet on Manandavadi Road. [2] Prices start in the tens of thousands of rupees and go up to a few lakh. [1] And still, the racks empty fast.

So what makes one saree worth a pre-dawn alarm? The answer runs through a sultan, a maharaja, a cocoon market, and a thread with real gold in it.

Prof. BoltNot just a saree. A century of Mysuru, folded into six yards.

02

A sultan’s silkworms, a maharaja’s looms

The story begins with Tipu Sultan. In the 1790s, impressed by gifts of silk from foreign rulers, he is said to have sent envoys to Bengal and China to bring back silkworms, and set up sericulture centres in his kingdom. [3]

The industry later struggled. The next big push came from Nalvadi Krishnaraja Wadiyar IV. Inspired by the machine-made silk he saw in Britain, he imported 32 power looms from Switzerland and set up a silk factory in Mysore in 1912. [3] Its first job was royal: fabric for the palace and ornamental cloth for the state’s forces. [4]

The Maharaja’s government even sent samples of Mysore silk to Switzerland in 1931 to test whether more power looms made sense; ten more were installed soon after. [5] More than a century later, the same factory on Manandavadi Road is still where the sarees are made.

03

From a mulberry leaf to your wardrobe

A Mysore silk saree travels further than most people realise before it reaches a showroom.

Farmers grow mulberry, rear silkworms→Cocoons sold at Ramanagara, Sidlaghatta, Kollegal→Boiled & reeled into thread at T. Narasipura→Woven on power looms in Mysuru→Cut, coded, sent to the counter

Ramanagara, on the Bengaluru–Mysuru road, is known as Asia’s largest silk cocoon market, where farmers from across Karnataka and nearby states bring their cocoons to be auctioned. [6] KSIC says it buys about 2,000 kg of cocoons a day, mainly from Sidlaghatta, Ramanagara and Kollegal, at around ₹700 a kilo. [7]

The cocoons are boiled to loosen the silk, reeled into thread at the T. Narasipura unit, and the yarn goes to Mysuru to be woven on dobby and jacquard power looms. [3] [4] The yarn is twisted hard, about 2,200 twists per metre, which gives Mysore silk its soft, grainy crepe feel. [8]

Karnataka is the heart of this: it leads India in mulberry silk, and was the country’s top raw-silk producer in 2023–24. [9]

04

The thread with real gold in it

65% · 0.65%silver and gold in the zari of a KSIC Mysore silk saree

Look at a classic Mysore silk saree and you’ll notice what it doesn’t do. The body is usually plain or lightly patterned, with a solid, contrasting gold border. [10] No heavy brocade, no busy pallu. The drama is in the colour, the drape and the border.

That border is the expensive part. The zari, the metallic thread, is about 65% pure silver with roughly 0.65% gold. [11] In other words, it is real silver thread with a thin coating of real gold, not coloured copper. It is a big reason the saree costs what it does.

THE BODY

100% pure mulberry silk, high-twist crepe, light enough to drape softly.

THE BORDER

Zari of real silver plated with real gold, woven in, not printed.

KSIC has been growing. Its looms went from 189 to 319, and daily output from about 280 sarees in 2021 to about 400 now. But its managing director says it still faces demand for nearly 600 more sarees a day. [7] In the Assembly in March 2026, the minister put current output at 300–350 a day and said whatever is produced sells within a day. [12]

The sales records back that up. Over three years, from 2022–23 to 2024–25, KSIC made 3,10,667 sarees and sold 3,09,659 of them, over 99.6%. [13] In 2024–25 alone, turnover was about ₹332 crore. [3]

That is the queue explained in one line: supply grows slowly, demand grew fast, and a token system decides who gets the day’s stock.

06

Why can’t they just make more?

It looks simple: buy more looms. The real bottleneck is people.

Only KSIC’s own trained artisans weave these sarees. Training a weaver for a simple-border saree takes six to seven months; for complex patterns, about three years. And skilled artisans are retiring faster than new ones can be trained. [3] One report noted the corporation had gone years without recruiting staff. [14]

The raw-material end matters too. In February 2026, about 1,100 employees stopped work for five days in protest against a proposal to use part of the filature factory’s land for a stadium, and stock ran short. [15] The government later dropped the plan and returned the land to KSIC, and in May the filature added two new machines and 30 workers to raise yarn output. [16]

Looms can be bought in months. Weavers take years.

ZappySo the queue is really a queue for weavers?

Prof. BoltExactly. You can’t download a weaver.

07

Real Mysore silk, or just “Mysore-style”?

When something sells out every day, imitations follow. Here is what separates the real thing.

“Mysore Silk” was the first product from Karnataka to get a Geographical Indication tag, [17] and KSIC holds the rights to it; only sarees meeting its standards of 100% mulberry silk and precious-metal zari can lawfully carry the name. [11]

Any silk crepe saree from Mysuru is Mysore silk.

A genuine KSIC Mysore silk saree carries a hologram and a unique code embroidered into the fabric, plus a woven barcode, so it can be traced. [11] [4] Plenty of shops sell beautiful “Mysore crepe” sarees. They can be good buys, but they are not the GI-protected product, and should be priced accordingly. [18]

Three quick checks: buy from a KSIC showroom or its official online store; look for the hologram and stitched code; and be suspicious of a “pure Mysore silk” at a fraction of KSIC’s price. Real silver and gold in the border cannot be cheap.

08

Six yards, a dozen hands

Unfold a Mysore silk saree and you are holding a long chain of people: a farmer in Ramanagara district who grew the mulberry, the silkworms that spun the cocoons, the reelers at T. Narasipura, a weaver on Manandavadi Road who trained for months, and a factory a maharaja started with Swiss looms in 1912.

That is why the queue forms before sunrise. Not because the saree is rare by design, but because it cannot be made faster than the people who make it.

And in a world of fast fashion, that slowness has become the very thing people line up for.
