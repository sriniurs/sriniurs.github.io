MYSURU · ART · A 10-SECOND TEST

Mysore or Tanjore?
Check Your Wall

Two golden painting styles, one family tree that starts at Hampi, and a quick test you can do on the picture in your pooja room.

MysuruThanjavurSpot the difference

ABOUT 7 MINUTES

01

The painting in the pooja room

Almost every South Indian home has one somewhere.

A gold-trimmed Krishna, a Lakshmi in a heavy frame, a Ganesha that catches the light when the evening lamp is lit. Ask whose style it is and the answer comes fast: “That’s a Tanjore.” Very often, it isn’t.

Mysore and Tanjore paintings look like twins at first glance. Both show gods and scenes from the epics. Both use gold. Both have that slightly raised, jewel-like surface. But they grew up in different royal courts, and once you know what to look for, you can tell them apart in about ten seconds.

Prof. BoltAmma may be right. Let’s ask the painting.

02

Two cousins from one fallen empire

The story begins at Hampi. When the Vijayanagara Empire fell after the Battle of Talikota in 1565, its court painters suddenly lost their royal patron. Families of painters, the Chitrakaras, scattered to the empire’s old outposts: Mysore, Tanjore, Surpur and others. [1]

One parent, two homes
Hampi painters · before 1565→Empire falls · 1565→Srirangapatna & Mysore · Thanjavur

MYSORE BRANCH

Raja Wodeyar I (1578–1617) resettled several painter families at Srirangapatna. The style peaked under Krishnaraja Wadiyar III in the 1800s. [1] [2]

TANJORE BRANCH

Flourished in the Maratha court of Thanjavur from the 17th to the 19th century, with King Serfoji II as a major patron. [3]

Same teachers, same gods, same gold. But two centuries in two different courts, with different tastes, gave the two branches different personalities. Tanjore grew bold and glittering. Mysore grew soft-spoken and precise.

03

The 10-second test

Stand in front of the painting, tilt it gently toward the light, and run through these five checks.

CheckLeans TanjoreLeans Mysore
1 · Raised goldThick and high, like sculpted jewelleryLow and fine, more embroidery than ornament
2 · StonesGlass beads or coloured stones set in the goldUsually none
3 · ShineGold that blazesQuiet, matte gold crossed with fine black lines
4 · ColoursDeep, strong reds, greens, bluesSoft, muted, almost pastel
5 · WeightHeavy: painted on a wooden plankLighter: paper or cloth base

Sources: [3] [4] [5] [6]

That wooden plank is where a Tamil nickname comes from: Tanjore paintings are called palagai padam, “plank picture.” [4] And the quiet Mysore shine is deliberate: painters drew black ink lines over the gold to soften it. [5]

One honest warning. The test points you in the right direction; it is not proof. Some Mysore-style paintings use gold paint instead of raised gold, and many modern pieces mix the two styles. [7]

ZappyNo stones. Thin gold. Soft colours. Light as a file…

Prof. BoltThen your Krishna was born near Chamundi Hill, not the Kaveri delta. Welcome home.

04

The raised gold has a recipe

The raised effect is called gesso. The traditional paste was white lead powder, gamboge (a yellow resin) and glue, built up in layers and then covered with gold foil. [4] Today’s Mysore painters typically use zinc oxide and gum Arabic instead. [5]

The same paste explains the two personalities. Pile it thick, set stones into it, and you get Tanjore’s sculpted crowns and arches. Brush it thin and precise and you get Mysore’s delicate necklaces and borders.

How a Tanjore panel is built
Jackfruit or teak plank→Cotton cloth glued on→Chalk & tamarind-seed coat→Raised gesso + stones→Gold foil, then colour

Source: [8]

Mysore painters traditionally started differently: many sheets of paper were pasted together with a flour paste to make a stiff board, and the finest lines were drawn with blades of a special grass. [5] [6] The best-known example of the Mysore school is a royal manuscript, the Sritattvanidhi, a painted encyclopaedia of gods and goddesses. [9]

05

Why real gold never goes dull

Here is a quiet bit of chemistry hanging on your wall. Pure gold barely reacts with air or moisture, which is why a well-made old painting still glows after a century. Cheaper metallic foils and gold paints don’t have that protection; over the years they can dull, darken or turn patchy.

That difference has become a business problem. After the art became fashionable, cheaper imitation gold foil flooded the market, and some sellers make low-cost “Tanjore” pieces decorated with coloured beads and aluminium foil. One Chennai expert recalled buyers asking for a Tanjore painting for as little as ₹200. [8]

THE REAL THING

Real gold foil over hand-built gesso, hand-painted for days or weeks. Old originals can sell for ₹25,000 to ₹2 lakh. [8]

THE ₹200 VERSION

Printed or quick-painted image, aluminium or imitation foil, plastic beads.

06

What the GI tag protects, and what it doesn’t

Both styles have a Geographical Indication, the same kind of legal tag that protects Darjeeling tea or Mysore silk. “Mysore Traditional Paintings” was registered in 2004–05 and “Thanjavur Paintings” in 2007–08. [10]

MYSORE

2004–05

GI application 32 · Karnataka

THANJAVUR

2007–08

GI application 47 · Tamil Nadu

In principle, the tag means only registered makers from the region can use the name, so a Tanjore-style painting made in Jaipur cannot legally be sold as a “Thanjavur Painting.” [11]

In practice, enforcement is weak. A fifth-generation Thanjavur painter who still uses his ancestors’ methods told Mint that, for him, the GI tag has meant little beyond cultural recognition, while cheaper counterfeits fill the market. [12] A label on the name does not put an inspector in every shop.

A GI tag protects a name. It cannot check the gold in a frame.
07

Five questions before you buy one

If you are buying a new piece rather than identifying an old one, a few plain questions separate a painting from a print:

  1. Who painted it, and where? A real maker can tell you.
  2. Is the gold real foil? Ask for it in writing on the bill, with the karat if stated.
  3. Is it hand-painted throughout? Look closely at faces and borders for brush marks rather than printed dots.
  4. What is the base? Wood plank for Tanjore; paper or cloth board for Mysore.
  5. Does the price make sense? Weeks of handwork and real gold cannot cost the same as a framed poster.

None of this needs an expert eye. It needs a few seconds of curiosity, which is exactly what the sellers of the ₹200 version are counting on you not to spend.

08

Two branches, still growing

The Hampi painters who scattered in 1565 could not have guessed that their grandchildren’s grandchildren would still be laying gold leaf on Krishna’s crown in Mysuru and Thanjavur four and a half centuries later.

The work goes on. Mysuru artists such as Raghavendra B B paint in the traditional style, and teachers such as Asha Prasad walk enthusiasts through its features. [13] In May 2026, ₹12 lakh was set aside to restore the murals of two temples in the Mysore Palace complex, with old Palace Library records guiding the work. [14]

So the next time someone points at the golden god on the wall and says “Tanjore,” you can tilt the frame, look for stones, check the shine, and settle the family argument in ten seconds. Either answer leads back to the same place: a fallen empire, and painters who refused to stop painting.
