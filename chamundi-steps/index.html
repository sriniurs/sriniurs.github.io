Who Counted Chamundi’s 1,000 Steps?

A king built them in 1659. Halfway up, a bull was carved out of the hill itself. Three and a half centuries later, nobody quite agrees on how many steps there are, how big the bull is, or which step it sits on.

ABOUT 8 MINUTES

01

The staircase that won’t agree with itself

Ask any Mysorean how many steps lead up Chamundi Hill and you will get a confident answer. Ask three Mysoreans and you may get three.

The state’s temples department, which manages the Chamundeshwari temple, says devotees climb “around one thousand” steps. [1] Encyclopedias and most devotional sites say 1,008. [2] [5] A popular temple-travel guide says 1,001. [4]

That is not as careless as it sounds. Where does the staircase begin: at the arch, or the first carved stone? Does a worn slab that has become part of a landing still count? Is a broad step with two levels one step or two? Change the rule and the total changes.

And 1,008 is not a random number. In Hindu practice 108 and 1,008 are auspicious, so it is easy to see why that figure might stick, whatever the tape measure says. (That last point is our observation.)

02

The king who wanted a staircase

The temple on top is much older than the steps. The original shrine is thought to date to the Hoysala period, around the 12th century, though the record is thin. [3] For centuries, reaching it meant a hard scramble up a granite hill that rises about 3,489 feet above sea level. [1]

The goddess mattered especially to one family. The Wodeyars of Mysore adopted Chamundeshwari as their kula devata, the family deity, in the 1570s. [5] Then, in 1659, Dodda Devaraja Wodeyar came to the throne. He ruled until 1673, and the stone stairway is credited to him, built so that ordinary pilgrims could make the climb. [2] [9]

The tall gopura you see today came much later, added under Krishnaraja Wodeyar III in the 1820s. [9] [2] So when you climb, the steps under your feet are about 165 years older than the tower in front of you.

The legend behind it all is the one every Mysuru child knows: the goddess Chamundi killed the buffalo-demon Mahishasura on this hill, and the city takes its old name, Mahishurapura, from him. [1] [9] That is a legend, told here as a legend.

03

Why every step is yellow and red

Climb on a busy morning and you will notice the steps are not grey. Many devotees dab turmeric and kumkum on each step as they go, often to fulfil a vow. [5] [13] Others break coconuts or light camphor along the way.

That is why counting is hard in practice: you are stepping around offerings, families and the occasional sleeping dog.

The hill is busy. The temple sees an estimated 20 to 25 lakh devotees a year, according to a Hans India report on the temple authorities’ plans. [16] Plenty of them come up by road, but early mornings on the steps belong to walkers, runners and pilgrims, all on the same slabs.

04

Meet Dodda Basava

Partway up, a short detour leads to the hill’s other famous resident: a huge seated Nandi, the bull who carries Shiva, facing a small Shiva shrine. Locals call him Dodda Basava, “the big bull.” [5]

He was not built from blocks or brought up the hill. He was carved out of a single granite boulder on the spot, during Dodda Devaraja’s reign. [6] [3] A photograph of him taken in 1860 is held by the Royal Institute of British Architects, so he was already a sight worth recording more than 165 years ago. [10]

Then the numbers start disagreeing again:

None of these is wildly wrong. A seated animal has no obvious top or end to measure to; does the height include the platform or the hump? And “which step” inherits every problem from episode 1. Our reading: he is taller than a single-storey house and nearly as long as three autorickshaws parked nose to tail, sitting roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of the way up.

05

Carved, not carried

Why carve in place? Because of what granite is like to move. A block big enough to hold this bull would weigh well over a hundred tonnes (our rough estimate), and in the 1600s there was no crane to lift it up a hillside. It is far easier to find the right boulder where it already sits and remove everything that is not a bull.

The hard part is that you cannot undo a cut. Granite is hard and made of interlocking crystals of quartz and feldspar, so shaping it is slow chiselling, not slicing. A mistake in the ear or the bell-chain is permanent, and the carver has to plan the whole animal inside the rock before the first chip comes off.

He has also needed care since. After carving, the statue was rubbed with coconut oil to protect the stone from cracking, a reporter was told in 2017, and centuries of oil abhisheka (ritual pouring) built up a dark coat. [6] That year, the Archaeology Department washed it with high-pressure water, and the famous “black” Nandi came out much lighter. Officials said no chemicals were used, and asked devotees not to bring brightly coloured powders, which they said can damage the stone. [6]

06

The hill is younger than its neighbours

Here is the most surprising number in the story. Chamundi Hill is old, but by the standards of the ground around Mysuru it is the baby of the family.

The grey banded rock under much of the region, the Peninsular Gneiss, includes some of the oldest rock exposed in South India: zircon crystals in gneiss from the Mysuru area have been dated to about 3.4 billion years. [11] The long belt of Closepet granite near Ramanagara pushed up through that crust about 2.5 billion years ago. [12]

Chamundi’s granite came much later. A University of Mysore geologist told Star of Mysore it is among the youngest intrusive rocks in the region, about 700 million years old; a Deccan Herald writer gives about 800 million. [11] [17] “Intrusive” means it rose as molten rock into older rock, cooled slowly underground, and was later uncovered as the softer rock around it wore away.

Put another way: the granite Dodda Basava is cut from had already been sitting there for hundreds of millions of years before the first dinosaurs appeared. The carver had a little under 15 years of a king’s reign to work with.

07

The climb in numbers

A route log kept by runners who time themselves on the steps records about 257 metres (844 feet) of climb from the first step to the temple entrance. [13] That one number unlocks some neat arithmetic.

How tall is a step? Divide 257 metres by 1,008 steps and you get about 25 cm each. That is an upper limit, because part of the climb happens on the slopes between flights. Even so, India’s National Building Code caps a step at home at 19 cm, and at 15 cm in public buildings. [14] Chamundi’s steps were not cut to any code. Many are taller than anything in your house, which is why your thighs notice.

How much energy? Lifting a 65 kg person through 257 metres takes about 39 kilocalories of pure lifting work. But muscles turn only about a quarter of their fuel into useful work; the rest becomes heat. [15] So the body burns roughly four times that, around 160 kcal, just to rise up the hill, before you count the walking itself. (Illustrative arithmetic.)

08

Who looks after a hill?

A staircase that has carried pilgrims for over 360 years now sits in the middle of a modern argument: how much building should a sacred hill take?

In 2026, works began on top of the hill under the Centre’s PRASAD scheme for pilgrimage sites. [18]

THE CASE FOR THE WORKS

Lakhs of visitors need parking, toilets, queues and safe paths. Officials said every effort would be made to balance development with preservation of the hill’s heritage. [19]

THE CASE AGAINST

Environmental groups, hill residents and heritage campaigners said construction threatened a fragile hill and the temple’s character, and climbed the steps in protest. [18]

In April 2026, the Karnataka High Court halted the works with an interim order. [20] We could not confirm the case’s status since, so treat that as the last firm news.

Step back and the whole story has the same shape. A king laid stones so ordinary people could reach the top. Centuries of feet, oil and turmeric changed them. Today’s arguments are about the same question he answered in 1659: how to bring people up the hill without losing the hill.

Maybe that is why the count never settles. A staircase that is used every single day is never quite finished. It is always being walked, worn, painted, cleaned and argued over, one step at a time.
