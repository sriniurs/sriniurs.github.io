Who Really Pays Your Hospital Bill?

India’s new health accounts are out. For every ₹100 spent on health, families still paid about ₹43 straight from their own pockets. Where does the rest come from, and where does your share go?

ABOUT 8 MINUTES

01

The fever that cost ₹1,500

Picture an ordinary week. A child runs a fever. You visit a clinic, get a blood test, and stop at the chemist on the way home. Say the consultation costs ₹500, the test ₹600 and the medicines ₹400. That’s ₹1,500, paid at three counters. (our illustration, not survey data)

Health economists have a name for that money: out-of-pocket expenditure, or OOPE. It means money a family pays directly at the point of care, which no insurer or government scheme pays back. [2]

What doesn’t count is just as interesting. Your insurance premium is not out-of-pocket spending. Neither is a bill your insurer pays, treatment covered by a government scheme like Ayushman Bharat, or free care at a government hospital. [3]

Every year India adds all of this up, across the whole country, in a report called the National Health Accounts. The latest one, for 2023–24, came out on 15 November 2026. [1]

ZappyWait, my insurance premium doesn’t count as money from my pocket? It certainly left my pocket.

Prof. BoltIt counts as insurance. “Out-of-pocket” means the shock at the counter, with nobody sharing it.

02

Splitting India’s ₹100

In 2023–24, India spent about ₹9.52 lakh crore on health, roughly ₹6,801 for every person. That’s about 3.3% of the economy. [3]

Imagine that whole bill shrunk down to ₹100. Here’s who paid it: [3]

Out-of-pocket spending came to about ₹2,901 per person that year. [3] For a family of four, that works out to roughly ₹11,600 a year, or about ₹970 a month. (our arithmetic; averages hide huge differences between families)

Notice how close the top two bars are. For three years running now, the government has paid a bigger share than families. [4] Ten years ago, it was the other way round, by a wide margin.

03

Ten years ago, the pocket paid ₹64

The headline of the new report is how far that pocket share has fallen. In 2013–14, families paid 64.2% of all health spending directly. By 2023–24, it was 42.7%, a drop of 21.5 percentage points. [1]

Over the same decade, government health spending per person nearly tripled, from ₹1,042 to ₹2,959. [1]

The path wasn’t a smooth slide. In 2021–22, the COVID year, one-time emergency spending by government pushed the pocket share down to 39.4%. [5] When that money was withdrawn, it bounced back up to 43.4%, then eased to 42.7%. [4]

For perspective: in the UK, which has a tax-funded health service, out-of-pocket spending was 14.1% of all health spending in 2025. [14]

ZappySo it went from ₹64 to ₹43 in ten years. Who picked up the other ₹21?

Prof. BoltMostly two players. One of them you know as a card in your wallet.

04

The quiet rise of insurance

Two kinds of insurance grew steadily through the decade.

The first is what the report calls social security expenditure: government-paid health cover such as the PM-JAY (Ayushman Bharat) scheme, plus medical benefits for government employees. [6] It rose from 6% of all health spending in 2013–14 to 11% in 2023–24. [1]

The second is private health insurance, the policy you or your employer buy. Its share tripled, from 3.4% to 10.2%. [1]

Together that is 21.2% of all health spending. (our arithmetic) News reports noted it is the first time insurance-type spending has crossed the 20% mark. [7]

Why does this matter? Insurance turns a sudden, huge bill into a small, predictable one. A ₹3 lakh operation becomes a premium you pay calmly every year. That’s exactly the kind of shock out-of-pocket spending measures, and exactly the kind insurance is built to absorb.

05

Where your own money actually goes

Here’s the catch. Most insurance, public or private, is designed around hospital admissions. PM-JAY, for example, covers up to ₹5 lakh per family per year for “secondary and tertiary care hospitalisation.” [9]

But a large chunk of what families pay isn’t for being admitted at all. A peer-reviewed study of national survey data found that medicines made up about 60% of what people paid for outpatient care (clinic visits where you go home the same day), and about 29% for hospital stays. [8]

The same study found a cost most people forget: travel, lodging and food. These non-medical costs were nearly a quarter (23.6%) of what families paid during hospital stays. [8]

Usually covered by insurance

Admission, surgery, room, ICU, medicines given during the stay.

Usually yours to pay

Clinic visits, routine tests, the monthly chemist bill, bus fare, the relative’s meals.

A general picture; what is covered depends on each policy or scheme. (our summary)

ZappySo the big scary bill is the one most likely to be insured, and the small boring ones aren’t.

Prof. BoltAnd small boring bills, every month for years, add up to a big one.

06

A falling share isn’t a smaller bill

If the out-of-pocket share fell, families must be spending fewer rupees.

Not necessarily. It’s a share of a growing total. From 2022–23 to 2023–24 the share fell, from 43.4% to 42.7%, yet out-of-pocket spending per person rose, from about ₹2,767 to ₹2,901. [11] [3]

The report has been read in two very different ways, and both sides have numbers.

The government’s reading: public health spending has tripled in a decade to about ₹4.14 lakh crore, and investment in primary health care reached ₹1.53 lakh crore. [1] Families carry a much smaller share of the load than ten years ago.

The critics’ reading: health economists quoted by ThePrint point out that government health spending as a share of the economy slipped from 1.48% to 1.43% of GDP, well short of the 2.5% target that the National Health Policy 2017 set for 2025. [10] They also note that spending at private hospitals (31.9% of current health spending) was nearly double the spending at government hospitals (16.8%). [10]

One more caution: India changed how it calculates GDP from 2022–23, so health spending as a share of GDP can’t be compared exactly with older years. [3]

07

Karnataka’s surprising number

Where you live changes the answer enormously. The report gives figures for 21 major states. In 2023–24, families in Uttar Pradesh paid 67.1% of health costs themselves, the highest. Uttarakhand was lowest at 26.7%. [3]

Karnataka sits near the bottom of that list, at 28.5%, far below the national 42.7%. [3] Two years earlier, in 2021–22, Karnataka had the lowest share of all 21 large states, at 25.4%. [12]

Here’s the puzzle. Karnataka gets there without especially heavy government spending. In 2022–23, government paid only about a third (33.2%) of Karnataka’s health bill, while its families paid 29.2%. [13] The rest came from other sources, which in the national accounts means insurers, employers and others. [3] The reports we checked don’t break that down further, so we won’t guess why.

And one more surprise: in 2021–22, Kerala, a state known for good health indicators, had one of the highest pocket shares, 59.1%. [12] A high share doesn’t automatically mean poor care. It tells you who paid, not how good the care was.

ZappySo the number tells you whose wallet opened, not whether you got better.

Prof. BoltExactly. Accounts count rupees. They don’t take temperatures.

08

What it means at your kitchen table

A national average can’t tell you what your family will pay. But it does point at a few useful facts.

Anyone aged 70 or above is eligible for free treatment up to ₹5 lakh a year under PM-JAY, regardless of income, through the Ayushman Vay Vandana card. [15] Enrolment needs Aadhaar-based e-KYC. [15] It’s worth checking whether the older people in your family have one.

Read your own policy for the small print on outpatient care, medicines after discharge and annual limits, because that is where family money quietly leaks, as episode 5 showed.

Count the small bills. Since medicines bought at the chemist are such a big share of outpatient spending, a simple notebook or phone note of monthly health spending shows where the money really goes.

In ten years, India moved from families paying ₹64 of every ₹100 on health to about ₹43. That is real progress. But ₹43 is still a lot of money coming out of ordinary wallets, much of it in small bills that no insurance card ever sees.
