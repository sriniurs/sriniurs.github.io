Your First ₹1 Lakh in a Mutual Fund. Where Does It Actually Go?

One bank debit. A surprisingly precise number of units. Follow Zappy’s ₹1 lakh from the tap on his phone to the money that can come back into his account.

₹1 lakh, tracedNAV & unitsFees & redemptions

7–8 MINUTE READ · 9 EPISODES

01

The button that bought 4,000 units

At a Mysuru darshini, Zappy balanced his phone against a steel tumbler of filter coffee. He had tapped Invest ₹1,00,000 and expected the screen to tell him which shares he now owned. Instead, when the allotment appeared, he got a quantity with a decimal point. He rounded it aloud. “Four thousand units. Four thousand of what?”

At the next table, his cousin Meera was looking at a deposit receipt. She could point to the amount she put in and the interest rate on the receipt. Zappy could point to his debit alert, but the app’s investment screen looked like a different language. Both screens showed money leaving a bank account. What each person received in exchange was different.

A mutual fund pools money from many investors. Each scheme has a stated objective that governs what it can hold. When you subscribe to an open-ended scheme, you receive units. Those units give you a proportional interest in its net assets. The underlying shares or bonds are held for the scheme. [1]

This story follows one fictional lump-sum purchase in a conventional open-ended Indian mutual fund. Keep the debit alert handy. By the time the coffee goes cold, we’ll reconcile it with the decimal on the screen.

02

The price of one slice

Prof. Bolt borrowed a napkin and drew a rectangle. Imagine the whole scheme has ₹25 crore in net assets and 1 crore units outstanding. Divide the first figure by the second and you get ₹25 per unit. That figure is the net asset value, or NAV. [2]

“Net” does some work here. The scheme values its investments and other assets, including cash, then accounts for its liabilities. The resulting amount is spread across the outstanding units. If the shares held by an equity scheme lose value, the slice can shrink even when you haven’t touched your account.

There’s a useful way to understand what happens when you join the pool. In a stripped-down model, imagine it has ₹25,000 in assets and 1,000 units. Someone adds ₹2,500 at ₹25 and receives 100 new units. The pool now has ₹27,500 and 1,100 units. Divide again and you still get ₹25. The newcomer brings cash and receives a matching claim on the pool.

The NAV you eventually get matters to how many units are issued. To work that out, though, we first need to follow the money out of Zappy’s bank.

03

Follow the money past the app

Zappy traced the journey with a finger on the table. First came the payment from his bank. Then came the fund’s acceptance of the purchase and allotment of units at the applicable NAV. The holding showed up against his folio, the account reference used by the fund in this example.

From the scheme’s side, the subscription adds to the common pool. The asset management company, or AMC, manages that portfolio within the scheme mandate. Its holdings may include listed shares, bonds and cash, depending on the mandate. A custodian holds the scheme’s securities for safekeeping, and trustees oversee the fund. [1] [3]

Suppose the scheme gets Zappy’s ₹1 lakh on a day when it is also meeting redemptions. Incoming cash can help meet outflows. On another day, net inflows may give the manager cash to deploy. The manager deals with the scheme’s overall cash position. Zappy’s individual payment doesn’t map to an identifiable purchase of a particular share.

That means there are two places to look. The allotment confirmation records what happened to his order. The scheme’s portfolio disclosure shows what the pool holds. Both help answer his original question.

04

The ₹5 hiding in the decimal

In the preview, ₹1 lakh divided by ₹25 gave Zappy 4,000 units. The actual illustration needs one extra line. Indian mutual fund purchase transactions attract 0.005% stamp duty on the applicable transaction value. On ₹1,00,000 that works out to ₹5. [4]

Assume an applicable NAV of exactly ₹25 and no other purchase deductions. The amount available to buy units is ₹99,995. Divide by ₹25 and the result is 3,999.800 units. That explains why his statement and his mental arithmetic can differ by a fraction of a unit.

Now imagine a higher allotment NAV of ₹25.50. The same ₹99,995 would buy about 3,921.373 units. If you look only at the unit count, the first deal might seem better. At allotment, both positions have the same value before rounding because each quantity is multiplied by the price at which it was issued.

The decimal is something you can check. Read the transaction amount, the NAV date and the NAV used. Reconcile the units using the amount available for allotment. The displayed number of decimal places can vary with the scheme’s rounding rules.

₹1,00,000 − ₹5 = ₹99,995
₹99,995 ÷ ₹25 = 3,999.800 units

05

Why 2:40 p.m. can become tomorrow

Zappy remembers pressing the button at 2:40 p.m. He assumes that fixes his purchase price. For a conventional equity fund, there are two things to check. When did the order reach an official point of acceptance, and when were the full funds available for the mutual fund to use? [3] [5]

For purchases in the type of scheme used here, both conditions must be met by the 3:00 p.m. cut-off on a business day for that day’s closing NAV. A bank debit or app confirmation can happen before the fund has the money available. Liquid and overnight schemes have separate rules. An intermediary may also require an earlier submission time. [5]

Suppose Monday and Tuesday are both business days. If the order and money make Monday’s cut-off, Monday’s closing NAV applies. If the order arrives on time and usable funds arrive at 3:20 p.m., the purchase moves to Tuesday’s NAV if all the required conditions are met then.

Either price can turn out to be higher. The useful thing to check is the applicable NAV date on the allotment confirmation. It tells you which day’s price was used, and helps explain an apparent mismatch with the NAV you saw while placing the order.

06

Same units, different rupees

At the next coffee stop, Zappy sees his investment value has moved. He checks the unit count. It still says 3,999.800. The market has moved the value of the assets inside the scheme. His app multiplies his units by the latest available NAV to show what his holding is worth at that price. [2]

At ₹26 per unit, his position is worth ₹1,03,994.80. At ₹24, it would be worth ₹95,995.20. These are hypothetical snapshots, with no additions or withdrawals. The starting NAV is ₹25, so the two scenarios represent a 4% rise and a 4% fall in the NAV itself.

A quoted NAV is also a poor way to compare two schemes. One could quote ₹10 and another ₹100. A ₹10,000 investment before deductions would buy 1,000 units of the first or 100 of the second. If each NAV gained 10%, each holding would be worth ₹11,000. The lower starting NAV offers no automatic bargain.

Think of the ₹26 figure as a valuation date on a receipt. If Zappy later redeems, the applicable redemption NAV and any relevant charges determine what the transaction yields.

07

The fee you rarely see leave your bank

Meera pulls up two listings for the same scheme, one marked Direct and the other Regular. A direct plan can be bought without routing the investment through a distributor. A regular plan includes distribution costs in its expenses. The two plans share the scheme portfolio and have separate NAVs because their expenses can differ. [3] [6]

Running expenses are reflected in the NAV. When the app shows Zappy’s holding using a published NAV, there is no reason to deduct the scheme’s expense ratio again from that balance. The AMC and AMFI disclose expense figures by plan. These can change, so use the current disclosures for an actual comparison. [7]

Picture a simplified year with a flat ₹1 lakh asset base for estimating costs. A 0.50% annual expense rate implies roughly ₹500 on that base. At 1.25%, it implies roughly ₹1,250. The difference is ₹750. These rates are hypothetical. Real costs accrue on changing assets and the calculation is a scale illustration.

A fair comparison starts with the same scheme and option, then looks at the cost of each plan and what support the investor wants. That also explains how two people can discuss the same fund and see different NAVs.

08

When a screen balance becomes cash

Suppose Zappy needs part of his money back. He submits a redemption for 1,000 units. For this illustration, assume the applicable redemption NAV is ₹26. The gross value of those units is ₹26,000. If the scheme applies a 1% exit load to all of those units, it comes to ₹260, leaving ₹25,740 before any applicable taxes or statutory deductions. [2] [3]

An exit load is a scheme-specific redemption charge that can depend on how long the units have been held and how many are being redeemed. With no exit load in this example, the corresponding figure would be ₹26,000 before any applicable taxes or statutory deductions. Look up the scheme’s terms for your own transaction.

The 1,000 units redeemed come out of the folio. Zappy has 2,999.800 units left. The scheme sends the redemption proceeds under the applicable payout timetable. If he needs cash on a particular day, he should check the scheme’s timetable and account for business days.

Tax is a separate calculation. It depends on the kind of scheme, relevant transaction dates and the investor’s circumstances. The ₹25,740 example illustrates the effect of an exit load. The actual amount credited can differ.

1,000 units × ₹26 = ₹26,000. At a hypothetical 1% exit load, proceeds are ₹25,740 before applicable taxes or statutory deductions.

09

Back at the darshini

Back at the steel table, Meera slides the phone towards Zappy. This time he reads the confirmation from top to bottom. Scheme name. Plan. NAV date. ₹25. Stamp duty of ₹5. And there at the bottom, 3,999.800 units. His lakh now has a paper trail he can follow.

A second question has emerged. When does he need the money? If it is for a bill due soon, a fall in the value of an equity fund could force him to sell at an awkward time. If the money is for a distant goal, he can judge the scheme by how its holdings and risks fit that goal. SEBI’s Riskometer gives a quick view of the scheme’s risk level. Read it alongside the scheme objective and holdings. [8]

The practical verdict is to be able to explain your own investment in one sentence. “I own units of this scheme, through this plan, for this goal.” Then open the confirmation and check that you bought the scheme and plan you meant to buy.

Zappy folds the napkin into his pocket. Meera reaches for the bill. The next time his app shows a decimal, he knows where to look.
