Why Keep Money Locked Up in an LPG Cavern?

A Texas export hub, a Chinese chemical plant and an Indian kitchen all need LPG at different times. What is it worth to have fuel ready before you need it?

Texas salt domesYantai chemical plantsMangaluru kitchens

ABOUT 6 MINUTES

01

The question under the question

Every tonne of LPG stored underground ties up money. If you’ve already paid for the fuel, why leave it sitting there?

Our first story on the Mangaluru LPG cavern looked at how the cavern holds LPG and how the fuel travels from port to bottling plant. This time, imagine you’re the person who has to buy the next cargo. If you order too early, you pay for stock you won’t use for weeks. If you order too late, your customers may need the fuel before the ship arrives.

To see how that choice changes with the customer, let’s visit three places. In Texas, a ship is waiting for fuel. In Yantai, a factory is waiting for feedstock. In coastal Karnataka, a bottling plant needs LPG for the next round of cylinder deliveries.

ZappySo the cavern is full of money?

Prof. BoltIt’s full of something you paid for. The question is what you gain by keeping it on hand.

02

Texas makes a cavern out of salt

At Mont Belvieu, east of Houston, companies store natural gas liquids in underground salt domes. Engineers can make a chamber by dissolving part of the salt with water and removing the resulting brine. In Mangaluru, engineers excavated chambers in hard rock and use groundwater pressure to help contain the fuel. Both designs use the geology at the site. [1] [2] [3]

Now picture propane arriving steadily by pipeline while an export ship needs a large cargo in one loading window. The hub can accumulate product before the ship arrives. Storage also lets the hub deliver to local petrochemical plants and other US customers on their own schedules. [3] [4]

Mont Belvieu handles several products. A capacity figure for the whole hub can include mixed natural gas liquids and petrochemicals alongside propane and butane. That matters when someone puts a Texas number next to Mangaluru’s LPG-only figure. [3] [4]

03

Yantai keeps a factory fed

A published engineering review describes a water-sealed LPG cavern in Yantai, China, that began operating in 2015, with a reported volume of one million cubic metres. Its containment principle is closer to Mangaluru’s hard-rock design than to a Texas salt cavern. [1] [5]

Yantai also shows a different reason to hold LPG. Wanhua’s petrochemical operation there uses propane to make propylene, a building block for products such as polypropylene. Propane is the material the process works on. If feedstock runs short, a plant can lose production even when its equipment and staff are ready. [6] [7]

That makes the buyer’s question concrete. Would you pay to carry more days of propane, or risk having to buy a replacement cargo on short notice? The answer depends on the cost of downtime and how quickly you can get another delivery.

ZappyThe propane ends up in plastic?

Prof. BoltSome of it can. The factory turns propane into propylene, which can then be used to make plastics.

04

Mangaluru keeps cylinders moving

HPCL’s Mangaluru cavern has a rated capacity of 80,000 tonnes of LPG. An incoming ship brings a large batch. Bottling plants and distribution centres need smaller quantities on a continuing basis. Bulk storage helps keep those schedules from colliding. [1] [8]

Imagine two otherwise identical Mondays. In the first, the next cargo is late and the terminal still has usable LPG that it can dispatch. In the second, the cargo is equally late and the terminal is already low on stock. The delay is the same. The options are different.

A Mysuru household is several steps downstream. To help that household, bulk LPG still has to reach a bottling plant, get filled into cylinders and move through the dealer’s delivery network. The cavern helps with the supply of bulk fuel. Each step after it has to work too.

THE DEEP DIVEWhat does it cost to buy one more day of supply?
05

The cavern and the fuel have separate bills

Building a cavern is a capital expense. Buying the propane or butane to put in it is a working-capital expense. The first gives you a place to store fuel for years. The second ties up money until the stock moves through the business.

Try a made-up example. A terminal carries 10,000 extra tonnes at ₹50,000 per tonne. That is ₹50 crore in additional inventory. At an illustrative financing or opportunity cost of 8% a year, holding that extra stock continuously would cost about ₹4 crore a year in capital alone.

These are round numbers for the arithmetic. The decision would also account for operating costs and the possibility that LPG prices fall while the product is in storage. On the other side of the ledger sit the costs the buffer might avoid, such as emergency purchases or interruptions.

₹50 crore tied up

10,000 tonnes × ₹50,000 per tonne

₹4 crore per year

₹50 crore × 8% annual cost of capital

06

How many days does the stock buy?

Use the same extra 10,000 tonnes. If a terminal needs to dispatch 2,000 tonnes a day and receives no new deliveries, that extra stock represents five days of dispatch, assuming the entire 10,000 tonnes is available and can be sent out at that rate.

If dispatch rises to 2,500 tonnes a day, those five days become four. The same stock can also be worth fewer days if some of it is the wrong product mix or the network can’t move it where it’s needed.

This is where the original article’s capacity figure and a terminal manager’s inventory figure answer different questions. Rated capacity tells you what the facility is designed to hold. Usable inventory and expected outflow tell you how long the fuel currently available could last.

07

Three customers, three costs of running short

At the Texas hub, the cost might be rescheduling a ship or failing to meet a delivery commitment. At a Yantai petrochemical plant, it might be lost production or an urgent feedstock purchase. In an Indian LPG network, it might be emergency transport or bottling delays that eventually show up in households waiting for cylinders.

So how big should the buffer be? Start with how long replacement supply can take, how quickly stock is drawn down and what a shortage would cost. Then ask whether the next tonne of inventory improves the response enough to justify paying to hold it.

One distinction matters for public policy. A commercial cavern gives its operator room to hold inventory. A requirement to maintain emergency stock would be a separate decision about who pays, how much to hold and when to release it.

08

What is an extra day worth?

Return to the delayed ship. At the export hub, fuel already in storage can help meet a loading window. At the chemical plant, it can keep a production line supplied. At the LPG terminal, it can keep fuel moving to bottling plants until another cargo arrives.

The simplest way to judge the money locked underground is to count the days it buys and ask what happens on the first day after it runs out. A cavern gives you the space. The inventory decision determines how much time you have.

ZappySo should they keep it full?

Prof. BoltI’d first ask how late the next delivery could be, what a day without LPG would cost, and who would feel it.
