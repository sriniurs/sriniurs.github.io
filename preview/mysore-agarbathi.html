THE PASTFrom "oodabathi" to a Mysuru brand

EP 1

The king who sold incense to the world

ZappyEvery pooja room in India smells of agarbathi. When did Mysuru get into it?

Prof. BoltOver a hundred years ago. Locals called them oodabathi, "sticks that blow fumes". In the early 1900s it became an organised industry around Bengaluru and Mysuru.

Royal backing

The Maharaja of Mysore supported making and promoting the sticks. After they won a certificate of merit at the Wembley exhibition in London, the kingdom handed them to foreign visitors as gifts, to push exports.

WHOOSH!

Karnataka's forests were rich in two key ingredients: sandalwood, and halmaddi, a sticky grey resin from the Ailanthus tree.

EP 2

1948: a ₹4,000 start

Mysuru, the year after Independence

A young man named N. Ranga Rao, who lost his father as a boy, had been making agarbathis at home with his grandmother. Buy raw material in the morning, make sticks, sell them, use the money for the next day.

In 1948 he set up shop in Mysuru with about ₹4,000. By 1958 sales were close to ₹10 lakh a year.

ZappyWhy name an incense brand after a cycle?

Prof. BoltSmart marketing. The business began in 1948; the Cycle brand came in 1950. A cycle meant freedom, and it's called a "cycle" in every Indian language. One word, the whole country understood it.

He treated a loose, unbranded product as a brand, with his own fragrance lab and packaging. That idea built Mysuru's biggest incense name. Today his grandson, Arjun Ranga, runs it as the third generation.

EP 3

A smell with a legal address

2005: the GI tag

Mysore Agarbathi

It was among the very first products in India to get a Geographical Indication (GI) tag, in the same early batch as Mysore Silk. The application came from the national agarbathi makers' association.

The tag covers agarbathi made across Karnataka, the old Mysore State, not just Mysuru city.

ZappyWhat does a GI tag actually do?

Prof. BoltIt ties a name to a place, like Darjeeling tea. The reason given: its history, and materials from the region's forests, like sandalwood and halmaddi.

HOW IT WORKSFrom bamboo to your pooja room

EP 4

Anatomy of one stick

The supply line

Thin bamboo stick→ Paste rolled on: a charcoal mix, or a herb-and-resin masala→ Dried in the shade or sun→ Charcoal sticks are dipped in perfume; masala sticks already carry their scent→ Packed and shipped

Two big families

Dipped sticks: a dried charcoal-coated stick soaked in fragrance oil. Cheaper, and most common in shops.

Masala sticks: a thick paste of herbs, resins and sandalwood rolled onto the bamboo, then dried. The famous Nag Champa, made in Bengaluru since the 1960s, uses grey, sticky halmaddi resin. That's why it feels slightly soft.

ZappySo the smell is just the last step?

Prof. BoltThe smell is the brand. The stick is the delivery van. Some makers, like the Mysuru firm, create hundreds of fragrances in their own labs; many others buy ready-made ones.

EP 5

The China and Vietnam twist

What went wrong

In 2011 import duty on raw sticks was cut from 30% to 10%. Cheap raw agarbathis poured in from China and Vietnam.

2009
2% imported
2019
80% imported

Raw-agarbathi imports went from about ₹31 crore (2009) to ₹546 crore (2019), per KVIC.

The fix, in two steps

31 Aug 2019: import of raw agarbathi put in the "restricted" list.

Makers then imported plain bamboo sticks instead: ₹210 crore → ₹370 crore in one year.

June 2020: duty on bamboo sticks raised from 10% to 25%.

PLOT TWIST!

ZappyIndia grows tons of bamboo. Why import sticks at all?

Prof. BoltImporters say much Indian bamboo is too fibrous and jams the machines. The right variety grows mostly in the North-East, far from Karnataka's factories.

EP 9

The Karnataka engine

Three numbers

  • 60%+ of India's agarbathi makers are in Karnataka (2019 report).
  • ~95% of agarbathi exporters are based in Karnataka, says the industry body's past president (2020).
  • The national makers' association started in 1949, and its big trade expo is held in Bengaluru.

ZappyAnd the sandalwood that made it famous?

Prof. BoltMuch scarcer now. Most sticks today use other fragrances. That story deserves its own episode, and Mysuru will get one soon.

EP 10

Prof. Bolt's verdict (opinion)

What Mysuru got right

It turned a smell into a brand, a brand into exports, and kept the headquarters at home. Few Indian consumer products travel this far from one small city.

The weak link

Raw materials. The right bamboo and the old forest resins are increasingly imported or scarce. A fragrance capital needs its own supply line.

My read: the next win isn't just more sticks. It's home-grown bamboo, premium natural fragrances, and taking the Mysore name to many more countries.

EP 11

So what should Zappy do?

Notice what you burn

  • 🌿 Try a masala stick and a dipped stick side by side; you'll smell the difference.
  • 🔥 Read the pack: the flower or resin named is the fragrance, the bamboo is just the carrier.

When you buy

  • 🏷️ Check where it's made. A Karnataka-made packet supports local jobs.
  • 🎁 A box of Mysore agarbathi makes a good gift for friends abroad, just like the Maharaja's guests got.

If you visit Mysuru

Look for the "Mysore Agarbathi" name. It's a GI-tagged product, in the same club as Mysore Silk and Mysore Sandal Soap.
