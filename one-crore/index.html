Why ₹1 Crore May Not Make You Rich Anymore

A number with eight digits. A lifetime of dreams. But why can two crorepatis have completely different futures? Follow the numbers that hide behind the zeroes.

The Human MachineMoney & LifeThe Invisible Thief8-Minute Read

ABOUT 8 MINUTES · 9 EPISODES

01

The crore club: one number, two lives

The bank app flashes a beautiful number: ₹1,00,00,000. Eight digits. A small private firework goes off in your head.

Picture two old school friends meeting at a Mysuru coffee shop. Both have exactly ₹1 crore in investments. Ravi owns his home, has no loans and spends ₹30,000 a month. Sameer rents in a metro, supports his parents and pays school fees. His family spends ₹1 lakh monthly.

Ravi looks relaxed. Sameer is still checking the school-fee email. They hold the same money but live in entirely different financial worlds.

Here is the trick. Wealth in rupees tells you how big the pile is. Financial independence asks a harder question: how many years of your own expenses can that pile support? In a no-return, no-inflation thought experiment, Ravi's annual ₹3.6 lakh bill is about 27.8 years of expenses. Sameer's ₹12 lakh is only about 8.3. Neither is a retirement forecast; it is simply the first clue.

A crore isn't “small change.” It is a major achievement. But the number cannot answer the question people most want it to answer: Am I safe?

ZappySo they are both crorepatis. Why is only one smiling?

Prof. BoltBecause money buys a lifestyle, not a title. The lifestyle comes with its own price tag.

02

The ₹1 crore time machine

Suppose you could hand your ₹1 crore to a relative living in the year 2000. You would not be giving away the same purchasing power.

The World Bank's India consumer-price index, on a common 2010 base, was about 54.3 in 2000 and 233.1 in 2025. Divide the second by the first: 4.29. The broad average basket of consumer goods and services that cost ₹1 crore in 2000 would cost about ₹4.29 crore in 2025. [1]

Turn it around and something startling happens. ₹1 crore in 2025 had the purchasing power of roughly ₹23.3 lakh in 2000. It compares what an average basket buys, using complete historical annual data.

Don't take this as a house-price chart. Land prices and medical bills need not track average inflation; 2025 is not a live 2026 figure. India's official CPI series has also been rebased, so mixing different index bases without linking them can mislead. [2]

But as a time machine, the lesson is unforgettable: the country's price tags have changed far more than our mental image of “one crore.”

Zappy₹23 lakh? Has my money been stolen?

Prof. BoltNo thief broke into the bank. Prices climbed the staircase while your picture of a crore stayed downstairs.

03

The grocery bag gets heavier

The sneakiest part of inflation is not the price of one tomato. It is what happens when thousands of little prices keep moving for twenty years.

Imagine a household that spends ₹50,000 a month today. At an illustrative steady 4% annual increase in expenses, maintaining the same lifestyle would cost about ₹74,000 after ten years, and ₹1.10 lakh after twenty. At 6%, the twenty-year bill becomes about ₹1.60 lakh a month. The bill grows while each rupee buys less.

The consumer-price index averages a basket, while your personal inflation depends on what you actually spend. A young couple may worry about rent and school fees; a retired couple may worry about medicines, helpers and care. Your expenses may rise faster than headline CPI. [3]

The Reserve Bank of India's inflation framework aims at 4% CPI inflation, with a tolerance range of 2% to 6%, for April 2026 to March 2031. But that is a policy target, not a promise about your family's next twenty shopping lists. [4]

So the crore is not just fighting today's expenses. It must also fight all the future versions of those expenses.

ZappyCan a ₹50,000 monthly budget really become ₹1 lakh?

Prof. BoltIn twenty years, yes, under a simple 4% scenario. Time does the heavy lifting.

04

The FD that looks richer than it feels

Here is a common retirement conversation: “Put the crore in a fixed deposit. Seven per cent interest gives seven lakh a year. Problem solved!”

It sounds tidy. At an illustrative 7% annual interest rate, ₹1 crore earns ₹7 lakh before tax. If that interest is effectively taxed at a 30% marginal rate, ignoring cess and special circumstances for the illustration, the interest left is about ₹4.9 lakh, or 4.9% of the starting capital. Now suppose prices rise by 5%. The after-tax amount has barely kept pace with the increase in the general price level—its exact real growth is slightly negative.

The point is not that bank deposits are bad. Deposits can be extremely useful for predictable cash flow and emergency reserves. The point is that nominal return (“my balance earned 7%”) is not the same thing as real, after-tax return (“how much extra life can I buy?”). FD rates also change.

Equities offer growth potential but can fall sharply; gold fluctuates and property is illiquid. No asset offers high growth, complete safety and instant access together.

For an ordinary family, the real task is to give different parts of the money different jobs: near-term spending, protection from unpleasant surprises and long-term growth. [3]

ILLUSTRATION: 7% BEFORE TAX → ~4.9% AFTER 30% TAX, BEFORE CESS

ZappySo a 7% FD can leave me barely ahead of 5% inflation?

Prof. BoltAfter tax, possibly. Always check what remains in your pocket and what it buys.

05

Is a crore enough to stop working?

This is where the conversation changes from being rich to staying solvent.

A corpus of ₹1 crore can give very different starting withdrawal rates. Draw ₹25,000 a month and the first year's withdrawal is ₹3 lakh, or 3% of the starting corpus. Draw ₹50,000 monthly and it becomes 6%. Draw ₹1 lakh monthly and it is 12%. That last figure is not the investment's promised return; it is simply how fast you are taking money out.

Withdrawals may need to grow with inflation: ₹4 lakh this year becomes ₹4.16 lakh next year at 4%. An increasing draw needs careful planning.

There is research relevant to India. A PFRDA-published analysis of withdrawal strategies under specified asset mixes and simulation assumptions found that starting around 3% can be more robust than higher withdrawal rates over a long retirement. But real-world longevity, taxes, markets and spending may differ. [5]

Think of your corpus as a water tank. The size matters, but so do the tap, the incoming rainfall and the number of summers ahead. Two people can start with an identical tank and make it last very different lengths of time.

06

The market's cruel timing trick

A market crash is unpleasant at any age. In the first year of retirement, it can be much more than unpleasant.

Imagine ₹1 crore invested in a volatile portfolio. It drops 20%, leaving ₹80 lakh. The retiree still needs ₹4 lakh for living expenses, so the balance becomes ₹76 lakh. Then the market recovers 25%—exactly enough for an untouched portfolio to recover from a 20% fall. But this retiree's remaining ₹76 lakh rises only to ₹95 lakh, even before a second year's withdrawal or fees.

The market recovered; the retiree did not, because some investments were sold while prices were low. That is called sequence-of-returns risk. A long-term average return can hide the painful timing of losses. India's National Institute of Securities Markets highlights this risk in retirement planning. [6]

A liquid spending reserve can reduce forced selling during market dips. Diversification and flexible spending may help, but cannot remove risk.

For a retirement lasting decades, a sound plan matters as much as the starting cheque.

₹1 crore→−20%: ₹80L→Withdraw ₹4L: ₹76L→+25%: ₹95L

Simplified example before tax, fees and any second withdrawal; not a market forecast.

ZappyBut the market bounced back 25%! Why am I still short?

Prof. BoltBecause you sold part of the portfolio while it was down. Timing can steal more than the headline loss.

THE DEEP DIVEWhat the headline does not tell you.
07

The home you live in is not the money you spend

A person may tell you, “My net worth is ₹2 crore!” But a large part of that could be the family home. Can it pay next month's electricity bill?

Of course the home has value. It provides shelter and may spare you rent. But unless you sell, rent out part of it, borrow against it or move somewhere cheaper, you cannot spend its market value like money in the bank. This is why “net worth” and “investable retirement corpus” are different figures.

Suppose one retiree has a debt-free home and ₹1 crore of financial assets; another has a rented home and the same financial assets. Their starting corpus is equal. Their required withdrawals are not. Now add two dependants, periodic repairs and medical expenses. The answer to whether the corpus is enough shifts again.

An inherited plot or investment flat may be valuable but slow to sell; a hospital bill will not wait for the buyer's paperwork.

On the other hand, a pension, rent from a reliably occupied property or an annuity can reduce what must come from invested savings. The correct plan counts all cash inflows, but does not pretend they are risk-free. Rent can stop. Vacancies, taxes, repairs and inflation can change the sums.

Financial security is always a household-level story.

NET WORTH

Everything you own minus what you owe. Some may be difficult to sell.

SPENDABLE CORPUS

Financial assets available to fund goals and withdrawals.

ZappySo my flat's estimated sale price isn't my monthly income?

Prof. BoltExactly. A valuable roof and a spendable bank balance are different animals.

08

The deep dive: four numbers worth knowing

Before making a retirement decision, collect four numbers. Not from a viral WhatsApp chart—from your own household.

1. Annual spending. Count ordinary expenses plus irregular bills: insurance, property maintenance, holidays, family responsibilities and replacement of things like cars and appliances. A ₹60,000 monthly life is already ₹7.2 lakh annually before extras.

2. Years to fund. Retiring at 50 is a very different maths problem from retiring at 70. Plan for either spouse living longer than expected.

3. Inflation and after-tax return. The exact real-return formula is (1 + nominal return) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1. At a hypothetical 8% nominal return and 5% inflation, real return is about 2.86% before tax and fees—not 8%. At a higher effective tax burden, the gap narrows.

4. The bad-year reserve. What happens if markets decline, a tenant moves out or medical costs arrive earlier than expected? A plan without shock absorbers looks great in a spreadsheet and miserable in a crisis.

CPI is not your personal cost of living; a single return estimate hides uncertainty. A resilient plan survives when assumptions prove wrong. [3] [6]

A plan that survives a bad scenario is more valuable than one that looks fantastic in a perfect scenario.

ZappyCan a spreadsheet predict my retirement exactly?

Prof. BoltNo. But a sensible spreadsheet can show you which assumptions are dangerous.

THE VERDICTProf. Bolt and the real meaning of rich.
09

A richer question than 'Am I a crorepati?'

Back at the coffee shop, Ravi and Sameer have stopped comparing balances. They have begun comparing responsibilities.

Ravi's debt-free home and low expenses make his crore go further. Sameer needs a bigger cushion for school, rent and family support. Their money has different jobs to do.

So what is the verdict? ₹1 crore remains a remarkable milestone. It buys choices and a strong foundation—but not a universal retirement ticket. Inflation does not know how many commas are in your bank balance. Neither does an unexpected health expense.

The intelligent question is not “Is one crore enough for everyone?” It is: “Is my corpus big enough for my spending, my time horizon and the risks I am willing to bear?”

List annual expenses, dependable income and debts; hold appropriate liquidity; project inflation; and test both good and difficult scenarios. Seek qualified advice for major decisions.

And remember the original lesson: a lakh is a number, a crore is a number—but freedom is the ability to live the life you need without constantly fearing the next bill.

GOOD NEWS

₹1 crore is substantial wealth, especially with low debt and modest expenses.

WATCH OUT

Inflation, taxes, large withdrawals and bad early market returns can shorten its life.

OPINION · PROF. BOLT

Replace the magic-number goal with an expense-based, risk-tested financial plan.

SO WHAT SHOULD ZAPPY DO?
Write down one year's true spending; estimate what it may become; subtract reliable income; plan an emergency reserve; review the retirement number every year.
