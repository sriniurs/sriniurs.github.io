SABARIMALA · 41 DAYS · 18 STEPS

The 41-Day
Sabarimala Vow,
Decoded

Your colleague has stopped shaving, comes to work barefoot in black and calls everyone “Swami”. Here’s what the bead chain, the two-pocket bundle and the eighteen steps mean, and how Kerala moves lakhs of pilgrims through a tiger reserve.

01

The colleague who stopped shaving

Every November, a few men in your office turn up in black, barefoot, with a bead chain round the neck and a beard that will keep growing for six weeks. Nobody makes an announcement. Everyone understands: he has “put on the mala”.

This is the start of the vratham, a vow of austerity kept for 41 days before the trek to the Lord Ayyappa temple at Sabarimala, which sits inside the Periyar Tiger Reserve in Kerala. [1] The 41-day stretch is called a mandala. It traditionally begins on the first day of the Malayalam month of Vrishchikam, which this year falls on 17 November. [2] [3]

The vow begins with a small ceremony, mala dharana. A Guru Swami, an experienced devotee who guides first-timers, places a tulsi or rudraksha bead chain with an Ayyappa locket around the devotee’s neck. [4] The mala comes off only after the pilgrimage is over. [3]

For the next 41 days the rule book is strict: vegetarian food, celibacy, no alcohol or tobacco, [5] a cold bath and prayers morning and evening, [6] no haircut, shave or nail-trimming, [3] walking barefoot where possible, and simple clothes in black, blue or saffron. [4]

Devotees explain the black as detachment: plain, identical clothes that leave no room for showing off. [3] And from the day the mala goes on, every devotee is addressed as “Swami”, the same word used for Ayyappa himself. A first-timer is a Kanni Swami. [6] The bank manager and the auto driver in the same queue now share a name, a dress code and a rule book.

02

A suitcase with two pockets

Near the end of the vow, the pilgrim’s luggage is packed in a ceremony of its own, the kettunirakkal, usually under the Guru Swami. [3]

The luggage is the irumudi: iru means two, mudi means bundle. [7] It is a cloth bag with two compartments, carried on the head. One holds offerings for the deity; the other holds the pilgrim’s own things. [3]

The front pouch, the munmudi, must reach the shrine untouched and is opened only after the final climb. The back pouch, the pinmudi, carries supplies for the road. [8] As the journey goes on, the back pouch shrinks while the front stays whole, a neat picture of what the trip is meant to do. [9]

The star of the front pouch is a coconut turned into a container. Its water is drained through one of the “eyes” of the shell, warm ghee is poured in, and the opening is sealed. This is the neythenga, the ghee coconut. [10]

The ghee coconut’s journey
Drain the coconut water→Fill with warm ghee, seal it→Carry it on the head to the shrine→Ghee is poured over the idol→Some comes home as prasadam

Source: [10]

That pouring is the Neyyabhishekam, the most important offering at the shrine: each pilgrim literally brings his own ghee from home. [10] Devotees read it as a parable. The drained water is worldly desire, the coconut is the body, and the ghee is the soul offered to the Lord. [11] Different Guru Swamis word it differently, but the coconut-as-body idea comes up again and again.

03

The first stop is a mosque

On the traditional route, pilgrims don’t go straight to the temple. They first stop at Erumeli, a town about 40 km away, and pray at a mosque. [1]

It is the mosque of Vavar, who in the Sabarimala legend is a devoted friend and companion of Ayyappa. Pilgrims on the traditional route climb the mountain only after offering prayers here, and there is a shrine for Vavar at Sabarimala itself, too. [12] The mosque stands between two Ayyappa shrines, and pilgrims walk around it, break coconuts and make offerings, while Muslim worshippers inside keep up their five daily prayers. Its administrators say more than 80% of Sabarimala pilgrims stop here. [1]

The most colourful moment comes in January. In the Petta Thullal, a dance of celebration, pilgrims paint their bodies, carry toy weapons, dance from a small temple to the mosque, circle it and make offerings, and then move on to the town’s main Ayyappa temple. [13] Two groups lead it. The Ambalappuzha group traces its lineage to warriors who, by tradition, helped Ayyappa in battle, and says it was given the right to lead the dance by the old Pandalam royal family. [14]

From Erumeli, the hardcore still walk the old forest path to the temple, roughly 61 km through the hills. [15] Most pilgrims today take a much shorter way in, which we’ll come to.

And from outside Kerala? The mosque says most of its visitors come from Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Odisha. [1]

04

Eighteen steps, no shortcut

At the end of the forest path, in front of the shrine, rise the Pathinettam Padi: pathinettu means eighteen, padi means step. [16]

They come with a rule found almost nowhere else. Only a pilgrim who has kept the vow and carries the irumudi on his head may set foot on them. [17] Everyone else reaches the shrine by another route. [4] The bundle is the ticket; the 41 days are what earn it.

Why eighteen? There is no single answer, and devotees seem to enjoy that. One explanation is the eighteen hills around Sabarimala. [18] Another says Ayyappa was master of eighteen weapons and laid one down on each step. [19] Others map the steps to the senses, emotions and qualities a person must climb past. [20] These are traditions and interpretations, not history, and they sit happily side by side.

Pilgrims break a coconut at the foot of the steps [16] and begin with the right foot. [20] And there’s a very physical reason the climb feels humbling. With a loaded bundle balanced on your head, you can’t look up. You climb head bowed, one step at a time.

05

Park, shuttle, climb

Most pilgrims now arrive by road. They reach the base camp at Nilakkal, and Kerala RTC buses shuttle them the rest of the way to Pamba, the river at the foot of the hill.

The numbers are big. For this season Kerala’s KSRTC has lined up 950 special buses and 1,456 staff, with uninterrupted service on the Nilakkal–Pamba route the priority. About 390 run in the Mandala phase, rising to 560 in the Makaravilakku rush. [21] This year it also plans direct air-conditioned buses from the Thiruvananthapuram and Kochi airports to Pamba. [22]

From Pamba, it’s on foot. There are two ways up:

THE TRADITIONAL PATH

Pamba → Neelimala → Appachimedu → shrine. About 3.8 km, steep granite steps. At least 1 hr 10 min. [23]

SWAMI AYYAPPAN ROAD

A winding hill road with hairpin bends. About 4.1 km. At least 1 hr. [23]

Those who find the Neelimala steps too daunting take the road instead. [24] The climb is hard enough that there are cardiology centres on the way, at Neelimala and Appachimedu. [23] That detail tells you something about who climbs: not just young men, but grandfathers on their 30th trip.

06

Booking a slot with God

You can’t just turn up any more. Darshan at Sabarimala now runs on a booked slot, and the daily ceiling for this season is 80,000 pilgrims, including those booking on the spot. [25]

The online virtual queue opened on 29 October on the official website and is free. There is no spot booking at Pamba this season; walk-in passes are issued only at three base points. [26] Here is how the 80,000 is split:

Why the fuss about booking? Crowd control on a narrow hill path is a safety problem before it is a convenience problem. The temple board has argued that an online booking is an authenticated record of who is on the hill, while a spot pass is only an entry ticket, and it noted spot bookings rising from about 3.96 lakh to 4.85 lakh over two seasons. [27] On Makaravilakku day last season, the virtual queue was capped at just 30,000, plus 5,000 spot passes. [28]

Even with caps, the totals are huge. Last season, more than 30.5 lakh devotees had darshan by Mandala Pooja day, and the 41-day Mandala period brought the temple a record ₹332.77 crore. [29]

07

The tin everyone carries home

Ask anyone who has made the trip what they brought back, and the answer is a dark, sticky sweet in a tin: aravana.

It’s a thick payasam made from red rice, jaggery, ghee, cardamom and dry ginger powder. [30] Families back home wait for it, which is why pilgrims buy it by the dozen, and why it is the temple’s single biggest earner. In the first fortnight of last season, aravana alone brought in ₹47 crore of ₹92 crore, more than the hundi’s ₹26 crore. [31]

Making it at that scale is a factory problem on a hilltop. Last December, daily sales of 3.5 to 4 lakh tins outran production, and the board had to ration it, first to 20 tins a person, then to 10. [32]

NEEDED THIS SEASON

3.6 lakh tins a day, plus a planned reserve of 75 lakh tins before the doors open. [33]

THE PLANT MAKES

About 2.4 lakh tins a day a week before the season, with an upgrade still incomplete. [33]

The bottleneck isn’t only the cooking. The plant packs tins with two semi-automatic machines installed in 1999 and 2018. The older one was built to fill 180 tins a minute and now manages about 110, and neither can run more than 20 hours a day. [33] Last year, a reserve of 47 lakh tins still wasn’t enough. [33]

08

The day the mala comes off

For Karnataka, Sabarimala is not a faraway Kerala story. Every season, groups from our side arrive at Kerala’s railway stations and hire buses onward, [34] and Karnataka’s own KSRTC launched a Volvo service from Bengaluru to Nilakkal for pilgrims in December 2023. [35] Karnataka pilgrims are among the biggest groups from outside Kerala at Vavar’s mosque, too. [1]

The season runs past the 41 days. After Mandala Pooja on 27 December, [2] the second peak is Makaravilakku, on 14 January 2027. [36]

And for each pilgrim the story ends quietly, back home. The ghee that went up the hill in a coconut comes back as prasadam. The tins of aravana are shared out. Then, with the pilgrimage done, the mala finally comes off. [3] The beard goes. The colleague is back in his blue shirt on Monday.

What stays is harder to see. For 41 days, a bank manager and an auto driver ate the same food, wore the same black and answered to the same name, “Swami”. Then they climbed the same eighteen steps, heads bowed.
