RUPEE · DOLLAR · YOUR BILL

Why Is the
Rupee at 94?

A year ago one dollar cost ₹88.66. In May it touched ₹97. Today it’s near ₹94. Nobody in Mysuru buys dollars at the kirana, so why should you care? Because you pay for them anyway.

ABOUT 8 MINUTES

01

A rupee has a price tag too

An exchange rate is just a price: how many rupees it takes to buy one US dollar. And like the price of tomatoes, it moves with demand and supply.

When Indians need more dollars than the world wants to hand over, each dollar costs more rupees. That is what “the rupee is falling” means.

Here is how far it has moved. On 7 November 2025 the rupee closed at ₹88.66 a dollar. [3] On 20 May 2026 it hit its weakest point, about ₹97.12. [4] On 9 November 2026 it closed at ₹93.93, close to its strongest level in six months. [1] [2]

02

Who is buying all the dollars?

Three big forces have been pulling the rupee down this year.

1. Oil. India buys most of its crude from abroad, and pays in dollars. From April to September 2026, imports met 88% of the country’s oil needs, and the crude import bill jumped 46% to $90.6 billion as prices rose. [6] When oil gets dearer, refiners need many more dollars every single day.

2. Foreign investors heading out. When a foreign fund sells Indian shares, it converts the rupees back to dollars before leaving. Reuters reported that foreign investors had pulled more than $23 billion out of Indian equities in 2026. [7] Every rupee sold that way adds to the pressure.

3. A strong dollar. In uncertain times, money worldwide runs to the dollar as a safe place to park, which makes it dearer against almost everyone. [7]

WHAT PUSHES IT DOWN

Dearer oil, foreign funds selling, a strong dollar, war in West Asia. [6] [7]

WHAT PULLED IT BACK UP

Softer crude, foreign money returning and hopes of calm in West Asia, says this week’s market report. [1]

Standing in the middle of all this is the RBI, which can slow the slide but not stop the tide. More on what it actually does in the deep dive.

The rupee doesn’t fall because India is weak. It falls when India needs more dollars than the world is sending in. Oil is the biggest dollar bill we pay.
03

What it does to your bill

Most of us never touch a dollar, so the effect sneaks in through other bills. Anything priced in dollars, or built from imported parts, slowly gets dearer: fuel, phones, laptops and other electronics, and foreign travel and education. [10] Fuel is the sneakiest, because it raises the cost of moving everything else around. [7]

But a weak rupee isn’t bad news for everyone. Anyone earning in dollars gains: exporters, IT companies and families who receive money from relatives working abroad. [7] [10]

04

The people cheering a weak rupee

For every family paying a dollar bill, there’s another one receiving dollars.

Money sent home. India is the world’s biggest receiver of money from its people abroad: $135.46 billion in 2024–25, according to RBI data. [11] Every rupee of weakness makes each of those transfers bigger once it lands in an Indian bank.

Software from our own backyard. IT companies sell services to the world in dollars and pay salaries in rupees. Karnataka is the largest single contributor: software technology park units in the state exported an estimated ₹3.5 lakh crore in 2025–26, the most of any state. [12] Mysuru’s own share of the state’s IT exports was put at around ₹3,000 crore by the state IT minister. [13] A weaker rupee quietly fattens all those earnings.

But not every exporter wins. If you first import gold to make jewellery, or chips to assemble a phone, your costs go up along with your earnings. For such businesses the currency gain can be partly cancelled out. [14]

THE DEEP DIVEWhere the rupee has been, what the RBI does on a bad day, and what to watch next.
05

From ₹4.76 to ₹94

The rupee has not always floated freely. For years its value was simply fixed by the government. From 1949 to 1966, one dollar cost ₹4.76. [15]

Then came crisis. After drought and economic strain, on 6 June 1966 India cut the rupee’s value overnight: the dollar went from ₹4.76 to ₹7.50, a devaluation of about 57%. [15] The second big jolt came in 1991, during a severe balance-of-payments crisis, with big deficits and investors losing confidence. Between July 1991 and March 1993, the rupee was effectively devalued by around 35%, and India moved towards letting the market set the rate. [16] Both times, a crisis came first and the devaluation followed. [16]

Prof. Bolt’s “₹45 a dollar” isn’t ancient history: the rupee traded around ₹44 in 2010 and near ₹39–40 in early 2008. [18] Since then the slide has been steady. 2008 was its worst single year, down almost 24%, and from 2008 to 2022 the rupee was the worst-performing Asian currency. [17]

A slow slide over decades is normal when a country’s prices rise faster than America’s. What alarms markets is speed, and 2026 was fast.

06

What the RBI does on a bad day

The RBI says it does not defend any fixed number. It lets the market set the rate and steps in to stop wild swings and panic. [20] Here is how a rescue usually works.

  1. Sell dollars. The RBI sells dollars from its reserves, often through big state-run banks, sometimes even before the market opens at 9 am so the rupee starts the day stronger. [19] More dollars on offer means each one costs fewer rupees.
  2. Put the rupees back. When the RBI sells dollars, banks pay in rupees, which drains cash from the banking system. So it often follows up with “swap” deals that pump rupees back in. [19] [20]
  3. Ease the biggest buyers. This year it opened a special window so state-run oil refiners don’t have to scramble for dollars in the open market, plus a deposit scheme to attract more foreign currency. [5]

The scale is huge. In the year to March 2026, the RBI sold a net $53 billion or so in the currency market, by one estimate the largest on record. [21] Its reserves stood at about $717 billion at the end of October. [8]

That pile is India’s financial shock absorber. Every dollar spent defending the rupee today is a dollar not available for the next storm. [21]

The RBI can slow a fall and calm panic. It cannot make India need fewer dollars. Only cheaper oil, more exports or more foreign investment can do that.
07

What to watch next

Nobody can promise where the rupee goes next, and forecasts change fast. In May, when the rupee was near its low, one bank’s analysts expected it to stay between ₹95 and ₹100 for the rest of 2026. [22] Six months later it is below ₹94. Instead of guessing the number, watch the forces.

COULD STRENGTHEN IF

Oil prices keep easing, foreign investors keep buying Indian shares, and West Asia stays calm, which is what lifted it this week. [1]

COULD WEAKEN IF

Oil spikes again, foreign funds head back out, or the dollar strengthens worldwide. [6] [7]

The most useful single number for an ordinary family to watch is the price of crude oil. It drives the biggest share of India’s dollar demand, and through fuel it reaches almost everything else you buy. [6]

08

The price on the price

The exchange rate is the price of a dollar. But because India buys so much oil, and so many phones, chips and machines, from abroad, that one price quietly sits inside thousands of others.

This year it swung from ₹88 to ₹97 and back towards ₹94. For a student abroad that swing is lakhs of rupees; for a family receiving money from overseas it is a bonus; for most of us it is a few rupees here and there, hidden in a bill that never mentions the dollar at all.

The rupee isn’t a scoreboard of national pride. It’s a thermometer for how many dollars India needs compared with how many flow in.
