Why Is the New Road to Coorg Taking So Long?

The Mysuru–Kushalnagar highway got its foundation stone on the same day the Bengaluru–Mysuru expressway opened. Three years and seven months later, about a quarter of it is built. Here is where the time went.

ABOUT 7 MINUTES

01

Two roads, one day

Anyone who has driven to Coorg on a long weekend knows the feeling. You fly down the expressway to Mysuru, and then the road shrinks to two lanes, a bus in front and a tractor coming the other way. [5]

That was supposed to change. On 12 March 2023 at Mandya, the Prime Minister opened the 118-km Bengaluru–Mysuru expressway, which cut that drive from about three hours to around 75 minutes. [2] At the same event he laid the foundation stone for the next piece: a four-lane highway from Mysuru to Kushalnagar, the gateway to Kodagu. [3]

The first road is now part of daily life. The second, by the National Highways Authority of India's (NHAI) own count, is 26.25% built after three years and seven months. [1]

02

What exactly is being built?

This is not a widening of the old Hunsur road. It is a brand-new road on fresh land, which engineers call a greenfield project. It starts near Paschimavahini, close to Srirangapatna, where it meets the expressway, and ends at Guddehosur near Kushalnagar. [4]

It is also access-controlled. That means you can get on and off only at fixed junctions; no gate, side lane or tea shop opens straight onto it. That is what keeps traffic fast. Hold on to that idea, because it explains much of the delay.

Officials say the full road should cut the Bengaluru–Kushalnagar trip from about five hours to about two and a half. [8] NHAI is building it in five pieces, called packages, each with its own contractor and deadline:

03

A quarter done, and slowing

No stretch is even halfway. The best package, Hunsur to Hemmige, is about a third built. The Kushalnagar end is the slowest at about a fifth. [1]

The pace is the real worry. From April to October 2026, progress across the packages averaged less than 2% a month, and some stretches barely moved. [1]

The road was first meant to be ready in 2026. [4] NHAI's regional officer for Karnataka now expects it in August or September 2027, while admitting that work has not picked up. [1]

04

The fight over a side road

Remember access control? For a driver it is a gift. For a farmer whose village is on one side of the new road and whose fields are on the other, it can be a wall.

The fix is a service road: a slow lane running alongside the highway that locals use to reach their fields, schools and markets, and to get to the nearest crossing. The Bengaluru–Mysuru expressway has two-lane service roads on both sides. [13] Villagers on this route want them too, and they want them continuous, not in broken pieces.

NHAI's regional officer told Star of Mysore that these protests are a main cause of the delay. Work on Packages 4 and 5, the stretch nearest Mysuru, stopped for eight months. [1]

THE VILLAGERS' SIDE

A fast road that cuts a village off from its own fields is not progress for the village. Without a continuous service road, a trip next door can become a long detour.

THE BUILDER'S SIDE

Every extra kilometre of service road needs more land, money and redesign, and an eight-month halt on two packages pushes back the whole highway.

05

The last few acres

On paper, land is almost sorted. Between 95% and 98% has been acquired across the stretches. [1] That's a big jump: in December 2024, the shares ranged from about 70% to 84%, depending on the package. [15]

But a road is not a pile of land. It is a chain, and a chain with one missing link carries no traffic. The plots still missing leave gaps in the alignment, and small land disputes add more. [1]

Land has also become far more expensive to buy. Since the 2013 land acquisition law, which pays farmers much more than before, NHAI's average cost of land rose from about ₹80 lakh a hectare before 2015 to about ₹3.6 crore. [9] Those payments matter to the people giving up their land. Even before construction began, a farmers' association opposed taking fertile farmland for this road and demanded better compensation, a job for one family member, and no tolls for farmers. [12]

A 98%-acquired road is not 98% usable. One unsold field in the wrong place is enough to stop a lorry.
06

Tigers, birds and a pile of permissions

Kodagu is forest country, and the route runs close to some of Karnataka's most protected land. Because the project touches wildlife sanctuaries and a tiger reserve, it needed clearance from the National Board for Wildlife, India's top wildlife body. [6] That came in December 2025, and work on the Kushalnagar package began on 8 December. [5]

Before that, a Union environment ministry panel had looked at a request to divert about 137 acres of forest, and asked for animal underpasses built to Wildlife Institute of India guidelines. [7] Environmentalists have argued that about 54 hectares in the eco-sensitive zone of the Ranganathittu bird sanctuary and the buffer of Nagarhole tiger reserve is too high a price for a shorter drive, and that it adds to human–wildlife conflict. [8]

Approvals are still holding things up. Forest clearance papers are pending, Forest Department approvals around Bannikuppe and Periyapatna have been slow, and the Pollution Control Board permits needed to open quarries (for stone and gravel) are held up. Frequent changes of project director have not helped either. [1]

Compare the expressway. Work on its two halves began in May and December 2019, and it opened in March 2023, about four years for 118 km. [11] [2] But that project upgraded an existing highway on a corridor already in use. [11] The Kushalnagar road is being carved through farmland and near forest, almost from scratch.

It is far from the only slow road. In March 2026, the Union road transport minister told Parliament that 85 national highway projects were delayed by more than three years, for reasons including land acquisition and permissions. [10] A parliamentary committee in 2024 counted 189 projects held up by land and 64 by environmental clearances. [9] The rules have tightened since: projects are now supposed to have 90% of their land in hand before contracts are awarded. [14]

08

What it means for your Coorg weekend

For now, the old two-lane road is still the way to Coorg. Plan for it: leave early on long weekends, and allow for construction diversions near the stretches where the new road is being built.

When the new road does open, it will take you only as far as Kushalnagar. The last 22 km to Madikeri, Package 1, has not started, because its route runs through dense forest that still needs environmental clearance. [1] The state's public works department plans to widen the existing Kushalnagar–Madikeri road meanwhile. [5]

On cost, the numbers don't quite match. ₹4,130 crore was announced when the foundation stone was laid. [3] The four construction packages add up to ₹2,502 crore. [1] The gap most likely covers land and other costs, but that's our reading; the reports don't break it down.

The bigger lesson is simple. Most of the work on an Indian highway is not laying asphalt. It is persuading thousands of landowners, a few forest officers and a national wildlife board, and agreeing with the villages on the route about where the side roads go. On this road, those talks are what decide the finish date.
