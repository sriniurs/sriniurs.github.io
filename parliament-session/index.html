What Actually Happens When Parliament Meets?

The Winter Session is due in the next few weeks. Here is the calendar, the daily clock, the journey of a bill, and the honest numbers on how much of it actually works.

ABOUT 8 MINUTES

01

Parliament has no fixed calendar

Most of us picture Parliament as always in session, like a court or an office. It isn’t. It works in bursts, and the dates are fixed fresh every time.

The Constitution says only this: Parliament must meet at least twice a year, and no more than six months can pass between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next. [1] Everything else is habit. By convention there are three sessions a year: Budget, Monsoon and Winter. [2]

The government picks the dates, and the President formally “summons” both Houses. That is why the announcement always arrives as a minister’s post saying the President has approved the session. [3]

This year’s Winter Session hasn’t been announced yet. The latest reports say it will probably begin in the first week of December. The government has also considered extending the Monsoon Session instead, since that session was never formally closed (“prorogued”). [4]

02

A day in the Lok Sabha, hour by hour

The Lok Sabha starts at 11 am and is normally scheduled to sit until 6 pm. The first half of the day belongs to MPs raising issues and asking questions on their own; the second half usually goes to government business, like bills. [5]

A typical sitting day
11:00 Question Hour→12:00 Zero Hour→Afternoon: bills and debates

Question Hour is the hour when ministers answer to the House. MPs send questions at least 15 days ahead. [6] A starred question gets a spoken answer, and MPs can fire follow-up questions on the spot. An unstarred question gets a written reply from the ministry. [5]

Zero Hour, which comes right after, is a purely Indian invention. It isn’t mentioned anywhere in the rules of procedure, yet it has become the slot where MPs raise urgent local matters. [7] (The Rajya Sabha flips the order: it moved its Question Hour to noon in 2014 to stop it being disrupted.) [7]

Question Hour is the only time a minister must answer a question the government didn’t choose. That is why the next numbers matter.
03

How a bill becomes law

A bill is just a draft law. A ministry writes it, usually after inviting comments from other ministries and sometimes the public. [8] Then it has to clear three “readings” in each House:

First reading: introduced→Sometimes: sent to a committee→Second reading: clause by clause→Third reading: final vote→Other House, then President’s assent

In the second reading every clause can be accepted, changed or thrown out; in the third, the House votes on the whole thing. [8] Only when both Houses have passed it and the President signs does it become an Act. [9]

What if the two Houses simply refuse to agree? For ordinary bills the Constitution offers a tie-breaker: a joint sitting of both Houses. It has been used just three times since 1950: for the Dowry Prohibition Bill in 1961, the Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill in 1978, and the Prevention of Terrorism Bill in 2002. [10] It can’t be used for Money Bills or amendments to the Constitution. [11]

ZappyThree times in 75 years? They must get along better than my family.

Prof. BoltUsually one side simply has the numbers in both Houses. No tie, no tie-breaker.

04

The quiet room where laws get read properly

The floor of the House is where bills get voted on. The place where they get read, line by line, is a committee room. MPs from different parties sit around a table, call officials and outside experts, and write a report suggesting changes. The government doesn’t have to accept them. [8]

But fewer and fewer bills go there. Across four Lok Sabhas, the share of bills sent to a committee has fallen sharply: [12] [13]

The trend continued in the latest sessions. Two of the bills in the 2025 Winter Session were sent to committees. [14] In the 2026 Monsoon Session, none went to a Standing Committee. [15]

05

“₹2.5 lakh a minute”: where that number comes from

Every time Parliament is disrupted, you’ll hear that it costs taxpayers ₹2.5 lakh a minute. It’s worth knowing where that comes from.

A 2008 Lok Sabha discussion paper put the cost at ₹29,000 a minute. Four years later, in 2012, the Parliamentary Affairs Minister of the time said a minute cost ₹2.5 lakh. [16] That 2012 figure is the one everyone still quotes, even though it is more than a decade old and there is no updated official number. [17]

Our arithmetic: ₹2.5 lakh × 60 minutes = ₹1.5 crore an hour. Over a six-hour sitting day that comes to about ₹9 crore. [our arithmetic] [17]

Treat it as a rough yardstick, not a bill. Most of the cost (the building, staff and security) is paid whether the House works or shouts. What a disruption really loses is the time: the questions that never get answered and the bills that never get read.

06

The session that lasted 17 hours

The 2026 Monsoon Session was scheduled for 19 sitting days. [18] According to PRS Legislative Research, an independent research group, the Lok Sabha worked for 15% of its scheduled time and the Rajya Sabha for 33%. [19] The government’s own figures were a little higher: 19% and 39%. [20]

In hours, the Lok Sabha functioned for about 17 and a half hours across those 19 sittings. [21] Question Hour ran for a total of nine minutes, and only two of 380 listed questions got a spoken answer. [22] Eleven bills were passed; for nine of them, no MP apart from the minister spoke in the Lok Sabha. [18]

The swing is the point: the same House sat for 103% of its scheduled time in the 2025 Winter Session. [14] Who is to blame for a washout depends on whom you ask. After the Monsoon Session, the government and the Opposition each blamed the other for the disruptions. [20]

07

What your MP can do for you in there

Karnataka sends 28 MPs to the Lok Sabha. [23] Each has a few real tools that can help a local issue:

A QUESTION

Starred for a spoken answer, unstarred for a written one. Even a written answer puts official figures on the record. [5]

ZERO HOUR

A short slot to raise an urgent matter: a stalled highway, a train stop, a flood. [7]

There’s also the private member’s bill, a law proposed by an MP who isn’t a minister. In the 17th Lok Sabha, MPs introduced 729 of them, but only two were discussed. [24]

You can check what your own MP has asked and how often they turned up: PRS keeps a public “Find your MP” tracker, and the questions and answers are published on Parliament’s website. [25] [6]

08

What to watch this winter

Once the dates are out, the big item to watch for Karnataka is delimitation: redrawing Lok Sabha seats by population. The Delimitation Bill, 2026, introduced in April, would raise the number of Lok Sabha seats from 543 to 850. [26] Whether and how it returns this session is still unclear.

THE CENTRE SAYS

No state’s tally will fall. Its figures show Karnataka going from 28 to 42 seats, with its share of the House staying almost the same (5.15% to 5.14%). [23]

CRITICS SAY

Leaders from several southern states argue that seats drawn purely by population would shrink the voice of states that controlled population growth. [27]

Whatever the agenda, three numbers will tell you how the session really went, and PRS publishes all three at the end: the share of scheduled time worked, how many questions got a spoken answer, and how many bills went to a committee. [19]

Those three are a better report card than any speech. A noisy day makes the news. A session where questions get answered and bills get read hardly ever does.
