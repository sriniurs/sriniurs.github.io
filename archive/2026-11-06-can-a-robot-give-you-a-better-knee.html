Can a Robot Give You a Better Knee?

The robot can help a surgeon place a knee replacement with greater precision. But does that mean less pain, faster recovery or a longer-lasting implant? Zappy wants the answer before Ajji signs up.

PART 1: THE BIG QUESTIONSame operation. A different set of tools.

EP 1

“A robot is going to replace Ajji's knee?”

ZappyProf, I saw a video of a robot doing knee replacement. Does the surgeon just press a button and watch?
BEEP! WHIRR!
Prof. BoltNo. In today's routine robotic-assisted knee replacement, the surgeon remains the pilot. The robot is an advanced tool for planning, tracking and guiding selected steps—not an independent surgeon.
The robot assists the surgeon. It does not decide that you need surgery, choose your treatment, or replace the surgeon's judgement.
EP 2

First, what is a total knee replacement?

When arthritis has worn away the smooth cartilage, the joint becomes painful and stiff. In a total knee replacement, the surgeon removes damaged joint surfaces and fits artificial components—usually metal on the thigh and shin, with a tough plastic bearing between them.

The operation is still a major knee operation. A robot does not turn it into a tiny keyhole procedure.

ZappySo the robot doesn't make a different kind of knee?
Prof. BoltCorrect. The replacement is still a knee implant. The difference is how the surgeon plans and guides the bone preparation and implant position.
EP 3

What happens when the robot enters the theatre?

Map the knee➜Plan implant position➜Track the bone➜Guide the cuts➜Check the result

Some systems build a 3D model from a CT scan before surgery. Others map landmarks and movement in the operating room without a pre-operative CT. Tracking markers or sensors help the system understand where the bone and instruments are.

Prof. BoltThe surgeon chooses the plan and checks it. The robot helps the surgeon reproduce that plan with controlled, measured movements. The exact workflow differs by platform.
“Robotic” is not one single machine design. Ask which system is being used and what it actually does.

EP 5

Where the robot clearly wins: precision

0.83°smaller average deviation from the hip–knee–ankle alignment target

A 2026 review of randomised trials found robotic assistance reduced average alignment deviation and the number of knees falling outside the planned alignment range. The surgeon still chooses the alignment philosophy and target; the robot helps execute that plan, not decide what is best for every knee.

0.35relative risk of an alignment outlier

That is about a 65% relative reduction in alignment outliers in the pooled data—not 65% less pain, 65% fewer revisions, or a 65% better knee.

Precision test: ROBOT WINS. The strongest and most consistent advantage is technical accuracy and fewer radiographic alignment outliers.
EP 6

But does a more precise knee feel better?

ZappyIf the cuts are more accurate, surely Ajji will have less pain and walk sooner?
Prof. BoltThat is a reasonable hope—but a technical advantage does not automatically become a noticeable benefit for the patient. Pain and recovery depend on many things.
Some pooled studies report small gains on selected scores, but the findings are not consistent. The large 2026 trial and updated RCT-only review do not show a broad, clinically meaningful advantage in pain, recovery or overall knee scores.

THE DEEP DIVEFive research checks: precision, pain, time and implant life

EP 7

The big randomised trial: 339 patients

RACER-Knee · The Lancet · 2026

339patients · 10 hospitals · 33 surgeons

Patients were randomly assigned to Mako robotic-arm-assisted or conventional total knee replacement. Participants and outcome assessors were masked. The main patient-reported measure was the Forgotten Joint Score at 12 months. This tested one platform; the result cannot automatically be applied to every robotic system.

Adjusted difference: −1.5 points (95% CI −7.5 to 4.5), favouring conventional surgery. No clinically meaningful patient benefit from the robot was demonstrated at 12 months.

Serious adverse events: 16 in each group. Robotic surgery cost more in this trial. Longer follow-up is still ongoing.
EP 8

What does the 2026 RCT meta-analysis say?

31randomised studies
4,086participants

The updated review pooled 35 papers representing 31 randomised studies. It found better alignment accuracy and fewer outliers with robotic assistance.

But patient outcomes?

  • Patient-reported scores: broadly comparable.
  • Function and safety: broadly comparable.
  • Blood loss and hospital stay: comparable.
  • Operating time: about 21 minutes longer on average with robotics.
The review estimated about 21 extra operating-room minutes on average (95% CI 15–27). Certainty ranged from very low to high; most assessments were low or very low. The authors also noted possible small-study effects for some radiographic results.
EP 9

Does the implant last longer? The registry check

BMJ · 30 September 2026

697,145knee replacement records, 2018–2024

Researchers used England, Wales and nearby national registry data, then matched robotic and conventional groups to compare revision risk.

Estimated 5-year total-knee survival

Conventional
98.5%
Robotic
98.6%

Hazard ratio 1.03 (95% CI 0.86–1.26); no significant difference. Mean follow-up was 2.5 years, so the 5-year figure is a survival estimate, not five full years observed for every patient.

This is a large observational registry study, not a randomised trial. Residual confounding cannot be ruled out. It is reassuring—but it cannot prove the two methods are identical in every patient.
EP 10

What about ten years and beyond?

96.9%pooled 10-year survival, conventional
97.8%pooled 10-year survival, robotic

A 2025 meta-analysis of 20 comparative studies found no statistically significant difference between these estimates (P=0.3). Only five studies contributed ten-year survival data, all using active robotic systems. That review did find a small pooled WOMAC-score advantage for robotic TKR, but pain scores (P=0.16) and Knee Society scores were not significantly different; pain results varied substantially between studies.

How to read this carefully

The numbers look different, but the study did not establish that robotic implants last longer. The ten-year subgroup was small and used active robotic systems, so it cannot settle the performance of every current platform.

LONGER LIFE: NOT PROVEN
EP 11

The robot has a learning curve too

17median cases in a 2025 learning-curve review

That review covered 31 studies and 9,916 knees. The interquartile range was 9–27 cases.

That is a research average—not a magic pass mark, not a universal credential and not a guarantee of competence. Platforms differ, and surgeons start with different experience.

Ask how regularly your surgeon uses the specific system, how the team is trained, and how results are audited.

PART 3: THE VERDICTWhat should a patient actually do?

EP 12

Prof. Bolt's verdict — OPINION

GOOD NEWS!

Robotics is a real precision advance. It helps the surgeon plan and execute bone cuts more reproducibly, with fewer radiographic alignment outliers.

WATCH OUT!

Precision is not the same as a guaranteed better-feeling knee. Current trials have not shown a reliable, clinically meaningful improvement in pain, recovery or implant survival for the average patient.

MY TAKE

Robotic wins the precision test; clinical outcomes are still a draw. If an experienced surgeon recommends it for your knee and the extra cost is acceptable, it is a sensible option. Conventional TKR is not obsolete or automatically inferior.

CLEAR VERDICT: MORE PRECISE ≠ GUARANTEED BETTER OUTCOME
EP 13

So what should Zappy do?

ZappyShould I tell Ajji to insist on the robot?
Prof. BoltTell her to choose the right surgeon and the right plan for her knee. Robotics can be part of that plan—but it is not a compulsory upgrade for everyone.
Simple rule: If there is a specific reason—such as a difficult deformity or unusual anatomy—the surgeon is experienced with that platform, and the extra cost is manageable, consider it. Do not choose it just because an advertisement says “latest”.
EP 14

Five questions before you decide

  1. Why is robotic assistance useful for my particular knee? Is there a deformity, unusual anatomy or a specific planning reason?
  2. Which robotic platform will you use? Does it use a CT scan or an imageless mapping workflow?
  3. How experienced are you with this system? How often do you use it for total knee replacements?
  4. What difference should I realistically expect? Talk separately about alignment, pain, walking, recovery and implant life.
  5. What is the additional cost and backup plan? What happens if the system is unavailable or cannot be used during surgery?
A good surgeon should be comfortable explaining both the advantages and the limitations—without pressuring you.
EP 15

The robot cannot do the rehabilitation

Whether the operation is robotic or conventional, recovery still needs pain control, swelling management, safe walking, muscle strengthening and physiotherapy.

Good plan➜Good surgery➜Good rehab➜Best chance of a good result
ZappySo the robot may help the surgeon—but Ajji still has to do her exercises?
Prof. BoltExactly. The robot cannot do the hard work of getting stronger. That part is still human!
