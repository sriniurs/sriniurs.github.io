The Dasara Exhibition: How Does a 10-Day Festival Run for Three Months?

Dasara ended on 21 October. The exhibition opposite the palace runs until 8 January. Here’s how a ten-day festival keeps a fair going for three months, and who makes money from it.

90 DAYS₹11.54 CR TENDER15 LAKH VISITORS

ABOUT 8 MINUTES

01

The festival ended. The fair didn’t.

Dasara 2026 ended on 21 October with the Jamboo Savari. More than a month later, the gates on the big ground opposite Mysore Palace are still open, the giant wheel is still turning, and families are still queuing for bajji.

That is the Dasara Exhibition. It opened with the festival on 11 October and is scheduled to run for 90 days, until 8 January 2027, and possibly longer if crowds keep coming. [1] [2]

So how does a 10-day festival end up with a fair that lasts three months? The answer is part history, part geography, and mostly business.

ZappyI thought the exhibition was Dasara.

Prof. BoltThe festival lasts ten days. The exhibition is the part of Dasara you can still visit at Christmas.

The procession is the festival. The exhibition is how Mysuru keeps Dasara going for another three months.
02

It began as a science-and-trade fair

The usual story is that Maharaja Chamaraja Wadiyar X started the exhibition in 1880, to show Mysoreans the newest ideas in farming, industry and science. [3] [4]

Not everyone agrees on the year. A history written for Star of Mysore by a member of the royal family dates the first “Agricultural and Industrial Exhibition” to October 1888. A reader’s letter to the same paper points out that in 1880 the Maharaja was still a minor, and power passed to him only in 1881. [5] [6] Either way, it is well over a century old.

His son, Nalwadi Krishnaraja Wadiyar, strengthened the exhibition as a showcase for the state’s farms and factories. [7] The idea was a fair you learned at. Today’s government stalls on schemes, the space dome and the defence models carry on that tradition. [2]

Sources disagree on 1880 or 1888. They agree on the purpose: a fair to show people what was new.
03

A fair on a dried-up lake

The grounds have a name most visitors never hear: Doddakere Maidan, the “big lake ground”. It was once Devaraja Sagara, or Doddakere, a lake that has since dried up. Royal processions once went to it during Dasara. [8]

In 1980 Dasara became a people’s festival run by the state, and the exhibition was held on Doddakere Maidan that year. [9] A permanent body was set up to run it, now called the Karnataka Exhibition Authority (KEA). Sources give different dates for this: 1980, 1981 or 1987. [9] [3] Earlier exhibitions were held elsewhere, including a permanent exhibition building on Irwin Road. [10]

ZappySo the fairground was a lake, like Kukkarahalli?

Prof. BoltYes, but this one dried up. Next time you’re in the queue for the giant wheel, you’re standing on an old lake bed.

A dried-up lake right next to the palace became the city’s biggest open ground. That is why a fair this size fits in the centre of Mysuru.
04

Who actually runs it?

You might picture government officials selling tickets. Mostly, they don’t. Each year the KEA puts the exhibition out to tender: companies bid for the right to run it. [11]

In 2025 the winner, Fun World, paid ₹11.54 crore. In return it collects the entry and parking fees, runs the commercial stalls in Block A, the amusement park and the food courts. [11] The same company has run the show for many years; in 2023 it said it had done so for nine. [12]

The government keeps the other half: the departmental stalls, where boards and corporations display their schemes. These have been criticised in the past for being empty, or for having nobody on hand to explain them. [13] For 2026 the KEA told departments to finish their stalls before opening day. [2]

How the exhibition is split

THE OPERATOR

Pays the KEA up front. Earns from tickets, parking, rides, food and commercial stalls.

THE GOVERNMENT

Owns the ground. Runs the departmental stalls and decides the dates and rules.

The exhibition is really a three-month lease of the city’s biggest ground, with a government showcase inside it.

In ten years, the price of running the fair has roughly tripled (our arithmetic). Interest isn’t always guaranteed: in 2018 the first tender drew no bids at all, and in 2022 only one company bid.

A company that pays ₹11–12 crore up front needs months, not days, to earn it back. That is the business reason the fair runs for 90 days.
06

The ticket maths

In 2025 entry cost ₹40 for adults and ₹30 for children. [19] It was ₹20 for adults in 2017. [14]

Take the 2025–26 crowd of about 15 lakh visitors. [20] Even if every one of them paid the adult price, tickets would bring in about ₹6 crore (our estimate, and an upper limit, since children pay less). That’s barely half of the ₹11.54 crore fee.

Tickets · up to ~₹6 crore (our estimate)→+ stall rents, rides, food courts, parking→= has to cover ₹11.54 crore, plus costs

Our estimate from published ticket prices and visitor numbers. The operator’s accounts are not public.

So the gate is only the start. Rides, snacks and stall rents earn the rest. That’s why food and rides take up so much of the ground (our observation).

ZappySo my plate of churumuri is paying for the giant wheel?

Prof. BoltMore or less. The ticket gets you in. Everything you buy once you’re inside is what pays for the fair.

The entry ticket is the cover charge. The real money is in the food, the rides and the stall rents.
07

15 lakh people

The crowds are growing. The 2019 exhibition drew about 11.5 lakh visitors in 90 days. [16] The minister in charge said earlier editions had drawn 8–9 lakh, and the 2025–26 one more than 15 lakh. [1] [20]

Most visitors come in a few busy bursts. In 2023, Vijayadashami day alone brought 55,000 to 60,000. [21] On one Sunday in October 2025, more than a lakh people came through the gates. [13] Crowds peak at weekends and on holidays. [20]

08

The plan: never close

For about nine months of the year, the ground in the middle of the city sits mostly empty. That’s the next fight. [20]

In 2026 the government announced a plan to turn the exhibition grounds into a year-round amusement park. It is building ₹4 crore of permanent facilities, including 32 permanent stalls and a food court with weather-proof shelter. [1] [2]

An earlier idea, a “Mysuru Haat” modelled on Delhi’s Dilli Haat, stalled. It needed about ₹130 crore for permanent structures. [20]

The question for the next decade is whether Doddakere Maidan stays a festival ground or becomes a permanent attraction.
!

OPINION: keep the “exhibition” in the exhibition

This section is our opinion, not reporting.

WHAT WORKS

A private operator running the fair brings in reliable money up front, and the KEA doesn’t carry the risk of a rainy season.

WHAT SLIPPED

The fair began as a place to learn. Empty or unexplained government stalls let that down. A stall that teaches nothing is just a tent.

WHAT WOULD HELP

Publish the stall list and the operator’s main terms each year. If the ground goes year-round, keep part of it as open public space, not just paid attractions.

✓

So what should Zappy do?

Go on a weekday evening. Weekends and holidays are packed. A December weekday is the calm version.

Check the price at the gate. It was ₹40 for adults and ₹30 for children in 2025. Last year’s operator also ran parking.

Visit the government stalls on purpose. They are free once you’re inside, and when they’re staffed, they’re the most useful part: schemes, subsidies and services you may qualify for.

Budget for the food, not the ticket. The ticket is the small part of the spend.

Go before 8 January. That is the scheduled last day, unless it gets extended.

The elephants went back to the forest weeks ago. The lights on the palace are off. And on Doddakere Maidan, the giant wheel is still turning.

The festival lasts ten days because the calendar says so. The exhibition lasts ninety because someone has paid ₹12 crore to run it. One is tradition, the other is business, and Mysuru has learnt to enjoy both.
