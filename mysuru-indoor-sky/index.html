Mysuru is getting
an indoor sky

At the foot of Chamundi Hill, a new kind of planetarium is taking shape. The clever part is not just what you will see. It is where the light comes from.

The Curious FilesMysuru + scienceAbout 6 minutes
01

A sky you can switch on

Imagine arriving while the Sun is still up—and sitting down beneath a sky full of stars.

A planetarium makes that possible by creating a sky indoors. You are watching a representation, rather than looking through the roof at the real stars. That gives a show something the actual night sky cannot offer on demand: the ability to change the scene.

Mysuru’s upcoming facility is part of COSMOS—the Cosmology Education and Research Training Centre, managed by the Indian Institute of Astrophysics. The institute describes an LED dome planetarium alongside a research training centre. [1]

The local news hook: Star of Mysore reported on November 7 that preparations were under way for a November 13 inauguration at the University of Mysore’s Chamundi foothill site. That is a reported schedule, not confirmation that public shows or ticket bookings have begun. [2]

ZappySo a cloudy afternoon need not cancel the stars?

Prof. BoltInside this dome, the weather does not choose the programme.

02

The screen makes its own light

In a projection-based planetarium, an instrument sends light onto the dome. The surface reflects that light towards the audience.

An LED dome changes the arrangement. LED means light-emitting diode: the image is made by tiny light sources on the dome’s inner surface. The surface displaying the picture also produces its light. The Mysuru project’s technology supplier describes numerous LED panels forming a 15-metre tilted dome. [3]

Here is an easy detail to miss: a projector can also use LEDs as its light source. So “LED” alone does not tell you which system you are looking at. The useful question is: are the LEDs inside a projector, or spread across the dome itself? [4]

ZappySo “LED” is only half the explanation.

Prof. BoltExactly. Ask where the lights live.

03

A planetarium is not a telescope

A telescope collects light arriving from objects in space. A planetarium constructs a view for an audience. The distinction matters: an indoor flight past a planet is not footage from a camera making that journey.

Modern planetarium software can use astronomical databases to build navigable views of space. Berlin’s planetarium describes a system that moves from a sky seen from Earth to a wider view of the universe. That is an example of what such software can do, not a promise about Mysuru’s opening programme. [8]

A useful way to watch is to ask what the scene represents: a photograph, a view assembled from data, or an explanatory animation. All can teach us something, but they are different kinds of evidence.

04

The indoor sky is over a century old

Long before digital screens, engineers faced an ingenious challenge: how could people sitting inside a room see the sky appear to move?

The early modern answer used a carefully engineered star projector. ZEISS presented its first model in Munich in 1923. The Deutsches Museum’s permanent projection planetarium opened on May 7, 1925. Its first projector displayed about 4,500 stars. [6]

That history helps explain the change in Mysuru. The dome remains a shared sky above an audience, but the image-making machinery has evolved. Mechanical and optical projection, digital projection and self-emitting LED surfaces are different ways of giving a room its own universe.

The clever invention was never merely a ceiling covered in dots. It was a controllable sky that could make an explanation visible.

ZappySo people were visiting indoor skies before space travel?

Prof. BoltYes. The room got a universe long before a rocket got a passenger.

05

Why tilt a sky?

Now return to the shape of the room. A screen can surround you with an image, but where that image sits in your field of view also matters.

The Mysuru design tilts the dome. In an earlier explanation to PTI, IIA’s director said this brings the night-sky view in front of the audience, reducing the need to crane the neck upwards. The change is in the theatre’s geometry, not in the astronomy being shown. [5]

Think of a classroom diagram: turning the board towards the people looking at it does not change the subject. It changes how they see it. A tilted dome applies that practical question to an image that wraps around a room.

06

The audience could become the authors

The project’s name contains an easily overlooked clue: COSMOS is a training centre as well as a planetarium. IIA’s stated goals include visualising scientific data, developing teachers’ skills and allowing students to program shows for the dome. [1]

Imagine a student preparing a short explanation of an astronomical observation. Choosing a beautiful image is only the beginning. What do its colours represent? Which part is measured, and which part helps explain the measurement? What needs a label?

Those questions turn a display into a learning tool. The student must understand the subject well enough to guide somebody else through it. This is an illustration of the educational opportunity, rather than a description of a course already open for enrolment.

07

Mysuru’s next sky has a local past

There is another side to the project that does not involve the newest screen. COSMOS has a history-of-astronomy initiative focused on older Kannada material from the Mysore region. Its plans include locating, scanning, cataloguing and translating literature and manuscripts. [7]

IIA names repositories including the Oriental Research Institute, Maharaja Sanskrit College and the University of Mysore’s Kannada studies institution, alongside private collections. The material is not all gathered in one convenient place. [7]

Why is this connected to a new planetarium? Because a place that explains astronomy can also explore how people here have studied and described the sky. Preserving an old text and building a modern display are different jobs, but both help knowledge find a new audience.

08

A room with a much larger view

Put the pieces together. The LED surface explains how the picture is made. The tilt changes how the audience faces it. The training and heritage projects explain why the institution could matter beyond a single show.

There are still practical details for a future visitor to check: public opening, bookings, show languages and timings. A planned inauguration does not settle those questions. This edition makes no claim that ticket sales have started.

But the idea already offers something worth being curious about. At the foot of a familiar Mysuru hill, a new room is being built to make unfamiliar distances easier to imagine. The journey begins by taking a seat.
