THE WARM, SWOLLEN KNEE

Why Is My Knee Still Warm and Swollen Weeks After Surgery?

How to recognise healing, manage everyday swelling and know when to call for help.

THE HEALING PATTERNSWELLING AT HOMEWHEN TO CALL

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The knee that felt warmer after dinner

Three weeks after her knee replacement, Padma sits down after making dinner. The operated knee feels warmer than the other one, and the leg looks puffier than it did that morning. The swelling had been easing over the past week. Today she spent more time on her feet. She looks at the discharge leaflet and wonders which changes she should mention to the surgical team.

Some warmth and swelling can persist for weeks after knee replacement as the tissues heal. Look at the change over time and how you feel. Swelling that is getting worse, new calf pain or swelling, wound drainage, fever or chills need urgent assessment. A warm knee on its own gives you only part of the picture. [1][2][3]

On the fridge is a sheet with her exercise times and the hospital’s contact number. She adds two notes to it: what the leg is like when she first gets up and how it feels after her usual activities. If anything changes quickly or she feels unwell, she knows to use the discharge contact instructions straight away. [2][3]

Prof. BoltTell the team how long it’s been warm and what else has changed. The wound, the rest of the leg and how you feel all matter. [1][2]
02

Why does a healing knee feel warm and puffy?

Knee replacement affects the tissues around the joint as well as the bone surfaces. The body’s healing response increases blood flow and fluid can collect in those tissues. The knee may feel warmer, look larger and feel tight when you bend it. Swelling can also be visible farther down the leg around the ankle or foot. [1][3][4]

In a review of studies of uncomplicated recovery after total knee replacement, the operated knee was warmer on average than the other knee. The temperature difference was greatest in the early weeks and generally narrowed over the following months. The researchers were describing a pattern across groups of people. A particular temperature difference at home can’t tell you whether you have an infection. [1][2][5]

Padma’s discharge advice says that swelling should gradually settle, with some variation from day to day. She’s interested in whether walking, dressing and bending are becoming easier across the week. If the knee is becoming more painful or harder to use, that is a change to report even if she can still walk. [2][3][4]

Three different things often get rolled into the word “hot.” One is the skin over the knee feeling warm to the touch. Another is redness or another change in skin colour around the wound. The third is feeling feverish or shivery throughout your body. When you speak to the team, describe each separately so they understand exactly what you’ve noticed. [1][2][8]

03

Why does it look bigger in the evening?

You may notice more puffiness after spending time on your feet. Standing to cook or sitting with the foot down for a long journey can make the leg look fuller later in the day. In an otherwise improving recovery, some swelling can settle with rest and elevation. It can take months for swelling to settle fully. [3][4][6]

Compare what’s happening with your usual pattern. For example, Padma remembers that the ankle looked a little puffy after lunch yesterday and better after she rested. If the swelling becomes newly pronounced, keeps increasing, or comes with new calf pain or worsening redness, she should seek urgent help. Don’t wait for the next morning just to see whether a warning sign settles. [2][3][4]

An ordinary day can involve more time with the foot down than you think. For Padma, that includes making tea, standing while someone visits and sitting at the table with her feet on the floor. She asks her physiotherapist how to fit short breaks into a day she can actually follow. [4][6][7]

ZappyI did more walking today. Can I assume that explains the swelling?
Prof. BoltAn active day can make the leg puffier. New calf symptoms, worsening swelling or wound changes still need attention. [2][3][4]
04

What can I do on an ordinary recovery day?

Follow the walking and exercise plan your team gave you. Try shorter periods of activity with breaks if longer ones leave the leg more uncomfortable. Use the walking aid they recommended. If the knee keeps swelling more after your routine or you’re finding it harder to move over several days, ask your physiotherapist how to adjust the plan. [3][4][7]

If your team recommends elevation, ask them to show you how to support the leg comfortably. If they advise a cold pack, wrap it in a towel and use it for the duration they specify. Check with your team before using cold over skin with reduced feeling. Follow their directions on dressings, compression and any blood-thinning medicine. [3][4][7]

Make one change at a time so you can tell what helps. Padma tries sitting for part of the meal preparation and takes a planned break afterwards. She still does the exercises her team prescribed. She notes what she could do comfortably and brings that to her next session. [3][4][7]

If the team has given you a cold-pack routine, check the skin during and after use and keep the pack away from direct contact with skin. If you have concerns about the dressing getting wet or about reduced sensation, ask what they want you to do before using it. Keep doing the exercises you were shown as directed. [3][4][7]

THE DEEP DIVEWarmth, swelling and changes to report
05

Which wound changes should I report?

Ask your surgical team what the wound should look like at your stage and when the dressing should be changed or removed. Follow their instructions for checking it. Get urgent medical help if you notice new wound leakage or pus, spreading redness, pain or swelling that’s getting worse, or fever or chills. These can be signs of infection. [2][8]

If you call, say when the operation took place and what changed. Describe what you can see, including whether there is new drainage or whether redness is extending beyond an area that was already pink. Even without a fever, a worsening wound or increasing pain is a reason to call. You can share a photo if the surgical team asks for one. [2][8]

If the colour change is hard to see, you can still tell the team about increasing tenderness, swelling, drainage or a change in how you feel. If a family member helps check the wound, show them the discharge advice and agree who will call if they notice a change. [2][8][9]

06

When could swelling mean a blood clot?

A blood clot in a deep vein, often called a DVT, can happen after knee replacement. New calf pain or tenderness, new swelling in the calf or leg, or throbbing and redness can be warning signs. Get urgent assessment even if you’ve been taking the blood-thinning medicine prescribed for prevention. A clinician can decide whether you need tests, such as an ultrasound. [2][9]

You can have swelling after surgery without a clot. What matters is that you can’t reliably tell the cause by looking at your leg, comparing it with the other one or pressing on the calf. Tell the team where you feel the symptoms, when they began and whether they are changing. [2][9]

Call your local emergency number for sudden shortness of breath or chest pain, particularly if you also have calf pain or leg swelling. A clot in the lungs is a medical emergency. [2][9]

The team may ask about your blood thinner, recent activity and other symptoms. Have the medicine name to hand if you can find it easily. If you have missed a dose or had difficulty taking it, tell the clinician so they can advise you based on your prescription. [2][3][9]

07

What should I tell the team when I call?

Start with the date of surgery and the leg that was operated on. Say whether the change is around the knee, in the calf or at the wound, and whether it has come on suddenly or increased over several days. Mention fever, chills, drainage or any change in your ability to walk. Have your discharge papers and medicine list nearby so you can answer questions. [2][8][9]

Padma writes down what she notices rather than trying to label it herself. At a routine review, she could say, “There’s still puffiness around the knee, but it’s less than last week and I can walk to the gate more easily.” If there’s a warning sign, she calls straight away and gives the team the time it started. [2][3][4]

Before you finish the call, check where you should be seen, how soon, and who to contact if things change again. If you’re told to attend, bring any information the team asks for. If the instructions are hard to follow, say so during the call so they can help you find the right route to care. [2][8]

08

Which pattern matters most?

Some warmth and puffiness may be present even as your walking and knee movement improve. Look for the general direction of recovery and note what’s different from your usual day. Ask for a review if progress stalls or symptoms are worsening, and use the urgent advice you were given if a warning sign develops. [1][2][3][4]

Before leaving hospital or at your next visit, make sure you know who to call during the day and outside normal hours. Keep the number where you and anyone helping you can find it. If you have sudden breathlessness or chest pain, call emergency services. [2][8][9]

ZappyWhat’s the most useful thing I can tell the nurse?
Prof. BoltSay what’s new, where it is and when it started. That helps the nurse decide how quickly you need to be seen. [2][8][9]

What can I expect as healing progresses?

Some warmth and puffiness can linger for weeks or months. Look at whether movement and swelling are generally improving. [1][3][5]

When should I get urgent help?

Call the surgical team or urgent care for worsening redness, swelling or pain, fever or chills, wound leakage, or new calf pain or swelling. [2][8][9]

When should I call emergency services?

Call your local emergency number for sudden breathlessness or chest pain after surgery. [2][9]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. After knee replacement, get urgent medical help for worsening redness, pain or swelling, fever or chills, new wound drainage, or new calf pain or swelling. If the surgical team is unavailable, use your local urgent care service. Call your local emergency number for sudden breathlessness or chest pain. [2][8][9]
