INSIDE THE NEW KNEE

What Exactly Gets Replaced in a Total Knee Replacement?

An illustrated look at the new joint surfaces, the plastic insert and what may happen to the kneecap.

THE METAL COMPONENTSTHE PLASTIC INSERTTHE KNEECAP

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The question the model brought up

Meera is due to have a total knee replacement. In the consultation room, she turns a plastic model over in her hands. There’s a shiny curved piece on top and a flat piece underneath. She can still see the shape of a knee. “When you say total,” she asks, “how much of my own knee will still be there?”

In a typical total knee replacement, the surgeon removes damaged cartilage and a thin layer of bone from the ends of the thighbone and shinbone. Metal and plastic components form new surfaces where those bones meet. The thighbone and shinbone themselves stay in place. If the kneecap is resurfaced, a small plastic component goes on its underside and the rest of the kneecap stays. [1][2]

ZappySo where are the shiny pieces on my model going?
Prof. BoltOne caps the end of the thighbone. The other usually sits on top of the shinbone, with a smooth plastic insert in between. [1][2]
02

What was there before the operation?

The thighbone is called the femur. The larger bone below the knee is the tibia, or shinbone. Their ends meet at the knee. Where the bones touch, smooth cartilage helps them glide as you bend and straighten. The kneecap, or patella, sits in front and moves in a groove on the thighbone. [1][2]

In osteoarthritis, the cartilage can wear unevenly. The inner and outer sides of the joint may be affected to different degrees. The joint behind the kneecap can be affected too. A clinician can show you on your X-rays where the space has narrowed and explain how the findings fit with your pain and movement. [1][2]

Ligaments are bands of tissue that steady the knee. Muscles and tendons help bend and straighten it. Those structures still matter after surgery. That is one reason the implant is only part of the operation and recovery. A surgeon checks how the knee moves and feels, and rehabilitation helps you regain the use of the leg. [1][2][6]

03

What goes on the thighbone and shinbone?

The surgeon shapes the lower end of the thighbone so a curved metal component can fit over it. At the top of the shinbone, the surgeon prepares a flat surface. A common design has a metal tray that rests on that surface, with a separate plastic insert that fits into the tray. Some designs use an all-plastic shinbone component. [1][2]

The inside and outside surfaces where the thighbone meets the shinbone are treated in a total replacement. The new thighbone component also provides the groove where the kneecap moves. Ask the surgeon to point out which surfaces your operation will treat and whether they plan to resurface the kneecap. [1][2][3]

The surgeon chooses a size that fits the prepared ends of the bones. Some shinbone trays have a short stem that sits within the shinbone for support. If your surgeon shows you a model with a stem, ask where it sits. The long part of the shinbone remains your own bone. [1][2]

ZappyDoes “total” mean every piece of the knee comes out?
Prof. BoltThink of the contact surfaces on the model. The components fit onto the ends of your bones, and the muscles and tendons still move the leg. [1][2]
04

What does the plastic insert do?

The curved metal component on the thighbone moves against the smooth plastic insert. The plastic is a hard, durable material called polyethylene. Its shape helps guide movement and contributes to stability. The surgeon checks how the knee bends and straightens with trial components before putting in the final ones. [1][2]

Meera sees a diagram where the insert is drawn floating between the pieces. In that drawing there’s a gap so she can see its shape. In the assembled implant, the insert usually locks onto the tray. In an all-plastic shinbone design, the bearing surface is part of the component itself. [1][2]

If you look at your X-ray after surgery, the metal parts are easy to see and the plastic usually is much less visible. Ask your team to point out where the insert sits. The space you see between metal components can be where the plastic bearing is doing its job. [1][2]

THE DEEP DIVEThe kneecap, implant fixation and getting the knee moving
05

What happens to my kneecap?

The kneecap stays in front of the joint. If the surgeon resurfaces it, they remove a thin layer from the underside and attach a plastic button to that surface. The button then moves along the groove in the new thighbone component. If the surgeon leaves the kneecap unresurfaced, its own underside moves against that new groove. [1][2]

Practice varies. NICE recommends offering resurfacing during a first planned total knee replacement. The AAOS guideline reports similar outcomes overall with and without resurfacing. A randomised study of 250 knees using one implant design found similar overall function and front-of-knee pain at its follow-up, with less stair pain in the resurfaced group. The study’s findings apply to the design and patients studied. [3][4][5]

You can ask what the surgeon looks for when choosing how to treat the kneecap and whether that choice might change once they see it directly. At the next visit, ask how the kneecap is moving in the new groove if stairs or getting out of a chair are still difficult. [1][2][3]

ZappyIf my kneecap keeps its own surface, is it still a total knee replacement?
Prof. BoltYes. The operation still resurfaces both sides where the thighbone meets the shinbone. Ask your surgeon to explain the separate decision about the kneecap. [1][2][3]
06

How do the new parts stay in place?

Some components are fixed with a material called bone cement. Cementless components have a surface that allows bone to grow onto or into it over time. Some operations use a combination. Ask how your surgeon plans to fix each part and what factors shaped that choice, such as the implant design and the condition of your bone. [2][4]

The surgeon also checks how stable the knee is. The ligaments along each side usually still help steady it. Depending on the design and the knee, the surgeon may keep the posterior cruciate ligament, a ligament deep inside the joint, or use an implant that helps do its job. Tell the surgeon if you’ve had previous knee surgery or a ligament injury. [1][2]

The menisci, the two pads between the thighbone and shinbone, are usually removed during total replacement. So is the anterior cruciate ligament, often shortened to ACL. The shape of the components and the balance of the remaining tissues help give the new joint stability. If the knee is unusually unstable, the surgeon may discuss a design that provides more support. [2][8]

You don’t need to remember every technical term. Ask what design is planned, why it suits your knee and what might change the plan during the operation. Afterwards, you can ask what was used and get a copy of the implant details for your own records. [2][3]

Keep those details with your discharge papers. If you see a different clinician later, you can show them which knee was operated on, when the operation took place and which components were used. [2][3]

07

What does the implant do, and what will I work on?

The operation gives the joint new surfaces. After surgery, the muscles around the knee still need to regain strength and control. The team can show you how to stand, walk and practise moving the knee within the plan they’ve given you. The amount of bend people regain varies, and recovery takes time. [1][3][6]

Meera brings the discussion back to her own life. She wants to walk to the vegetable stall and get up from her low sofa. She asks which of those tasks she can practise early and how she and the team will know when to move on to something harder. If there’s an activity you especially want back, tell the team before surgery. They can explain what might be realistic. [1][3][6]

At follow-up, mention how well you can straighten the knee, walk and manage stairs, and what still limits you. The surgeon and physiotherapist can use that information alongside the examination and any imaging. A question about how the parts look on the X-ray is also a chance to ask how the whole knee is working. [1][3][6]

The first weeks can feel different from what you imagined when you held the model. A model moves easily in your hand. Your knee has muscles, a healing wound and swelling to contend with. Ask your team what progress they expect at your stage and who to call if everyday movement is getting harder. [1][6][7]

08

When should I call the surgical team?

Before you leave hospital, ask who to contact if you’re worried about the wound, pain or swelling. If you develop a fever, wound drainage, new calf pain or swelling, or redness or swelling that is getting worse after the operation, contact your surgical team or get urgent medical help. These symptoms need assessment for complications such as infection or a blood clot. [3][7]

Call your local emergency number for sudden breathlessness or chest pain after surgery, especially if you also have leg pain or swelling. These symptoms can indicate a blood clot in the lungs. [7]

ZappyWhat can I ask at the next visit if I’m still unsure what went in?
Prof. BoltAsk the team to show you the thighbone component, the shinbone component and where the plastic sits. Ask whether your kneecap was resurfaced and how the components were fixed. [1][2][3]

The fridge-door cheat sheet

What gets replaced?

Metal and plastic resurface the contact areas at the ends of the thighbone and shinbone. [1][2]

What happens to my kneecap?

The kneecap stays. If it is resurfaced, a plastic button is attached to its underside. [1][2]

What should I ask my surgeon?

Ask which components were used, how they were fixed and whether your kneecap was resurfaced. [2][3]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. After a knee replacement, get urgent medical help for fever, wound drainage, new calf pain or swelling, or redness or swelling that’s getting worse. Call your local emergency number for sudden breathlessness or chest pain. [7]
