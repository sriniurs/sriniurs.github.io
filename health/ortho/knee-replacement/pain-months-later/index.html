PAIN MONTHS LATER

Why Does My Knee Still Hurt Months After Replacement?

How the team investigates pain that persists or returns, and what to bring to your appointment.

THE PAIN PATTERNEXAM AND TESTSA FOLLOW-UP PLAN

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The stairs that still hurt

Four months after his knee replacement, Dev can walk farther than he did before surgery. Coming down the stairs still brings on pain around the front of the knee. He sometimes takes the lift to avoid it. He had expected some discomfort early on, but this is starting to affect what he does. At his review he tells the team which step hurts and shows them how he comes down the stairs.

Recovery takes time, and some people continue to have pain months after a total knee replacement. If pain keeps interfering with sleep, walking or everyday activities, or if it returns after a period of improvement, tell your surgical team. The timing and location help them decide what to look at first. Increasing redness or swelling, fever, drainage or sudden severe pain calls for urgent assessment. [1][2][6][7]

02

Which details can tell the team more?

Start with the timeline. Has the pain been there since the operation? Is it similar to the pain you had before? Did it ease and then return? Say whether it started after a twist, fall or change in activity. Tell the team if it occurs with the first few steps, on stairs, during prolonged standing or when you are resting. That description is the starting point for an examination. [1][4][8]

If you can, point to the place where it feels strongest. Say if the knee swells after activity, gives way or catches. Mention burning, tingling or sensitivity to light touch, and whether discomfort runs down the leg or comes with back or groin pain. Write down any change to the routine that now makes the pain easier or harder to manage. [1][4][8]

Dev brings a short note. It says he can walk to the shops, the pain is strongest going downstairs and he sometimes wakes if he rolls onto that side. He also brings his medicine list and notes what happened when the therapist adjusted his exercises. [1][2]

If you cannot remember the exact date, use something you do remember. You might say the pain started in the week you stopped using the walking stick, or that you first noticed it on a trip. Tell the team if it follows every outing or mainly turns up after a longer day. A short description tied to a real activity can be easier to interpret than a pain score on its own. [1][4]

03

Could the replacement or surrounding tissues be involved?

There are several possibilities. The team may check for loosening, where a component is no longer securely fixed, or instability, where the knee feels unreliable as you put weight on it. They may also look at how the kneecap moves. The tissues around the replacement, including muscles, tendons and scar tissue, can contribute to pain or make certain movements more difficult. These problems can feel similar during everyday activities. [1][3][4][8]

Your clinician may watch how you rise from a chair and how you load the leg on a step. They can check the knee’s bend and straightening, whether it swells, which areas are tender and whether it feels stable when examined. If your symptoms only occur at a particular point in a movement, show them that movement when it is safe to do so. [1][3][8]

04

Can pain come from the hip or back?

Pain felt at the knee sometimes starts elsewhere. Hip arthritis can cause pain that reaches the thigh or knee. A problem affecting a nerve in the lower back can cause pain or other symptoms farther down the leg. Both knee and hip or back problems can be present at the same time. The team may examine those areas if your history or the knee exam gives them reason to do so. [1][4][8]

Tell the clinician if you have difficulty putting on shoes, pain in the groin, back pain, numbness or tingling, or symptoms that change with sitting or standing. They may ask whether the pain you have now resembles your pain before surgery. Each answer gives them a better idea of where to examine and what to test. [1][4][8]

Dev also mentions that his scar feels sore when fabric rubs against it. The clinician asks whether that is the same pain he feels on the stairs. Describing those experiences separately helps the team choose what to assess during the visit. [1][8]

05

Why start with an X-ray?

For a painful knee replacement, X-rays are usually the first imaging test. They show where the components sit and can help the team look for changes around them, a fracture or signs of loosening. Earlier films can help the clinician tell whether an appearance has changed. Bring information about where the operation was done and where your previous images can be obtained. [1][3]

If the initial assessment leaves a specific question, the clinician may arrange another test. For example, a CT scan can help in some cases when the team needs to assess component position. Ultrasound or other imaging may be useful for selected problems with the tissues around the replacement. The choice depends on what the examination and initial scans suggest. [3]

If you’re referred for imaging, ask what question the test is meant to answer. That can make it easier to understand why the team has requested one test at this stage, and why they might ask to see you again before deciding about another. [1][3]

Prof. BoltTell the team what you are still struggling with. They can decide whether a further exam, a different test or a treatment trial would help. [1][3]
06

How does the team check for infection?

Infection around a replacement can cause pain. If you have increasing redness, swelling or warmth with worsening pain, new drainage, fever or chills, seek urgent help. Some infections can be more subtle, so the team may consider infection during the assessment even if you feel well overall. [3][5][6]

Depending on what they find, the team may order blood tests for signs of inflammation. If more information is needed, a clinician may use a needle to take a joint fluid sample. This is called an aspiration. The lab can look for clues such as a raised number of inflammatory cells or bacteria that grow in culture. The team puts the findings together with your symptoms and examination. Ask when and how you will hear the results. [3][5]

Tell the team if you have taken antibiotics recently or have had another infection. These details may matter when they interpret the tests. If new warning signs develop while you are waiting for an appointment or test result, use the urgent contact route. Sudden breathlessness or chest pain needs emergency help. [5][6]

If you’ve had fluid taken from the knee before, mention when and where it was done. The surgeon may want to compare the results or ask the previous team for their records, especially if the earlier assessment took place at another hospital. [3][5]

07

What happens if the first visit leaves questions?

A review can end with a plan even if the team needs more time to identify the cause. They might adjust rehabilitation, arrange tests, ask a clinician with another specialty to see you or review pain management. Ask who will coordinate the next step and when you will hear back. If another operation comes up, ask what problem it would address and how the team determined that it was contributing to your pain. [1][3][8]

In the UK STAR trial, 363 people with troublesome pain three months after knee replacement were assigned to usual care alone or usual care plus a structured assessment and follow-up. At one year after assignment, the group offered the additional care reported slightly less pain and slightly less interference with daily life on average. Follow-up was part of the intervention, as were referrals based on what the assessment found. [1]

Dev books a follow-up and asks whether he should keep doing the steps exercise in the meantime. He leaves with a plan for the activities he can continue and a date to discuss any test results. [1][2][3]

08

What should I do next?

If pain keeps getting in the way, contact the team that did your surgery or ask your usual clinician to help arrange an orthopaedic review. Bring the date of surgery, a brief pain timeline, a list of treatment you’ve tried and one or two activities you want to get back to. Ask what the team is checking first, which changes need a call sooner and when someone will reassess your progress. [1][2][3]

Dev’s aim is to use the stairs without planning his day around the lift. That gives him and the team a way to talk about the effect of pain and judge whether the next step is helping. If the pain changes before his review, he knows whom to call. [1][2][6]

When should I ask for a review?

Contact the team if pain is limiting sleep or daily activity, has stalled despite recovery work, or has returned after improvement. [1][2][3]

What should I bring?

Bring a short symptom timeline and your medication list. Mention swelling, falls, giving way and any hip, thigh or back symptoms. [1][4][8]

When is the pain urgent?

Get urgent help for sudden severe pain, inability to bear weight, worsening swelling or redness, fever, wound drainage or new calf symptoms. Call emergency services for sudden breathlessness or chest pain. [6][7]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Ask for a review if pain persists, returns or limits daily life. Get urgent medical help for sudden severe knee pain, inability to bear weight, rapidly worsening redness or swelling, fever or chills, new wound drainage or new calf pain or swelling. Call your local emergency number for sudden breathlessness or chest pain. [1][6][7]
