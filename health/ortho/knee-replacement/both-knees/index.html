BOTH KNEES

If Both My Knees Hurt, Should I Replace Them Together or One at a Time?

What changes when both knees are replaced together, how to weigh the risks and what recovery at home may look like.

ONE OPERATION OR TWOTHE FIRST DAYS AT HOMEWEIGHING THE RISKS

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

“Could we do both while I’m there?”

Ramesh puts his right foot on the first step, grips the rail and draws his left foot up beside it. Both knees ache, but the left one usually makes him stop. At his appointment he asks whether both knees could be replaced in the same hospital stay. He lives upstairs. His daughter can stay for a few days, and he likes the idea of arranging help once.

If both knees are suitable for replacement, the team may discuss operating on both during one anaesthetic or doing one knee first and considering the second later. Doing both together means one hospital admission for the operations, with both knees recovering at the same time. Staging separates the operations and the early recovery periods. The decision depends on each knee, your health and the help you’ll have after surgery. [1][2][3]

ZappyWould one hospital stay make the whole thing easier?
Prof. BoltIt simplifies some arrangements. Ask what the first days at home would be like with two newly operated knees. [1][4]
02

Do both knees need replacing?

The surgeon assesses each knee. Both may look arthritic on X-rays, but one may make it much harder to stand, sleep or walk. Tell the surgeon what each knee does on an ordinary day and which treatments have helped. Replacement becomes a discussion when symptoms substantially limit daily life and suitable nonsurgical care has been insufficient or is unsuitable. [2][3]

Ramesh’s left knee is the one that makes him stop on the stairs. The right knee aches after errands but still lets him walk to the shops. He asks whether he can deal with the left knee first and reassess the right when he has recovered. If one knee is still manageable, continuing treatment on that side is an option to discuss. [2][3]

Describe each knee separately at the appointment. You might say, “The left knee wakes me, and the right one hurts when I get up from a low chair.” If you sometimes feel one leg give way, say which one and what you were doing. Those details help the team understand what you need each leg to do during recovery. [2][3][4]

03

What would one operation for both knees mean?

Replacing both knees during one anaesthetic is called simultaneous bilateral knee replacement. The hospital plans one admission and both knees begin rehabilitation together. This can be convenient for someone who would otherwise need to arrange two admissions, time away from work twice and two periods of help at home. There is still a substantial recovery to plan for. [1][4]

Both legs can be sore and weak in the early days. Ask the team how they would teach you to get out of bed, rise from the toilet and walk with an aid. At home, count the stairs you must climb and look at where you can sit down safely. Some people will need more help or a different discharge plan. [1][4]

Think through the day after you get home. Where will you have breakfast? How far is the toilet from the bed? Can your helper bring meals while you’re getting used to the walking aid? These questions let the hospital team plan for the home you actually live in. [1][4]

ZappyCould my daughter help me up the stairs on the day I come home?
Prof. BoltTell the hospital about the stairs before surgery. Practise with the physiotherapist and ask what help or equipment you’ll need. [1][4]
04

What changes if I do one knee at a time?

With staged replacement, the team operates on one knee first. You then recover before deciding with the surgeon when to do the other, if it still needs surgery. During the first recovery the other leg may help with standing and walking. If that knee is also very painful, tell the physiotherapist so they can plan how you’ll manage transfers and stairs. [1][2][4]

Two operations generally mean arranging two hospital stays, two anaesthetics and two periods of early rehabilitation. There can also be advantages to having time between them. You can see what help you actually needed, work on strength and review how much the other knee is still limiting you. Ask what could change the plan for the second knee. [1][4][7]

Ramesh asks which side would go first. He and the surgeon talk about the knee causing the greatest disability and how well the other leg could support him early on. Bring a question like this if your knees bother you in different ways. The order matters most when it helps solve a practical problem in your own recovery. [1][2][4]

THE DEEP DIVEHow the team weighs risk, timing and support at home
05

What does the evidence say about risk?

One large US study matched people having both knees replaced together with people having staged operations. In that study, the simultaneous group had more in-hospital complications, even when the researchers counted both hospital stays for the staged group. It was a study of hospital records, so it could account for measured differences between the groups, with other differences still possible. [5]

A smaller 2024 study of patients selected for bilateral replacement found no clear difference in complications or reported knee outcomes between the two approaches. Results can vary with whom hospitals select and what the researchers count. The AAOS guideline advises caution with simultaneous surgery and recommends avoiding it in patients at high risk of heart or lung complications. [6][7]

When you hear a risk figure, ask what time period it covers. A study of problems during the hospital stay answers a different question from one that follows people after discharge. Ask whether the comparison includes both operations and whether the people studied had health concerns similar to yours. [5][6][7]

ZappyCan two hospital stays mean twice the risk?
Prof. BoltAsk your team to compare one combined operation with the full plan for two operations. Different complications have different risks, and your health affects the comparison. [1][5][7]
06

How will the team decide what’s suitable for me?

The surgeon and anaesthesia team review your health and assess both knees. Tell them about heart or lung problems, a previous blood clot, anaemia, and any difficulties with an earlier anaesthetic. Bring the medicines you take, including blood thinners. Ask what needs checking or treating before an operation and get clear instructions about any changes to your medicines. [1][2][7]

Ask for the risks in terms that make sense to you. Which complications are more likely in your case? What would the hospital do to reduce them? What would change the recommendation? Your surgeon may discuss blood loss, clots and heart or lung complications alongside the chance of pain relief and better movement. [1][2][7][8]

A plan that works for someone who has a bedroom and bathroom on the ground floor may be harder for someone who lives alone upstairs. Tell the team how things are set up at home, how much help is available and what equipment you already have. Ask who can help you work out the gaps. [1][4]

07

How soon could I have the second knee done?

If you choose staged surgery, ask when the team will review you for the second knee. They can check wound healing, walking and strength, and see whether any complication or new health issue needs attention. Studies have compared different intervals, but they can’t tell you the exact right date. The team can explain what they need to see before recommending another operation. [1][4][7]

At his follow-up, Ramesh wants to answer two questions. How well can he move and manage stairs after the first operation? How much does the other knee still interfere with the things he wants to do? Those answers will help him decide whether to book the next operation or continue with the treatment he is using for the other knee. [2][3][4]

Ask how much time you should set aside for each recovery and what happens if plans change. If work or family responsibilities make a particular month difficult, talk about that before booking. It helps to have a follow-up plan for both knees, including the one you may be leaving for later. [1][4]

If a second date has already been pencilled in, ask what would prompt the team to reschedule it. Tell them if the first knee still limits basic tasks or if the support you expected at home is no longer available. [1][4]

Before you leave the hospital after the first operation, ask who will review the other knee. If it starts to limit your exercises or feels less steady when you walk, tell the team rather than waiting for the next operation date. They can help you adapt the recovery plan while they reassess how the second knee is doing. [2][3][4]

08

What should I take to the decision appointment?

Bring a short note on each knee, your medicine list and your questions about home. Ask whether both knees currently meet the criteria for surgery, what the team recommends for you, and how much help you’d need in the first days with either plan. If you’ve discussed costs or time off work, check what would be involved across the entire course of care. [1][2][3][4]

After knee replacement, get urgent medical help for new calf pain or swelling, fever, wound drainage, or redness or swelling that’s getting worse. Call your local emergency number for sudden shortness of breath or chest pain. [8]

ZappyWhat if I’m still unsure after hearing the options?
Prof. BoltAsk what a one-knee plan would look like for you and what would make the team consider both together. You can discuss the choices with your family and come back with questions. [1][2]

The fridge-door cheat sheet

Do both knees need surgery?

Ask the surgeon to assess each knee and how much it limits you. One knee can be treated first and the other reassessed. [2][3]

What’s the practical difference?

One combined operation starts recovery for both knees at once. Staged surgery separates the operations and early recovery periods. [1][4]

What should I ask before booking?

Ask how your health affects the risks, how you’ll manage at home, and what would guide the timing of a second operation. [1][4][7]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. After either operation, get urgent medical help for fever, wound drainage, new calf pain or swelling, or redness or swelling that is getting worse. Call your local emergency number for sudden shortness of breath or chest pain. [8]
