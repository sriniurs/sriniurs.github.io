THE FIRST ASSISTED WALK

Why Am I Asked to Stand and Walk So Soon After Surgery?

What the team checks before you stand, how a walking frame helps and what the first few steps are for.

READY TO STAND?USING A WALKING FRAMEGETTING HOME SAFELY

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The frame beside the bed

On the afternoon of her knee replacement, Shobha sees a physiotherapist wheel a walking frame over to her bed. She had expected to stay tucked under the blanket until tomorrow. Her knee feels heavy and she’s worried that the first step will hurt. The physiotherapist explains that they’ll start by seeing how she feels sitting up.

The first standing and walking session helps you begin using the leg again and lets the team see what assistance you need. A few supported steps can be enough to start. NICE recommends offering rehabilitation on the day of a planned knee replacement if possible, and within 24 hours. The team adjusts what you do to how alert, steady and comfortable you are. [1][2][3]

02

What is the first walk meant to achieve?

Getting out of bed is part of learning to manage everyday tasks again. It gives you and the team a chance to practise standing, shifting weight and taking a few steps with support. They can see where you need help, such as moving your leg to the edge of the bed or turning to sit in a chair. That helps them plan the next session. [1][2][3]

Early movement also forms part of recovery care. Moving and walking are among the measures used to reduce the risk of blood clots. You may also be given a blood-thinning medicine or other measures such as compression, depending on the plan your team has made for you. Follow their instructions even if you’re already walking. [3][4][5]

Shobha had imagined being asked to walk the full length of the corridor. The physiotherapist starts with a goal that matters to her right away: getting from bed to the chair beside it. Once the team sees how that goes, they can decide whether she’s ready to do more. [1][2][3]

Practising those small tasks also gives the team information for discharge. If you can stand with the frame but need help sitting down, they have something specific to work on with you. They can also ask whether there will be someone at home who can help in the same way. [1][2][3]

03

How does the team know I’m ready to stand?

Before helping you up, staff check how you’re recovering from the operation and anaesthetic. They may check your blood pressure, whether you’re alert and how well you can feel and move your legs. They ask about pain and nausea. If you’ve had a spinal anaesthetic or a nerve block, the team checks how it’s affecting movement and feeling before you put weight on the leg. [1][2][3][8]

Ask how much weight you’re allowed to put on the operated leg. The surgical team gives instructions for your operation. Tell the physiotherapist if another joint hurts, if you normally use a walking aid or if one leg was weak before surgery. Those details can change what support is needed. [2][3][5]

Prof. BoltUse the call bell and wait for staff to show you how much help you need. The first attempt is a supervised check. [2][3]
04

Why might I sit at the edge of the bed first?

Some people feel dizzy, faint or nauseated when they first go from lying down to sitting or standing after surgery. A small study after knee replacement found that pausing in a seated position before standing reduced these symptoms in the patients studied. Your team will guide the change of position and decide when to move on. [2][6]

Shobha sits with her feet on the floor and tells the physiotherapist she feels a little light-headed. They pause and check how she feels. Tell the team as soon as you feel unsteady, sweaty, sick or faint. They can help you sit or lie down safely, assess what’s happening and decide when to try again. [2][6]

If you’re still too dizzy or your leg feels too numb or weak, the first walk may be delayed. The team can reassess you and help you start once it’s safe. Ask what you can do in the meantime and what they’ll check before coming back. [1][2][3]

Tell staff if you’ve felt faint when standing before, particularly if it happened after another operation. If you feel well sitting and only notice symptoms once you stand, tell them then. How you feel can change during the session, and they can adjust the plan as they go. [2][6][8]

THE DEEP DIVEFrom a few supported steps to getting around at home
05

What does the walking frame do?

The frame gives you a stable support while you learn how to stand and step with the operated leg. The physiotherapist selects and adjusts an aid for you. They show you how to stand up, where to put your hands and how to move the frame and your feet. They’ll stay close or arrange more help if you need it. [2][3][7]

On her first attempt, Shobha concentrates on one instruction at a time. The physiotherapist helps her stand and checks that she feels steady. She takes a few steps to the chair, then asks how to turn and sit down safely. Getting back down safely is part of the session. Later the same day, she asks whether she should still call for help before getting up. [2][3][7]

You can ask the physiotherapist to watch you do the same transfer again if it felt confusing. Tell them which part was difficult, such as keeping the frame close as you turned or knowing when you were near enough to the chair. Practise with the aid the team has selected for you. [2][3][7]

06

How will the team manage pain while I move?

The team can help time your prescribed pain relief around rehabilitation. Tell them where it hurts, how strongly and what happens when you try to move. Mention nausea, dizziness or unusual sleepiness as well, since those can affect how safely you can stand. Your team can review the plan if pain or side effects are getting in the way. [1][2][3]

You may feel soreness around the knee during early movement. If the pain suddenly changes, the leg feels as if it will give way or you feel faint, tell the person helping you straight away. They can stop, help you sit and decide what to check. Ask the team what sensations they expect during your exercises and when to call them. [2][3][4]

Shobha asks whether there’s a better time for her next walk. She also tells staff that standing was easier than getting back down to the chair. A detail like that helps the physiotherapist focus the next session on the part you found hardest. [2][3]

07

What happens after those first few steps?

The next sessions can include walking a little farther, practising getting on and off the toilet and learning exercises for home. If you have stairs, tell your team how many there are and whether there’s a handrail. They can practise stairs with you when appropriate and discuss what help or equipment you might need. [1][2][3]

Before discharge, ask which aid to use, whether you still need someone nearby and how to increase walking at home. Your instructions may differ from those of someone else on the ward. Short, regular walks can be part of the plan, alongside rest, exercises and the other advice you’ve been given. The team can tell you whom to contact if you find the plan difficult. [2][3][7]

For Shobha, the first milestone is reaching a chair safely. Later she wants to manage the short walk to her bathroom. She writes that down so she and her physiotherapist can make a plan for the route she’ll actually use at home. [1][2][3]

If someone will be helping you at home, ask whether they can be there when the team explains the plan. Show them where the walking aid will be kept and talk about which tasks you can do with it and which ones you’ll need help with. That can make the first trip to the bathroom or kitchen easier to organise. [2][3][7]

Ask for the instructions in writing, especially if the plan will change over the next few days. It helps to know how the team will decide when you can walk farther or switch to a different aid. If you’re finding a task harder at home than it was on the ward, describe the setup and ask for advice. [1][2][3]

08

When should I call for help?

While you’re in hospital, tell staff promptly about sudden severe pain, a new feeling of weakness or numbness, or dizziness when you try to stand. Before you leave, get the surgical team’s contact details and ask who to call outside normal hours. [2][3][4]

After discharge, get urgent medical help for new calf pain or swelling, fever, wound drainage, or redness, swelling or pain that’s getting worse. Call your local emergency number for sudden breathlessness or chest pain, especially with leg pain or swelling. [4][5]
