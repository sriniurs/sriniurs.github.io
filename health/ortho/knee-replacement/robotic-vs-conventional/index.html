ROBOTIC ASSISTANCE

Robotic vs. Conventional Knee Replacement: What Difference Does It Make for Me?

What the robot helps the surgeon do, what studies found about recovery and what to ask about the cost.

WHAT THE ROBOT DOESPAIN AND RECOVERYCOMPARING COSTS

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The two estimates on the kitchen table

Dev has agreed to talk about total knee replacement. At home he spreads two estimates on the table. One says “conventional.” The other says “robotic-assisted.” His son asks whether the more expensive one would get him back to walking to the shops sooner. Dev realises that neither estimate tells him what the robot actually does.

Both operations replace the damaged surfaces of the knee. Robotic assistance changes how the surgeon plans and carries out parts of the operation, particularly how the bone is prepared for the implant. It can help the surgeon follow the planned cuts and implant position. For patients, the important questions are how the knee feels and works afterwards, what risks apply, and what the extra technology costs. [1][2][3]

ZappyDoes the robot decide how to operate?
Prof. BoltThe surgeon makes the decisions and supervises the technology. Ask which parts of the operation the system helps with. [1][2]
02

How does the surgeon prepare the knee in each operation?

In conventional knee replacement, the surgeon uses measurements and physical guides to prepare the ends of the thighbone and shinbone. The guides help set the position and angle of the cuts. The surgeon checks how the trial components fit, how the knee bends and straightens, and whether it feels stable before fitting the final components. [1][2]

With robotic assistance, the surgeon uses a computer model to plan where the components will sit. Depending on the system, the robot may guide a cutting tool or help position a cutting guide that the surgeon uses. Some systems can restrict where the cutting tool moves. The surgeon reviews the plan and can adjust it based on what they find during the operation. [1][2]

Dev asks to see what the components would look like. The familiar pieces are still there: a cap on the thighbone, a component on the shinbone and a plastic bearing between them. Whether the kneecap is resurfaced is a separate decision. Ask which implant the surgeon plans to use and why it suits your knee. [1][2][7]

If the surgeon shows you a screen with a model of the knee, ask them to point out which information they use. For example, they may show where a component is planned to sit or what they’ll check as they bend the knee. That gives you a clearer picture of what the equipment contributes. [1][2]

03

Will I need a CT scan or extra incisions?

Some systems use a CT scan taken before surgery to build a three-dimensional model of the knee. Other systems build their map from points the surgeon records during the operation. Ask which method your hospital uses, whether you’ll need a separate appointment and whether the scan is included in the estimate. A CT scan uses X-rays. [1][2]

Many systems also use small pins in the thighbone and shinbone to hold tracking markers. A camera follows the markers so the computer knows where the bones are as the leg moves. Depending on the system and how the pins are placed, you may have small additional cuts in the skin. Pin-site problems can include infection or, rarely, a fracture through a pin hole. [1][2][8]

ZappyWill the pins stay in my leg?
Prof. BoltThe tracking pins are taken out at the end of the operation. Your team can show you where the pin sites will be and explain how to look after them. [1][2]
04

What does “more precise” tell me?

Precision usually describes how closely the cuts or the implant position match the surgeon’s plan. The plan itself includes choices about how the components line up and how the knee is balanced. A closer match is a technical result that can be measured during surgery or on an image. [1][2][3]

Walking to the bus stop, climbing stairs and sleeping comfortably are different outcomes. To find out if a technique improves those things, researchers need to ask patients and follow their recovery. Dev writes down the two things he cares about most: walking to the shops and sitting through a meal without needing to straighten his knee. [3][4]

If a brochure promises better alignment, you can ask what was measured and whether people also noticed a difference in their daily lives. Ask how long they were followed and which robotic system was studied. Different systems and surgical plans can make it hard to apply one result to every hospital. [2][3][4]

Ask the surgeon what a change in the plan would mean in practice. They may describe positioning a component differently after checking how tight the tissues feel when the knee is bent or straight. Both techniques include checks during surgery. The equipment changes how some measurements are made and how the plan is carried out. [1][2]

05

Will it hurt less or get me walking sooner?

There are studies that suggest an early advantage for some patients. The ROAM randomised trial found a greater average improvement in pain at two months with robotic assistance. By six months, the difference in average pain improvement was uncertain, and the groups did not have a meaningful difference in knee function. That leaves room for individual experiences to vary. [5]

The 2026 RACER-Knee trial randomly assigned 339 people to robotic-arm-assisted or conventional total knee replacement. At one year it found no meaningful difference in how aware people were of their knee during daily life, its main measure. The groups had similar safety results during the trial. The trial tested a particular system in routine UK practice. It did not establish how long newer implants will last. [4]

Whatever technique you choose, ask what the first walk will involve and how the team will manage pain so you can take part in rehabilitation. The recovery plan covers walking aids, exercises and how you’ll manage everyday tasks. It can be adjusted as you recover. [1][9]

ZappySo can I count on being back at work sooner with the robot?
Prof. BoltAsk for a recovery estimate for your own job and knee. Early results from a study can’t tell you when you’ll be ready. [4][5][9]
06

Will the replacement last longer?

Implant life is usually studied by counting how many knees need a further operation to change or remove components. That operation is called a revision. A longer-lasting replacement is an important goal, but it takes years of follow-up to test it. An early X-ray can show position. It can’t show how a replacement will perform for that person in ten years. [3][6][7]

One randomised trial that followed patients for an average of 13 years did not find better implant survival or knee function with the robotic system it studied. That system was an earlier design. Longer follow-up is still needed to learn whether particular newer systems change the chance of needing revision. [3][6]

Ask your surgeon what is known about the implant itself, as well as about the tools used to place it. If a predicted benefit is based on short-term research, ask which outcomes the team can discuss with confidence and which they are still studying. [2][3][6]

07

What am I paying for?

Ask for an itemised estimate for each approach. Check whether the price includes any planning scan, the implant, the hospital stay, and follow-up or physiotherapy. If you have insurance, ask what it covers and what you would pay yourself. Look for differences in what’s included before comparing the totals. [3][4]

In RACER-Knee, the robotic approach cost more over the first year in the UK setting studied. Those costs do not tell Dev what his own hospital will charge. They do give him a useful question to ask: if the estimate is higher, what would the additional spending cover and what benefit would his team expect for his particular knee? [3][4]

It’s also reasonable to ask how often the team uses each technique and what they would do if the robotic system were unavailable on the day. Ask whether the recommendation would change because of something about your knee, such as its shape or a previous operation. A clear answer helps you compare the plans. [1][2][3]

Dev takes both estimates to the consultation. He circles the items where the descriptions differ and asks the hospital to explain each one. He also checks who will arrange physiotherapy and what follow-up is included. Now he can compare the whole course of care. [3][4][9]

08

What should I ask before I choose?

Dev brings his estimates and his list of daily goals. He asks the surgeon what would be done differently with each method, what either choice would mean for his rehabilitation and whether he would need help at home for the first few days. He wants to understand what can reasonably improve, how soon, and what risks he should be ready for. [1][3][4][9]

If a friend recovered quickly with one technique, tell the team what impressed you about their recovery. Then ask whether your starting strength, other health conditions or home setup might make your experience different. [1][9]

The usual risks of knee replacement, including infection and blood clots, apply with either approach. Before going home, get a number to call if the wound starts draining, redness or swelling worsens, you develop a fever, or your calf becomes newly painful or swollen. Those symptoms need urgent medical assessment. [1][10]

After either type of knee replacement, call your local emergency number for sudden breathlessness or chest pain, especially if you also have leg pain or swelling. [10]

ZappyWhat question should I put at the top of my list?
Prof. Bolt“For my knee, what would robotic assistance change about the operation, my recovery or the cost?” [1][3][4]

The fridge-door cheat sheet

Who does the operation?

The surgeon plans and supervises the operation. The robotic system assists with steps such as guiding bone preparation. [1][2]

Will it make my recovery faster?

Some studies find differences early on. Ask what was measured and whether the benefits lasted. [4][5]

What should I compare?

Ask what the technique would change for your knee, what recovery involves, and what each estimate includes. [1][3][4][9]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. After either type of knee replacement, get urgent medical help for fever, wound drainage, new calf pain or swelling, or redness or swelling that’s getting worse. Call your local emergency number for sudden breathlessness or chest pain. [10]
