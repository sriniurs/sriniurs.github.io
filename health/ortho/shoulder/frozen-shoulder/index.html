FROZEN SHOULDER

Why Can’t I Reach Behind My Back Anymore?

Why a stiff shoulder makes dressing harder, what the clinician checks and what may help you move more comfortably.

THE TIGHT CAPSULETHE MOVEMENT CHECKGETTING MOVEMENT BACK

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The apron string she couldn’t reach

Leela puts on her apron to make dosas. She reaches around to tie the strings, the same way she has done for years. Her right hand gets as far as her hip. She twists at the waist, but the shoulder still won’t turn. Over the past few weeks she has also started putting that arm into her kurta sleeve first. At night, rolling onto her right side wakes her.

One possible reason for a steadily shrinking reach is frozen shoulder, also called adhesive capsulitis. The sleeve of tissue around the ball-and-socket joint becomes stiff and tight. That can make it hard to turn the arm, even if somebody else supports it. Reaching behind your back takes several movements at once, so you may first notice the loss of motion when dressing or washing. [1][2][3]

Leela can still stir the batter with her elbow near her side. Her trouble begins when the upper arm needs to turn or move farther from her body. She tells her clinician which tasks changed first and how the problem has developed over several weeks. [1][3]

02

What is getting tight inside the joint?

The shoulder’s ball is the rounded top of the upper arm bone. The socket is a shallow part of the shoulder blade. A sleeve of strong tissue called the joint capsule surrounds the ball and socket. The capsule normally has enough give for the ball to turn as you lift your arm or slide your hand behind your waist. [1][2][3]

With frozen shoulder, the capsule becomes inflamed and can thicken and tighten. Movement can hurt before the stiffness becomes obvious. Later, even a gently supported arm may stop sooner than expected. Reaching farther by leaning or twisting your trunk can disguise how much motion the shoulder itself has lost. [1][2][3]

You can mention where your hand gets to when you dress, but a behind-the-back reach by itself can’t identify the cause. The clinician checks several movements, especially your ability to turn the upper arm outward with your elbow by your side. That outward turn is often particularly limited in frozen shoulder. [1][2]

03

Why has it happened to me?

Frozen shoulder often begins without a clear injury. It is more common in midlife and in people with diabetes or certain thyroid conditions. Shoulder stiffness can also develop after an injury or operation, especially when the arm has been kept still for a while. The details matter because the clinician may need to consider the original injury or operation as part of the assessment. [1][2][3]

Tell the clinician if you’ve had shoulder surgery, a fracture or a period in a sling. Mention diabetes or thyroid disease and bring your current medicine list. If you’re still recovering from an injury or surgery, check with the treating team before changing the exercises or movement limits they gave you. [1][2][3]

Leela can’t remember a fall or a particular reach that started it. That’s a useful detail. She can also remember that the ache at night came before she struggled to tie the apron. Together those details help the clinician understand how the problem has changed. [1][3]

04

Why does it hurt less but still feel stuck?

Early on, pain may dominate and movement can get steadily harder. Later the ache may ease while the shoulder stays stiff. In the recovery period, movement can gradually return. You may hear these periods called freezing, frozen and thawing. People do not move through them on a fixed timetable, and pain and stiffness can overlap. [1][2][3]

Recovery is often measured in months and sometimes takes years. Many people improve substantially, though some have lasting stiffness. A useful change might be sleeping through the night, washing your hair with both hands or getting a little farther into a sleeve. Those gains can matter while the behind-the-back reach is still limited. [2][3]

At a follow-up, describe what is hardest now. If sleep is the main problem, the discussion may focus on pain relief. If the pain has eased and dressing is still awkward, your physiotherapist can review how you’re working on movement and how the shoulder responds afterwards. [1][2][3]

THE DEEP DIVEFrom the shoulder examination to treatment choices
05

What will the clinician check?

The clinician may watch you lift and turn your arm yourself and then gently move it while you relax. These are called active and passive movement. In frozen shoulder, both are limited. They may compare each side while keeping your elbow near your waist and gently turning your forearm outward. They also check the history for clues such as an injury, weakness or symptoms coming from the neck. [1][2]

Shoulder arthritis can also restrict both kinds of movement. An X-ray can help look for arthritis or a bone problem when the history or examination suggests one. Frozen shoulder is usually identified from the story and examination. Ultrasound or MRI can help investigate another suspected cause in selected cases. [1][2][3]

Bring an example of what you’ve lost. “I could fasten my apron last month, and now my hand stops at my hip” helps the clinician understand the change and gives you both something concrete to check again. [1][3]

Think of two other movements to mention. Can you turn your forearm out to reach for the seat belt? Can you raise your elbow enough to wash your hair? Some tasks use the shoulder in several directions. Giving examples lets the clinician separate the movements and check each one. [1][3]

06

What can help while the shoulder is sore?

Start with the parts of the day that are hardest. Leela ties her apron at the front and wears a loose shirt she can button. She puts the sore arm into its sleeve first. You might try supporting the arm on a pillow at night and keeping up gentle everyday movement within a manageable range. Ask a physiotherapist how far to move and how to adjust if a stretch makes the ache linger. [2][3][4]

Ask a pharmacist or clinician about pain relief that fits your health and other medicines. A clinician may also offer a corticosteroid injection into the joint for short-term relief, sometimes alongside physiotherapy. If you have diabetes, tell the clinician before the injection and ask about monitoring your blood sugar, which can rise temporarily. [1][2][4]

Agree on a practical goal for the next review, such as putting on a jacket with less help. Tell the clinician if the exercises make sleep more difficult or the shoulder stays sorer afterwards. The plan can change as pain settles and stiffness becomes the bigger problem. [1][2][3]

Ask the physiotherapist to show you a movement you can repeat at home and tell you what to do if it flares the pain. The best time for Leela to practise may be when she’s already moving around the kitchen. Her plan needs to fit her day as well as her shoulder. [2][3][4]

07

What if it stays stiff despite treatment?

If pain and stiffness still significantly affect daily life, ask for a review. A specialist may discuss hydrodilatation, where fluid is injected into the joint to stretch the capsule, often with a steroid. Some studies show short-term gains in movement or function. The longer-term advantage is less certain. [1][2]

Options in selected cases also include moving the shoulder under anaesthesia or releasing the tight capsule through keyhole surgery. The UK FROST trial compared these procedures with structured physiotherapy plus a steroid injection in 503 adults referred to hospital care. At one year, the differences in average shoulder scores fell short of what the researchers had defined as clinically important. Discuss the likely benefit, risks and rehabilitation for your circumstances. [1][5]

Leela’s review may end with a change to her exercises or a discussion about another treatment. Her priorities are sleeping more comfortably and dressing on her own. Those priorities help frame the decision alongside what the examination shows. [1][2]

08

When should I get help sooner?

Arrange an assessment if you’re gradually losing shoulder movement, if pain is worsening or if you can’t manage everyday tasks. You can bring notes on how long it has been going on, any injury or surgery, and what the shoulder does when you dress, reach or sleep. [3][4][6]

Seek urgent medical care if the pain starts after an injury, is suddenly severe, or you can’t move the arm. A shoulder that changes shape, a hot swollen joint with fever or feeling unwell, or persistent numbness also needs urgent attention. These symptoms can point to a problem that needs faster assessment. [6]

Call your local emergency number if shoulder or arm discomfort comes with chest pressure, shortness of breath, a cold sweat or feeling faint. These can be signs of a heart attack. [7]

Why is reaching behind my back harder?

A tight joint capsule can limit how far the upper arm turns. An examination can check which movements are restricted. [1][2]

What can I try while it’s sore?

Keep up gentle movement, adapt dressing and ask which stretches and pain relief suit you. [2][3][4]

When do I need help quickly?

Get urgent care for sudden severe pain, stiffness after injury, or a hot, swollen shoulder with fever. Call emergency services for possible heart attack symptoms. [6][7]
