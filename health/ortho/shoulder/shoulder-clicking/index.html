CLICKING AND CATCHING

Why Does My Shoulder Click When I Move It?

What a painless click can mean, why a painful catch deserves attention and when an examination or scan can help.

THE PAINLESS CLICKTHE PAINFUL CATCHWHEN TO GET HELP

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

One click, two very different mornings

For years, Asha has heard a little click when she pulls her hair into a ponytail. It has never hurt or stopped her finishing. One morning she reaches into the back of the wardrobe and feels a sharp catch deep in the shoulder. She lowers her arm, then tries a different angle. The arm moves again, but the catch returns when she pulls on a jacket. This feels different enough that she makes an appointment.

Shoulder noises are common. A painless click with easy movement often needs little attention. A new painful catch, a shoulder that gives way or a movement you can no longer finish deserves a closer look. The sound is only one part of the story. What the arm does at the same moment helps a clinician work out where to look. [1][2][3]

Prof. BoltTell the clinician which one is new and what happens when you hear or feel it. That difference is worth describing. [1][2]
02

Where can a click come from?

Think about reaching into a cupboard. The ball at the top of your upper arm turns in a socket on the shoulder blade. The shoulder blade itself moves over the ribs to help your hand go higher. Muscles and tendons guide these movements. A sound felt near the shoulder can come from more than one place, which is why listening for the click gives only a limited clue. [1][2][4]

There is a rim of firm tissue called the labrum around the socket. It helps deepen the socket, and one of the biceps tendons attaches near its top. An injury to the upper rim can sometimes cause painful catching or popping. A fall or a sudden pull can injure it, and the rim can also change with age. Similar symptoms occur with other shoulder problems. [2][4]

Sometimes the sound seems to come from the back, near the shoulder blade. Tell the clinician whether it is there, deep inside or at the top of the shoulder. You may be able to point to a spot, or you may only be able to describe an area. Either is useful. [1][4]

03

What do I mean by catching or locking?

One person may say “it catches” when a short jab of pain makes them pause. Another uses the same words when the arm seems to stop partway and they have to lower it before trying again. Tell the clinician what happened in your case. If you could carry on through the movement, say so. If you repeatedly have to reposition the arm to get it moving, mention that too. [2][3]

A clunk with a sense of slipping or giving way is a clue to possible instability. In that case the ball may shift within or partly out of the socket. Mention any previous dislocation and the position that makes the shoulder feel unreliable. A shoulder that looks out of place or suddenly won’t move after injury needs urgent care. [3][4][5]

Prof. BoltDescribe the stop in your own words. Say whether pain made you back off or the movement felt blocked even when you tried to continue. [2][3]
04

What other clues might matter?

Some shoulders feel rough or grind as they move. When that comes with steadily increasing stiffness or a deep ache, the clinician may consider arthritis. A gradual loss of movement has a different history from an arm that suddenly stops moving after a fall. Explain when the problem started and how it has changed. [3][6]

A painful shoulder can also feel weak, especially when lifting. A clinician may check the rotator cuff, the group of muscles and tendons that helps move and steady the shoulder. Tell them if you suddenly lost strength after an injury or if the movement has become gradually more difficult. Pain or weakness on its own can have several causes. [2][3]

Asha writes down three useful details. The new catch is painful, it happens when she reaches back, and she has to lower the arm before trying again. She can still carry a shopping bag by her side. Those details give more information than how loud the click was. [1][2][3]

Think about what changed in the days before you noticed it. Did you return to throwing after a break, do more lifting at work or catch a heavy object that slipped? Tell the clinician if the shoulder felt fine before then or if the same movement had been getting harder for months. [2][4]

THE DEEP DIVEWhat the movement check and scan can tell you
05

What will the clinician check?

They’ll ask when the click started, whether there was an injury and which activities bring it on. They may watch how you raise and turn the arm, check how far it moves with support and assess strength. They may also check for signs that the joint is unstable. If you have tingling or numbness, describe where it goes and when it started. [2][3][4]

There is no need to keep making it catch to prove the symptom is real. Describe something that happened recently, like pulling on a coat or reaching behind a seat. If you had a fall, tell the clinician whether you landed directly on the shoulder or caught yourself with your hand. A history of earlier injuries or operations can change what they check. [2][4]

Prof. BoltTell us what happened during a real task. We can still check motion, strength and the history even when the click stays quiet. [2][4]
06

Does a click mean I need a scan?

A painless noise with comfortable movement usually needs no scan. If pain persists and the examination raises a specific question, the clinician may start with X-rays. They can show bone changes such as arthritis. If the history and examination point to the labrum or instability, an MRI or a special MRI with contrast in the joint may help in selected cases. [1][2][7]

Images can show changes that have been there quietly for years. In one study of 53 people aged 45 to 60 with no shoulder symptoms, two specialists interpreted the same MRI scans as showing upper labral tears in 55% and 72% of shoulders, respectively. Those figures describe the people and scans in that study. They show why a scan finding needs to fit the symptoms and examination before guiding treatment. [8]

If a scan is offered, ask what question it could answer and how the result might change your next step. Also ask what the clinician would do if the scan found a change somewhere that didn’t fit the spot or movement that hurts. [2][7][8]

The best test also depends on which tissue the clinician wants to see. Ultrasound can help assess rotator cuff tendons when the examination points there. If the main question is about the labrum, MRI or contrast MRI is generally more useful. Tell the clinician if you already have images or reports from a previous injury. [2][7]

07

What can I do while I wait for a review?

For a familiar painless click, carry on with movement you find comfortable. If a specific reach keeps causing a sharp catch, change how you do it for now. Asha moves her everyday clothes to the front of the wardrobe so she can reach them without turning her arm back as far. Keep up daily tasks the shoulder tolerates, and follow any restrictions you were given after an injury or operation. [1][2][3]

If the shoulder needs rehabilitation, the plan depends on what the clinician finds. A physiotherapist may work with you on movement, strength or control. Tell them if an activity produces a painful catch or makes the shoulder feel as if it will slip. Those are useful details when deciding which movement to practise and how far to progress. [1][2][4]

Pick an everyday task to check again, such as putting on a jacket. Tell the clinician whether you can do more of it, whether there is less pain, and whether the arm still stops halfway. Changes in what you can do are easier to compare than a click that varies from day to day. [1][2]

Asha can still wear a loose cardigan while she waits. She notes whether the catching is becoming less frequent or happening during more tasks. If the shoulder starts giving way or she can no longer raise it comfortably, she brings that change to the appointment. [1][3][4]

08

When should I get help sooner?

Arrange an assessment if a new click repeatedly hurts, interrupts movement or comes with giving way. Get it checked if movement is getting harder, pain is worsening or you’ve developed weakness since an injury. Tell the appointment team if the shoulder repeatedly locks or if you have a history of dislocation. [2][3][4]

Get urgent medical help if the shoulder suddenly won’t move or looks out of place after a fall, or if pain is sudden and severe. Seek urgent help for persistent numbness or a hot, swollen shoulder with fever. Go to emergency care for a suspected dislocation. Support the arm where it is and leave it for a clinician to put back. [3][5]

ZappyWhat if I notice a click today but everything still moves comfortably?
Prof. BoltPay attention to whether pain, catching or a change in movement develops. Those are more useful details to report than the noise alone. [1][2][3]

Is a painless click usually a problem?

A familiar click with comfortable movement is common. Pay attention if pain, catching or loss of movement develops. [1][3]

What should I tell the clinician?

Describe the task, whether the arm felt blocked or gave way, and any pain or weakness. Mention an injury or earlier dislocation. [2][4]

When do I need urgent help?

Get urgent help for severe sudden pain, loss of movement after a fall, lasting numbness or a hot, swollen shoulder with fever. [3][5]
