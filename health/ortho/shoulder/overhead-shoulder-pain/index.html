ROTATOR CUFF–RELATED SHOULDER PAIN

Why Does My Shoulder Hurt When I Reach Overhead?

Why a shirt on the clothesline can make your shoulder ache, what a clinician checks and how to build back to reaching overhead.

THE PAINFUL REACHTHE SHOULDER CHECKBUILDING BACK

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The shirt on the clothesline

Anita shakes out a wet school shirt and raises it to the clothesline. Halfway up, an ache catches the outside of her upper arm. She hooks the shirt on with her other hand. By the time she comes inside, the ache has almost gone. Later, reaching for a steel plate in the cupboard brings it straight back.

One common reason for this pattern is rotator cuff–related shoulder pain. The rotator cuff is a group of muscles and tendons that helps move and steady the shoulder. A cuff tendon or the slippery tissue beside it can become painful when the arm lifts or lowers. You might feel it at the shoulder or down the outer upper arm, even though the movement starts at the joint. [1][2]

Anita can still chop vegetables and hold her phone. She makes a note of the actions that hurt, including lowering the plate after she gets it off the shelf. Those details give a clinician more to work with than “my arm hurts.” [1][2]

02

What goes on inside the shoulder when I lift?

The rounded top of your upper arm bone sits in a shallow socket on your shoulder blade. As your arm rises, the shoulder blade moves too. Four muscles and their tendons make up the cuff. A tendon attaches muscle to bone. The cuff helps keep the ball steady in the socket while other muscles help raise the arm. [1][2]

On top of the cuff is a thin, slippery sac called a bursa. It helps the tendon glide under the acromion, a bony shelf formed by part of the shoulder blade. A painful tendon may be described as tendinopathy. A sore, inflamed bursa is called bursitis. The symptoms often overlap because the tissues lie close together and work during the same movements. [1][2]

You might also hear the word impingement. It’s often used to describe this kind of shoulder pain. The label alone leaves open which tissue is contributing and what care makes sense. A clinician uses your history and examination to sort through those possibilities. [1][2]

03

Why does it catch halfway up?

Try remembering the last reach that hurt. Was the arm close to your side, partway up or almost overhead? Some people get an ache across one stretch of the lift and feel it ease as the arm goes higher or returns to the side. A clinician may call this a painful arc. It can help guide the examination when considered alongside strength, stiffness and other findings. [1][2]

Think about what changed around the time it began. A weekend of washing windows, extra badminton practice or a new job with repeated high reaches are useful details. A clear injury may be absent. Also mention if the ache starts when you lie on that shoulder or if it interrupts sleep. [1][2][4]

Anita realises how often she raises her arm just to finish one load of washing. She tells the physiotherapist when the ache starts and how soon it settles. That gives them a task to revisit as she recovers. [2]

04

Could the pain be coming from somewhere else?

If you’ve gradually lost movement in several directions, including turning your arm out to slip on a jacket, your clinician may consider frozen shoulder or arthritis. Pain right on top can come from the joint where the collarbone meets the shoulder blade. Tingling or numbness down the arm may lead to a check of your neck and nerves. Tell the clinician if moving your neck changes the arm symptoms. [1][2][3]

Pain may make you ease off during a lift, so the arm can feel weak. A sudden loss of strength after a fall, a forceful pull or a heavy lift changes what needs checking. A torn tendon, a broken bone or a dislocated shoulder may need prompt attention, particularly if you suddenly struggle to raise the arm. [1][3][4]

It helps to describe what you could do before the problem started. If you used to place a pressure cooker on the counter and now need your other hand to help, say so. Mention whether it happened suddenly, whether the arm feels stiff and whether it’s affecting dressing, work or sleep. [2]

THE DEEP DIVEWhat the examination tells you and how to get back to reaching
05

Will I need an X-ray or an MRI?

At the appointment, you may be asked to lift and turn the arm yourself. The clinician may then support the arm and see how far it moves with help. They may ask you to push gently against their hand to check strength and see what brings on the familiar ache. Looking at several movements gives them a fuller picture than any single test. [1][2]

When the story and examination fit typical rotator cuff tendinopathy, care can usually start before any imaging. A recent injury or an unexpected finding can change that decision. X-rays can look for broken bones or joint changes. Ultrasound or MRI can show the cuff tendons when there is a specific question about them. [1][2][3]

If symptoms persist after an appropriate course of care, ask what the clinician wants to learn from a scan and how the result would affect your plan. A review can also help decide whether your rehabilitation needs adjusting or another cause needs investigating. [2]

For a shoulder that still hasn’t improved after up to twelve weeks of appropriate care, the 2025 guideline says imaging may be considered. That time frame applies to typical tendinopathy. An injury, sudden weakness or other worrying symptoms can warrant earlier assessment. [2][3]

06

What can I change around the house this week?

Anita sorts the washing on a table and moves the clothes she wears most to a lower shelf. At the clothesline, she hangs lighter items first and asks someone else to lift the wet towels. You can try a similar change by making a reach lower, bringing the load closer or doing fewer high reaches in one stretch. Keep the shoulder moving gently through daily tasks you can manage. [2][3]

If sleep is disrupted, try lying on your other side or on your back with a pillow under the sore arm. A warm or cold pack wrapped in a towel may ease discomfort. A pharmacist or clinician can help you choose suitable pain relief, especially if you take other medicines or have a long-term health condition. [3][4]

Pick one practical change you can keep doing at home or at work. Someone who stacks shelves may need to ask a colleague to take the high shelves for a while. If it’s becoming harder to move the arm or the pain is getting worse, arrange a review. [2][3]

07

How do I build back to reaching overhead?

Tailored exercise is a first treatment for typical cuff tendinopathy. A physiotherapist might start with a comfortable movement or light resistance and build from there. Once Anita can hang a dry shirt comfortably at a lower height, she might try a higher line before adding the weight of a wet shirt. The starting point depends on her examination and how the shoulder responds. Tell your physiotherapist if pain repeatedly builds up or is worse the following morning. [2][5]

What about a steroid injection? The GRASP trial studied 708 adults with a new episode of rotator cuff–related pain. Participants who had an injection reported modestly better pain and function at eight weeks. Over twelve months, the study found no clear benefit from adding the injection. If one is offered, ask what short-term relief could help you do and how it fits into your rehabilitation. [2][6]

Improvement might first show up as an easier shower or one more shirt on the line. Choose a task you care about and mention what’s changed at your next review. If sleep, work or everyday movement remains limited despite following the plan, ask for reassessment. [2][3]

At a follow-up, Anita can say, “I can hang up a shirt now, but I still need help taking the big pot down.” The physiotherapist can use that difference to decide whether to work on the height of the reach, the weight she can handle, or both. [2]

08

When should I get help sooner?

Arrange a routine assessment if the pain is getting worse, if moving the shoulder is very difficult, or if you’ve seen little improvement after about two weeks. When you call, say whether it started with an injury or whether there’s new weakness. Both details help the service decide how soon to see you. [2][3]

After a fall, a shoulder that has changed shape or an arm you suddenly cannot raise needs urgent attention. A hot, swollen shoulder with fever or feeling very unwell also calls for urgent assessment. Get help promptly for lasting numbness or tingling or if the arm feels unusually hot or cold. [3]

Shoulder or arm discomfort with chest pressure, breathlessness, a cold sweat or feeling faint can be a heart attack. Call your local emergency number immediately. [7]

Why does it hurt when I reach?

The cuff tendons or nearby bursa can become sore when the shoulder works. An assessment can help identify the cause. [1][2]

What can I change today?

Move everyday objects lower, bring loads closer and keep using the arm gently for tasks you can manage. [2][3]

When do I get help quickly?

Seek urgent help for sudden inability to lift the arm after an injury. Call emergency services if shoulder pain comes with possible heart attack symptoms. [3][7]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. A clinician can check what’s causing your shoulder pain and advise you on care. Seek urgent help for sudden severe pain, a changed shoulder shape, sudden inability to lift the arm after injury, persistent numbness, or a hot, swollen shoulder with fever. Call your local emergency number immediately if the pain comes with chest pressure, breathlessness, a cold sweat or faintness. [3][7]
