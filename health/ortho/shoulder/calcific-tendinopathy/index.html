CALCIFIC TENDINOPATHY

How Can a Tiny Calcium Deposit Cause So Much Shoulder Pain?

Why a calcium spot in a tendon can cause a sudden flare, what a scan can show and how treatment is chosen.

THE CALCIUM DEPOSITTHE SUDDEN FLARETREATMENT OPTIONS

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The night the shoulder wouldn’t settle

Maya reaches for her bedside lamp and feels a sharp pain at the outside of her shoulder. She tries sitting up, then resting her arm on a pillow. By morning, getting her arm into a sleeve is so painful that she asks for help. She hasn’t fallen or lifted anything unusually heavy. Because the pain is sudden and severe, she gets assessed that day.

One possible cause is calcific tendinopathy, a calcium deposit in one of the shoulder’s rotator cuff tendons. A deposit can sit there quietly. If the body starts breaking it down, the surrounding tissues can become irritated and painful. That can make a small spot on an X-ray feel like a very big problem. Several conditions can cause sudden severe shoulder pain, so the clinician checks the whole picture. [1][2][3][4]

02

Where is the calcium sitting?

The rotator cuff is a group of muscles and tendons around the shoulder. A tendon is the tough tissue that joins muscle to bone. The cuff helps steady the ball of the upper arm bone in its shallow socket and helps you lift and turn the arm. In calcific tendinopathy, the mineral collects inside one of these tendons, often in the tendon that crosses the top of the joint. [1][2]

Just above the tendon is a thin, slippery sac called a bursa. It helps tissues glide as the arm moves. When the deposit changes or some of it moves into the bursa, the nearby tissues can become inflamed. That can make reaching for a glass or lying on that shoulder uncomfortable, even though the deposit itself takes up little space. [1][2][9]

Maya asks her clinician to point to the tendon on a diagram. Seeing the spot beside the bursa helps her understand why the white mark on the image is only one part of the story. What matters is how the tendon and the nearby tissues are behaving. [1][2]

03

Why can it flare so suddenly?

Deposits can form and stay in a tendon for a time with little or no pain. Later the body may start to absorb the calcium. Clinicians call this the resorptive phase. During that process the deposit can soften and the tendon and nearby bursa can become inflamed. Pain can come on quickly, wake you at night and make even a small movement difficult. [1][2]

The process varies. Some people notice a sudden flare. Others have a more gradual ache when they reach or lift, and some only learn they have a deposit when an image is taken for another reason. The size of the mark is only one detail. The location, the surrounding tissues and the way symptoms began also matter. [1][2]

04

Does a white spot explain all my pain?

The clinician asks how the pain started, where it hurts and which movements became difficult. They may check how far the arm moves on its own and how far it moves when supported. Pain, stiffness and weakness can overlap. Mention an injury, a recent injection or operation, or any fever or feeling of being unwell. These details can change what needs checking. [3][4]

An X-ray can show a deposit near the top of the upper arm bone. Ultrasound can help locate it in the tendon and examine the nearby bursa. A deposit can also appear in someone whose pain comes from another problem. The clinician compares the location and appearance of the deposit with your symptoms and examination. [1][2][3]

Maya asks what the image adds to what the clinician found. If you already have a report, bring it to your review and describe how things have changed since it was taken. A new loss of movement or a change in the pattern of pain may be more useful than a measurement from an earlier scan. [2][3]

You can ask whether the deposit is in a place that fits your pain and whether the scan showed another reason for your symptoms. The same questions are useful if the ache is in both shoulders but the deposit is on only one side. The clinician may need to examine each shoulder separately. [2][3]

THE DEEP DIVEFrom settling the flare to weighing treatment options
05

What helps in the first few days?

After assessment, the first job is often to make ordinary activities manageable. Support your arm with a cushion when you sit or lie down. Take a break from lifting that repeatedly brings on the pain, and use gentle movement within a range you can manage. Ask a pharmacist or clinician what pain relief is suitable, especially if you take other medicines or have a long-term condition. [1][3][4]

When the pain is quieter, a physiotherapist may help you build back movement and strength. Start with what matters to you. Maya wants to wash her hair and reach a plate. She asks which movements she can do comfortably and what to change if the shoulder is still sorer afterwards. The plan can be adjusted as her shoulder settles. [1][3]

For now, Maya moves the plates she uses most to a lower shelf. She notices that dressing is easier before she can comfortably reach overhead again. Both tasks give her a way to describe progress. At a follow-up, bring one example of what is getting easier and one task that still feels out of reach. [1][3]

06

When might a specialist discuss another treatment?

If pain or difficulty using the arm persists despite initial care, ask for reassessment. A clinician may consider a corticosteroid injection into the bursa for short-term pain relief in selected cases. They can explain the possible benefits and side effects and how the injection would fit into your rehabilitation. [1][3]

You may also hear about shockwave therapy. A device sends pulses through the skin towards the affected area. A 2025 rehabilitation guideline says it may be used for calcific tendinopathy. In the UK, NICE limits its use to research because it judged the evidence of benefit inadequate. If it is offered, ask what benefit is expected for your symptoms and what other options are available. [3][5]

Decisions depend on how long symptoms have lasted, how much they affect daily life and whether the deposit fits the examination. If symptoms are gradually improving, that progress is worth bringing into the discussion. If you’re still struggling to sleep or dress despite following the plan, describe that too. [1][3]

07

What do we know about washing out the deposit?

Ultrasound-guided lavage, sometimes called barbotage, uses a needle to wash the deposit with fluid and try to withdraw some of the material. It is an option some specialists discuss for persistent symptoms. A guideline supports considering it after initial treatment has been insufficient, but research has found mixed results. [3][6]

In a 2023 trial, 220 adults with symptoms for at least three months were assigned to lavage with a steroid injection, a simulated washout with a steroid injection, or a simulated washout without steroid. At four months, the groups had similar shoulder outcomes, and the trial found no advantage for either active treatment at 24 months. These findings are one part of the decision about persistent symptoms. [6]

Ask what improvement the specialist expects, how soon you would judge it and what would happen if you waited. Surgery is usually reserved for selected people whose symptoms remain limiting despite other care. The decision should start with what is still difficult for you and whether the proposed treatment is likely to help. [1][3][6]

The trial also found that whether the deposit disappeared on an X-ray did not reliably predict how well people felt or functioned. If your clinician proposes a repeat image, ask what question it will answer. At the same visit, describe changes you can feel, such as how often pain wakes you or whether you can use the arm to dress. [3][6]

08

When should I get help sooner?

Get urgent assessment for sudden severe shoulder pain or an arm you suddenly can’t move. Seek urgent help if it follows an injury, the joint looks misshapen, or you have lasting numbness. A hot, swollen shoulder with fever or feeling very unwell needs urgent assessment even if you already know there is a calcium deposit. [4][7]

Call your local emergency number if shoulder or arm discomfort comes with chest pressure, shortness of breath, a cold sweat or feeling faint. These can be signs of a heart attack. [8]

If you’ve already been assessed, follow the plan you were given and arrange a review if pain is worsening or you’re still struggling with everyday tasks. Tell the clinician what has changed since the last visit. Maya takes a note of the first bad night, how much sleep she is getting and when she first managed to dress without help. [3][4]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. A calcium deposit can appear on a scan even when another problem is causing the pain. Get urgent assessment for sudden severe pain, inability to move the arm, pain after an injury, lasting numbness, or a hot, swollen shoulder with fever. Call your local emergency number if shoulder or arm discomfort comes with chest pressure, shortness of breath, a cold sweat or faintness. [2][4][7][8]
