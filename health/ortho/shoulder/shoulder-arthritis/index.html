SHOULDER OSTEOARTHRITIS

Why Does My Shoulder Grind and Feel Stiff?

How arthritis can change shoulder movement, what an X-ray can show and when replacement may be discussed.

THE STIFF JOINTREADING THE X-RAYTREATMENT CHOICES

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The pot he moved to a lower shelf

Krishna used to reach up for his favourite steel pot without thinking. Now his shoulder feels stiff when he lifts the arm, and he sometimes feels a grind deep in the joint as he lowers it. Over several months he has started keeping the pot on the counter. He can still cook, but pulling a shirt over his head takes longer and he sometimes wakes when he rolls onto that shoulder.

One possible reason for a shoulder that gradually grinds, aches and loses movement is osteoarthritis of the main shoulder joint. The smooth cartilage on the bone surfaces becomes thinner and uneven, and the joint can become harder to move. Grinding can also occur with other shoulder problems, so a clinician considers how the pain and stiffness developed and what they find on examination. [1][2][3]

02

What changes inside an arthritic shoulder?

The rounded top of the upper arm bone forms the ball. It fits against a shallow socket in the shoulder blade. Both surfaces are covered with cartilage, which lets them glide as you lift or turn the arm. Clinicians call this the glenohumeral joint. There is also a smaller joint at the top of the shoulder where the collarbone meets the shoulder blade. Arthritis can affect either joint. [1][2]

In osteoarthritis of the main joint, cartilage can thin and the space between the bones may look narrower on an X-ray. Small bony growths, called bone spurs, can form around the edge. The shoulder may become stiff, and movement can feel rough or make a grinding sound. Clinicians sometimes call that sensation crepitus. [1][2]

Age and previous injuries can play a part. Inflammatory arthritis and arthritis associated with a large rotator cuff tear are other ways a shoulder joint can become damaged. Tell the clinician if a past break or dislocation affected that shoulder, or if you already receive treatment for arthritis in other joints. [1][3]

The shoulder that feels stiff in the morning may loosen a little as you get dressed, then ache after a long spell of cooking. Describe how it feels at different times of day and which tasks make it worse. These details help the clinician understand what you’re dealing with between visits. [1][3]

03

Why do some movements feel harder than others?

Turning the upper arm outward to put on a jacket or reaching up to wash your hair requires motion at the ball-and-socket joint. As that motion becomes limited, you may lift the whole shoulder blade or turn your body to finish. The ache can build during activity, and some people notice it at rest or at night. [1][3]

Frozen shoulder can also cause a gradual loss of motion. Pain and weakness from the rotator cuff can make lifting difficult too. A clinician compares the movements you can do yourself with those the shoulder can do when the arm is supported, then checks strength and other clues. The place where you feel pain can also help, such as a deep ache or tenderness right at the top. [1][3][4]

Prof. BoltDescribe a task that brings it on. Let the clinician decide how to check your movement comfortably. [1][3]
04

What can the examination and X-ray show?

The clinician will ask which movements have changed, whether your sleep is affected and what you do at work or home. They may watch you lift and turn the arm, check how far it moves with gentle support and assess strength. Tell them about earlier injuries, operations or treatments. [1][3]

An X-ray can show signs of arthritis, including a narrower joint space or bone spurs. It helps the clinician see whether the main joint or the smaller joint on top is affected. Other imaging may be useful if there is a specific question about the rotator cuff, another cause of pain or the shape of the bones before surgery. [1][2][3]

In a study of 246 people with arthritis of the main shoulder joint, the severity seen on X-rays did not track closely with the pain and loss of function people reported. A clinician can use the X-ray to understand the joint and your account to understand what needs treating. Bring one example of a task you miss doing. [3][5]

THE DEEP DIVEFrom everyday adjustments to treatment choices
05

What can I change at home?

Krishna keeps the heavy pot lower down and uses both hands to move it close to his body. When choosing which tasks to adapt, start with something you do often. Try a lighter container or split a heavy load. If lying on the sore side wakes you, try the other side or your back with the forearm supported by a pillow. [1][6]

Activity changes can make daily life easier while you keep using the shoulder within a comfortable range. A physiotherapist may suggest movements and strengthening suited to your shoulder and your goals. The available evidence does not identify one best exercise programme for shoulder osteoarthritis. Tell your physiotherapist what happens during the exercises and how the shoulder feels later. [3][4]

If you’re unsure how much to do, ask the physiotherapist to show you where to start and how to adjust the plan on a more painful day. At follow-up, compare the same task, such as putting a light bowl on a shelf, to see whether it’s getting easier. [3]

Prof. BoltPick something that matters to you, such as putting on a shirt more easily. Your physiotherapist can help you choose a starting point and review it with you. [3]
06

Can medicines or injections help?

Ask a pharmacist or clinician about pain relief that fits your health and your other medicines. Anti-inflammatory medicines can help some people, but whether they are suitable depends on factors such as stomach, kidney or heart problems and other medicines you take. Tell the clinician what you already use, including tablets you buy yourself. [1][2][10]

A clinician may discuss a corticosteroid injection into the joint for short-term relief in selected cases. Ask what relief is realistic and what you and the clinician would do if it wears off. If you’re offered a hyaluronic acid injection for arthritis of the main shoulder joint, ask about the evidence. The AAOS guideline found no benefit for this use. [1][2][7]

At your next review, describe how pain relief has changed your day. Perhaps you can sleep for longer or reach a tap more easily. If the shoulder still limits what you can do, say which part of the plan you’ve been able to follow and where you need help. [3]

07

When might shoulder replacement be discussed?

If pain, loss of movement or sleep disruption still limits daily life despite nonsurgical care, ask whether a specialist review would help. Shoulder replacement can reduce pain and improve function in selected patients. The discussion includes what you want to regain, what the joint and tendons look like, your general health, and what surgery and recovery would involve. [1][2][3][7]

In a conventional total shoulder replacement, the surgeon replaces the ball and covers the socket with an artificial surface. In a reverse replacement, the positions of the ball and socket are switched. That design can help the shoulder work when the rotator cuff is badly damaged. The choice also depends on bone shape and other findings. Ask why an option is recommended for you. [1][2][7]

Ask what help you might need at home and when you could return to tasks such as dressing, cooking or driving. Recovery involves a rehabilitation plan with timing that depends on the procedure and the surgeon’s instructions. Krishna tells the team that sleeping comfortably and getting dressed on his own matter more to him than reaching his highest shelf. [1][3]

The surgeon can also explain possible complications, such as infection, and any limits you may have after recovery. If you carry children, do heavy work or play sport, bring those examples to the discussion. What is reasonable to expect after replacement depends on your shoulder, your health and the demands of the activity. [1][3][7]

Krishna asks how the team will judge whether treatment has helped. He can already point to two things he wants to change. On a good day he wants to get his shirt on without help, and at night he wants fewer interruptions from the ache. Those are useful goals to bring to the next appointment, whichever treatment he chooses. [3][5]

08

When should I get help sooner?

Arrange a review if shoulder pain or stiffness is worsening, disturbing sleep or making everyday tasks difficult. Tell the clinician what changed and over what period. Seek urgent help if pain becomes suddenly severe, the shoulder changes shape, you suddenly can’t move the arm, or the pain follows a fall. [4]

Get urgent medical help for a hot, swollen shoulder with fever or feeling very unwell. If shoulder or arm discomfort comes with chest pressure, shortness of breath, a cold sweat or feeling faint, call your local emergency number. [4][8][9]

ZappyWhat if I already know I have arthritis, but today the pain came on suddenly after a fall?
Prof. BoltGet it checked urgently and tell the team what happened. An injury can cause new symptoms in a shoulder that already has arthritis. [1][4]

The fridge-door cheat sheet

What can make the shoulder grind?

In osteoarthritis the joint surfaces can change and movement may feel rough. The examination and an X-ray can help identify the cause. [1][2][3]

What can I try first?

Keep up comfortable movement, adapt tasks that repeatedly hurt and ask about exercises or pain relief suited to you. [1][3][10]

When should I get help quickly?

Get urgent help for sudden severe pain, pain after a fall or a hot, swollen shoulder with fever. Call emergency services for possible heart attack symptoms. [4][8][9]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Get urgent medical help if a painful arthritic shoulder suddenly becomes very painful, hot and swollen, or if you develop fever, feel very unwell or suddenly can’t move the arm after a fall. Call your local emergency number if shoulder or arm discomfort comes with chest pressure, shortness of breath, a cold sweat or faintness. [4][8][9]
