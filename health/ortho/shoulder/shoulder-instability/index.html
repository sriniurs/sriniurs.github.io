SHOULDER INSTABILITY

Why Does My Shoulder Keep Slipping Out?

Why the shoulder can give way after a dislocation, what helps it feel steadier and what to do if it comes out again.

THE SLIPPING SHOULDERBUILDING STABILITYWHEN TO GET HELP

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The throw he stopped halfway

Kiran is playing cricket when he lifts his arm to throw the ball back. His shoulder briefly feels as though it slides forward. He lowers the arm and the sensation passes. Months earlier, he fell during a match and needed hospital care to put that same shoulder back in place. Now he hesitates before reaching into the back seat of the car, too. He books a review and explains both episodes.

A shoulder can keep slipping because the structures that centre the ball in its socket were stretched or injured, or because the joint is naturally loose and difficult to control. The term shoulder instability describes a shoulder that repeatedly slips or gives way. It can happen during sport, at work or during ordinary movements. [1][2]

02

What normally keeps the ball in the socket?

The rounded top of the upper arm bone sits against a shallow socket on the shoulder blade. A rim of firm cartilage called the labrum runs around the socket and helps deepen it. The joint capsule and its ligaments help keep the ball in place, especially near the limits of movement. Muscles around the shoulder, including the rotator cuff, help keep the ball centred as the arm moves. [1]

During a forward dislocation, the ball comes out towards the front. This can stretch the capsule or tear the labrum where it attaches to the front and lower edge of the socket. A tear there is often called a Bankart lesion. A dislocation can also dent the ball or damage the bony edge of the socket. The pattern and amount of damage help guide treatment. [1][2]

Think of what happens between the first fall and the next throw. The pain from the fall can settle and the joint can be back in position, while the structures that support it are still healing or have been left less effective. This is one reason a movement that used to feel ordinary may suddenly feel unsafe. [1][2]

03

Did it slip partway or come all the way out?

A subluxation means the ball shifts partway out of the socket. It may slide back quickly and leave the shoulder sore or difficult to trust. In a full dislocation, the ball comes all the way out. The arm may be hard to move and the shoulder may look different. A full dislocation can sometimes go back in on its own, too. [1][3][7]

The distinction can be hard to make after the event. What helps is your description of the position of the arm, how long the episode lasted and whether anyone saw the shoulder change shape. Tell the clinician whether someone at the hospital had to put it back and whether X-rays were taken. Even a shoulder that looks normal again deserves assessment after a suspected dislocation. [1][2][7]

ZappyDoes a click mean my shoulder has slipped?
Prof. BoltA click alone tells us little. Describe whether the shoulder actually shifted, gave way or made you stop the movement. [1]
04

Why does it happen with smaller movements now?

Once a shoulder has dislocated, another episode can happen with less force. Younger people and those who play contact sports can be more prone to recurrence after a traumatic dislocation. Looser ligaments or damage to the bone or soft tissues may also influence the risk. Tell the clinician if the slips are occurring more often or with movements that used to feel safe. [1][2]

Some people have instability without a major injury. A shoulder may slip in more than one direction, particularly in people whose joints are naturally very flexible. Clinicians call this multidirectional instability when the joint is symptomatic in multiple directions. The history and examination guide the plan. A person with naturally loose joints and a person with repeated slips after one hard fall may need different treatment. [1]

Kiran tells the clinician he now pulls his arm down as soon as he feels that forward shift. The feeling that a movement might make the shoulder slip is called apprehension. It is worth mentioning even if you’ve never had another full dislocation. [1][2]

THE DEEP DIVEFrom the shoulder examination to getting back to sport
05

What will the clinician check?

They’ll ask about the first injury and the most recent episode. Tell them how often it has happened, what positions bring it on and what you have stopped doing because the shoulder feels unreliable. They may examine the shoulder’s movement and strength and look for signs of excessive looseness. After a recent injury they can also check sensation, muscle function and blood flow in the arm. [1][2]

X-rays can show where the ball is sitting and whether a bone was injured. If instability keeps recurring, the specialist may ask for an MRI to examine the labrum and other soft tissues, or a CT scan to show how much bone has been damaged. The choice depends on what they need to learn and whether it could change the treatment. [1][2]

If the shoulder is still out of place, the first job is to assess it and put it back safely. The emergency team usually takes X-rays around the time of treatment and checks feeling and circulation. The detailed discussion about repeated slipping can follow once the immediate injury has been treated. [2][3]

06

Can physiotherapy help me trust the arm again?

After a dislocation has been assessed and treated, follow the instructions you’ve been given about a sling and when to start moving the arm. Rehabilitation usually begins with protecting the shoulder and restoring comfortable movement. As the shoulder improves, exercises can work on strength and coordination. A physiotherapist may focus on the rotator cuff and the muscles that guide the shoulder blade. [1][3][4]

Ask what would tell you that it’s time to move to the next stage. Reaching a shelf, returning to manual work and throwing a cricket ball all make different demands. Your clinician can use your movement, strength and ability to do the task to guide the return. Feeling less sore is welcome, but tell the team if the shoulder still slips or feels about to give way. [1][4]

Kiran brings a specific goal to physiotherapy. He wants to put on his backpack without bracing for a slip. Later he wants to throw again. Telling the physiotherapist about both goals gives them something to work towards from the first visit. [4]

Tell your physiotherapist which part of a task makes you hesitate. For Kiran, it’s bringing his arm back before the throw. The return-to-sport plan can build towards that movement in stages. Ask what you can practise now, what should wait, and what would mean you’re ready for a harder step. [4]

07

When might an operation be discussed?

A shoulder specialist may discuss surgery if the shoulder repeatedly dislocates or remains unstable enough to limit work, sport or everyday life. The discussion can also happen after a first traumatic dislocation in someone at higher risk of recurrence. Ask what the scan or examination showed and how that finding affects your options. [1][2]

A Bankart repair reattaches a torn labrum at the front of the socket. When a sizeable part of the bony rim is missing, a specialist may consider a procedure to rebuild that part of the socket. The best choice depends on the injury pattern, previous treatment and what you want to return to. Ask what recovery and rehabilitation would involve. [1][2][4]

In one randomised trial of patients under 25 with a first forward dislocation, fewer people had another dislocation over two years after early Bankart repair than after nonsurgical treatment. Those findings apply to the young group studied. A specialist can help you weigh the chance of another dislocation against the risks and recovery time of surgery. [2][5]

For someone who depends on their arm at work, time away from lifting may matter as much as time away from sport. Bring those details to the appointment so the team can explain how each option might affect your recovery. [2][4]

08

What should I do if it comes out again?

If the shoulder looks out of place or you suddenly can’t move the arm, go to emergency care. Support the arm with a folded towel or sling in the position you found it. Keep the upper arm still. Leave the joint for a clinician to put back. If the shoulder appears to have slipped back on its own, arrange prompt medical assessment, especially after an injury or if pain or weakness persists. [2][3][7]

Get emergency help now if the hand becomes cold, pale or numb, the shoulder looks out of place, or you cannot move the arm after a slip. Call your local emergency number if you can’t get to emergency care safely. [2][3][6]

If you keep having brief slips or giving-way episodes between injuries, book a shoulder review. Bring any old X-ray reports and a note of what happened each time. Ask who to contact if it happens again and what you should do about work or sport while you wait. [1][2][4]
