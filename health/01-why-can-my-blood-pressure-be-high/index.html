BLOOD PRESSURE

Why Can My Blood Pressure Be High When I Feel Perfectly Fine?

What the cuff can tell you when you feel well, and how to make sense of the numbers.

TWO NUMBERSWHY NO SYMPTOMS?WHEN TO ACT

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

I feel perfectly fine. Why is my BP high?

Meera has come to the clinic for a routine check. She walked from the bus stop, answered two messages and is already thinking about what to buy for dinner. Then the cuff squeezes her arm. The screen shows 154/94. “That’s mine?” she asks. “I feel perfectly fine.”

Her clinician asks her to sit quietly before they measure again. There are two questions to answer. Was the first reading influenced by the rush to get there or the way it was taken? And is her blood pressure regularly high when she’s going about an ordinary day? A careful repeat and, when appropriate, readings outside the clinic help answer those questions. [1][2]

High blood pressure, also called hypertension, usually gives you no symptoms. Feeling well is entirely compatible with having high readings. A measurement provides information your body may never announce. [3]

Meera leaves with a plan to check again. That evening, as she tells her family about the appointment, the number feels less like a verdict and more like a question they now know how to investigate.

02

What do 120 and 80 actually mean?

Put two fingers on your wrist and you may feel a pulse. That pulse comes from the heart pushing blood into the arteries, the vessels that carry blood away from it. Blood pressure is the force of blood against artery walls. It rises and falls with each heartbeat. [4]

In a reading of 120/80, said as “120 over 80,” the first number is the systolic pressure. It measures the pressure when the heart contracts and pumps. The second is the diastolic pressure, the pressure between beats as the heart relaxes. Blood is still under pressure during that pause. Both numbers are reported in millimetres of mercury, written mm Hg. [4]

Imagine the two numbers as two frames from the same heartbeat. One catches the squeeze. The other catches the pause. Either number can be persistently high, so tell your clinician both when giving a reading. The pulse number some monitors display is a separate measurement. It counts beats per minute. [4][5]

03

Why does the pressure rise?

Think of a pump sending water through pipes. The pump matters, and so do the amount of fluid and how easily it passes through the pipes. In your body, the heart pumps, arteries can tighten or stiffen, and kidneys help adjust how much salt and water you keep. Hormones help regulate fluid levels and how tightly blood vessels squeeze. Together, these systems influence your blood pressure. [5][6][7]

For many people, high blood pressure develops gradually through a mix of influences. Age and family history play a part. A diet high in salt, too little activity and drinking too much alcohol can also raise the likelihood. A salty meal is a detail in the story. Your usual eating pattern and other risk factors matter over time. [3][6]

Sometimes a clinician finds a specific cause. Kidney disease, certain hormone disorders, obstructive sleep apnoea (repeated pauses in breathing during sleep) and some medicines can contribute. Bring a list of what you take, including over-the-counter products. It gives your clinician a better starting point for working out what may be pushing your numbers up. [5][6]

04

Shouldn’t I get a headache first?

At home, Meera’s sister asks whether she’s had a headache. Meera hasn’t. She slept well and made up the stairs with her usual shopping bag. This is where a very reasonable instinct can mislead us. We’re used to noticing a problem because something hurts or feels different.

Blood pressure can stay high without producing a sensation you can reliably recognise. Your heart can keep pumping and your organs can keep doing their jobs while the extra strain accumulates. A headache on a busy day has many possible explanations. A day without a headache tells you very little about the pressure inside your arteries. [3][8]

Think of a tyre that looks fine from across the street. To find its pressure, you use a gauge. For your arteries, the cuff provides the measurement. The numbers help your clinician spot a pattern while you’re still feeling well, when there’s time to act on it. Very high readings and sudden serious symptoms need a different response, which we’ll cover in Episode 8. [3][8]

THE DEEP DIVEWhat high pressure can do over time and what you can do about it
05

Which parts of my body feel the strain?

A single reading captures a moment. The concern with hypertension is what repeated exposure to higher pressure can do over months and years. The heart works harder to push blood out. Its muscle can thicken, and over time it can struggle to pump effectively. High pressure can also damage arteries and raise the chance of a heart attack. [3][9]

In the brain, a vessel can become blocked or burst, causing a stroke. In the kidneys, damage to small vessels can make it harder to filter the blood. When damaged kidneys retain more fluid, pressure can rise further. In the retina, the light-sensitive layer at the back of each eye, delicate blood vessels can be damaged and sight can suffer. [7][9]

There’s a reason to act before any of that happens. An analysis of 48 randomised trials found that a 5 mm Hg reduction in systolic pressure with medication was associated with about a 10% relative reduction in major cardiovascular events. Personal benefit depends on your starting risk. Your clinician may check your urine, blood and heart to help assess that risk and look for early signs of strain. [2][10]

06

Does one high reading mean I have hypertension?

Blood pressure changes through the day. A clinic reading can run higher than your usual pressure, sometimes called a white-coat effect. The reverse also happens. Readings can look fine at the clinic and run high elsewhere, a pattern called masked hypertension. A clinician may ask you to record readings at home or wear an automatic monitor that checks your pressure over 24 hours. [2][11]

For a home check, use a validated upper-arm monitor with a cuff that fits. Avoid caffeine, smoking and exercise for 30 minutes beforehand. Empty your bladder and sit quietly for five minutes. Support your back, put both feet flat on the floor and rest your bare arm at heart height. Keep silent while the cuff runs. Take two readings at least one minute apart and record both. Follow your clinician’s schedule. [1][2]

Ask which numbers apply to you. The UK NICE guideline, for example, confirms hypertension at a clinic reading of 140/90 or higher plus a daytime monitor or home average of 135/85 or higher. The US AHA/ACC guideline classifies an average systolic pressure of 130–139 or a diastolic pressure of 80–89 as stage 1 hypertension. Your diagnosis and treatment target depend on the setting, the guideline used and your health. [2][5]

07

What can I actually do about it?

At her follow-up, Meera brings the log and asks three questions. “What are we aiming for? What’s one change I can start this week? When should I come back?” They choose a first step that fits her routine and agree on when to review the readings.

Start with what’s practical. Less salt can mean a smaller helping of pickle, papad or packaged namkeen and more flavour from lemon, herbs or spices. An eating pattern rich in vegetables, fruit, pulses and whole grains can help lower pressure. Regular activity, such as a brisk walk if it suits your health, can help too. If you drink alcohol, cutting back can be part of the plan. [3][5][12]

Some people also need medicine. Different medicines can ease pressure in different ways, including relaxing vessels or helping the body get rid of extra salt and water. The choice depends on your readings, overall risk and other health conditions. A better set of readings can mean your treatment is working. Keep taking prescribed medicine and discuss side effects, cost or missed doses with your clinician so you can agree on a plan that works for you. [1][5][13]

08

When is a high reading an emergency?

For an adult who is neither pregnant nor recently postpartum, the situation depends on how high the reading is and what else is happening. Keep these two actions in mind. [14][15]

Call your local emergency number immediately if a very high reading comes with chest pain, shortness of breath, sudden weakness or numbness, a change in vision or trouble speaking. Suspected stroke or heart attack symptoms need emergency help at any pressure. Call straight away, even if you haven’t measured your blood pressure. [14][16][17]

If you feel well and the top number is above 180 or the bottom number is above 120 mm Hg, sit quietly, wait at least one minute and check again. If either number is still above its threshold, contact your clinician immediately for guidance. [1][14]

Pregnant or recently delivered? Contact your maternity team urgently about high readings. Seek immediate medical care for a severe headache that won’t go away, vision changes, chest pain or trouble breathing. Pregnancy has its own, lower thresholds for severe high blood pressure. [15][18]

Meera still feels perfectly fine. What has changed is that she now knows her usual readings, has a plan she understands and knows when to ask for help.

Feeling well and having high blood pressure can happen together. Measure carefully, follow up on the pattern and know the urgent warning signs. [1][3][14]

KEEP

The fridge-door cheat sheet

Can I have high BP and feel well?

Yes. Hypertension usually has no symptoms. [3]

What do I do with a high reading?

Repeat it with good technique. Share a record of readings with your clinician. For very high readings, follow the guidance in Episode 8. [1][14]

What if I have sudden serious symptoms?

Call your local emergency number. Chest pain or signs of a stroke need immediate care. [14][16]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Use this article to discuss your readings, diagnosis and treatment with your clinician. Get their advice before starting, stopping or changing any medicine. For emergency symptoms, call your local emergency number immediately.
