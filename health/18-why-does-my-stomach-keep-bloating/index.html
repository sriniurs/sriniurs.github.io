IRRITABLE BOWEL SYNDROME (IBS)

Why Does My Stomach Keep Bloating Even When All My Tests Are Normal?

How a sensitive gut can cause real symptoms, what tests check for and what can help.

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The waistband that felt tighter by evening

Farah starts the day comfortably. By late afternoon her waistband feels tight, and she has cramps low in her abdomen. Some weeks she struggles to pass hard stools. On other days she needs a toilet quickly. Her initial tests are reassuring. She asks her clinician how she can feel this uncomfortable when the results look normal.

Irritable bowel syndrome, or IBS, is one possibility when recurring abdominal pain goes along with changes in how often you pass stool or what it looks like. Bloating is common. A routine scan may look normal because IBS involves how the gut works and senses what is happening inside it. The pattern of symptoms and the clinical assessment both matter. [1][2][3]

Farah describes what happens before and after a bowel movement. The clinician asks about the pain, stool changes and any warning signs. Bloating by itself can have several causes, so these details help decide whether IBS fits. [2][3][4]

02

What do the gut and brain have to do with it?

The gut and brain exchange signals. These help regulate how the bowel moves and how strongly you feel what’s happening inside it. In IBS, bowel movement can vary and the gut can be more sensitive to stretch. An amount of gas or stool that someone else barely notices may feel painful or uncomfortable. [1][3][5]

Think of the gut as sending feedback all day. Sometimes the signal from a small stretch feels louder than expected. Bloating is the feeling of fullness or pressure. Some people also notice their abdomen visibly expand, which clinicians call distension. The feeling and the visible change can occur together or separately. [1][5][6]

Sleep, stress and a previous gut infection can influence symptoms for some people. Stress is one possible influence in a condition involving the bowel and nervous system. Farah’s clinician takes the discomfort seriously whether it happens on a busy day or a quiet one. [1][5]

Farah says the bloating sometimes builds even when she can’t see much change in the mirror. Other days her abdomen feels visibly larger by evening. Her clinician asks whether this settles overnight or keeps getting worse. A useful description can include when the pressure starts, what it feels like and whether her abdomen actually changes size. [4][5][6]

03

Why do my bowel habits keep changing?

For some people with IBS, hard stools and straining are the main problem. Others have loose stools or urgency. Some have both at different times. Doctors sometimes use names such as IBS with constipation, IBS with diarrhoea, or mixed IBS. The pattern can help them choose a treatment that’s less likely to worsen the other symptoms. [1][3][5]

The pain may change after a bowel movement. It can improve for some people and worsen for others. Farah starts noting the days when cramps go with hard stool and the days when loose stool makes her rush. Saying “my bowel habits keep changing” is a useful start. A few examples show what that change means in practice. [2][3][4]

If the pattern changes suddenly or starts waking you with diarrhoea, tell your clinician. New symptoms deserve a review even after an earlier IBS diagnosis. [3][5]

For a short record, you could note whether stools were hard and lumpy, soft and formed, or loose and watery. Add how many times you went, whether you had to rush and how any cramps changed afterwards. Bring a few typical days as well as the difficult ones. [2][4]

04

Is IBS the same as inflammatory bowel disease?

The names sound alike. Inflammatory bowel disease, or IBD, includes Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis. These cause inflammation that can damage the bowel. IBS involves changes in sensation and bowel function. Both can cause cramps, bloating and altered stools, which is one reason clinicians ask about the full pattern. [1][3][7]

Tell your clinician about blood in the stool, unexplained weight loss, fever, persistent diarrhoea at night or anaemia. Those findings can point towards another cause and may change what the clinician checks. A family history of IBD, coeliac disease or bowel cancer is also worth mentioning. [3][4][5][7]

Coeliac disease can cause symptoms such as bloating and diarrhoea. If testing is planned, ask before cutting out gluten. Taking gluten out of your diet before testing can make the results harder to interpret. [4][8]

05

How can a clinician diagnose IBS if the tests are normal?

Clinicians can make a positive diagnosis when the history fits, the examination is reassuring and appropriate checks don’t suggest another explanation. Common initial tests can include a blood count, a blood marker of inflammation and a blood test for coeliac disease. If diarrhoea is part of the picture, a stool test called faecal calprotectin can help assess whether bowel inflammation is likely. [2][3][5][9]

The choice of tests depends on your symptoms, age, family history and any warning signs. A colonoscopy is not routinely needed for everyone with a typical IBS pattern and reassuring assessment. It may be appropriate if there are new concerns or you’re due for bowel cancer screening. Ask what your tests checked for and what would change the plan. [2][3][5]

Farah asks which symptoms in her story fit IBS. Her clinician explains why the recurring pain and stool changes matter more to the diagnosis than bloating alone. [1][2][3]

06

Should I cut out foods if I keep getting bloated?

Start with a pattern you can realistically track. Regular meals, enough fluid and noticing which foods repeatedly cause trouble can be helpful. If constipation is a problem, soluble fibre, such as oats or ispaghula, may help some people. Adding a lot of bran can worsen bloating. Discuss changes gradually so you can tell how each affects you. [2][5][10]

A low-FODMAP diet temporarily reduces certain fermentable carbohydrates that can draw water into the gut or be broken down by gut bacteria. In a randomised trial in India, about 63% of adults with diarrhoea-predominant IBS assigned the diet responded at four weeks, compared with about 41% given traditional dietary advice. Those results reflect the specific group and trial. [11][12]

A low-FODMAP trial usually includes planned reintroduction and personalisation. Ask a dietitian whether it’s suitable, especially if your diet is already limited. The aim is to identify what you tolerate and keep meals varied. [3][5][11]

Farah asks whether she should stop several foods on the same day. Her dietitian starts by reviewing what she normally eats, including meals at work. They agree on a trial and a date to review it. If a restriction doesn’t help, it’s worth asking whether there’s any reason to keep it. [5][11]

07

What if changing meals isn’t enough?

Talk about the symptom that causes the most trouble. A plan for hard stools may differ from one for urgency, cramps or diarrhoea. Clinicians can discuss medicines that target particular symptoms and help you weigh possible side effects. Farah says her immediate goal is to get through her commute with less worry about finding a toilet. [2][3][5][13]

The gut–brain connection also gives clinicians another way to help. Approaches such as gut-directed cognitive behavioural therapy or hypnotherapy can improve symptoms for some people. These treatments focus on how the gut and nervous system respond and how you manage symptoms in daily life. [3][5][13]

Agree on when you’ll review the plan and what improvement would count. Farah notes the days when bloating or urgency changes her plans, alongside stool changes. Those details make it easier to judge whether a treatment is helping in a way that matters to her. [2][5]

At follow-up, she can say whether the commute is easier and whether a change that helped urgency made constipation worse. That gives the clinician a practical reason to revisit the plan. [2][5]

08

When should I get help sooner?

Get recurrent bloating or a new change in bowel habits reviewed. Mention unexplained weight loss, blood in the stool, persistent nighttime diarrhoea or unusual tiredness. New persistent bloating, particularly if you also feel full quickly or have pelvic pain, can need assessment for causes outside the bowel. [4][5][14][15]

Get emergency care for sudden severe abdominal pain, or for a swollen abdomen with vomiting and an inability to pass stool or wind. Get emergency help for heavy or ongoing rectal bleeding, fainting or collapse. These can be signs of a problem that needs prompt treatment. [15][16][17]

Farah’s next appointment has a clearer purpose. She’ll discuss what has improved, which symptoms still interrupt her day and what changes should bring her back sooner.

Describe pain, bloating and stool changes together. Ask what your tests checked, what to try next and which changes need a review. [2][3][5]

The fridge-door cheat sheet

Can tests be normal even with symptoms?

Yes. IBS can affect how the gut moves and what you feel. The symptom pattern and appropriate checks guide the diagnosis. [1][2][3]

Should I cut out lots of foods?

Start with changes you can track. If a low-FODMAP trial is suitable, plan reintroduction with a dietitian. [5][10][11]

What needs a faster review?

Report blood in the stool, unexplained weight loss or a new persistent change. Get emergency care for sudden severe pain or heavy bleeding. [5][15][16][17]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Use this article to discuss bowel symptoms, test results and treatment with your clinician. Ask them before changing any medicine. For severe symptoms, call your local emergency number immediately.
