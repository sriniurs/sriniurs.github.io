VARICOSE VEINS

Why Are the Veins in My Legs Becoming Thick and Twisted?

How leg veins move blood uphill, why they can bulge and what helps when your legs feel heavy.

ONE-WAY VALVESHEAVY LEGSWHEN TO GET HELP

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The veins she noticed at the end of her shift

Lakshmi works at a shop counter. By closing time her legs feel heavy, and the strap on her sandal leaves a deeper mark around one ankle. In the changing room she notices a raised, twisting vein she hadn’t paid much attention to before. She asks her clinician why it’s getting bigger and whether that could explain the aching.

Varicose veins are enlarged, twisted veins close to the surface of the skin. They often develop when one-way valves in the leg veins no longer close well. Blood can flow backwards and increase pressure in the vein. Over time, that can stretch it and make it bulge. [1][2][3]

Some people notice only the appearance. Others have aching, heaviness, itching or swelling, often worse after standing. The clinician asks about the timing, checks her skin and looks for other possible causes of swelling. A visible vein gives a clue, and the wider pattern helps determine whether it is responsible for the symptoms. [1][3][4]

02

How does blood get back uphill?

Arteries carry blood from the heart. Veins carry it back. In the legs, much of that return travels through deep veins. The veins nearer the skin connect to that deeper network. Standing puts a column of blood below the heart, so the legs have ways to help move it upwards. [2][5]

When you walk, your calf muscles squeeze the veins around them. That pressure helps push blood towards the heart. One-way valves open to let it through and close to limit backward flow. Think of the calf as a pump with a series of little doors that keep blood moving in the right direction. [2][5]

If some doors stop sealing, blood can leak backwards, particularly when you’re upright. Clinicians call that backward flow venous reflux. It raises pressure in veins farther down the leg. That extra pressure helps explain why symptoms can get worse over a long shift and ease when you walk or put your feet up. [1][2][5]

03

Why are these veins getting bigger now?

Age, family history, pregnancy, carrying excess weight and prolonged standing are among the factors associated with varicose veins. A previous deep vein clot can also damage valves and affect how blood returns. Often several factors are involved. Two people can spend the same hours standing and have very different-looking legs. [1][2][5]

Standing at a counter for hours gives the calf muscles fewer chances to help pump blood back than walking around. Lakshmi notices that the heaviness is worse on her longest shifts. It eases when she gets home and rests with her legs raised. She tells her clinician about the change over the day. [1][2][5]

Small red or purple lines, often called spider veins, are different in appearance from the larger raised veins. The appearance alone gives limited information about deeper circulation. If you have pain, swelling or skin changes, describe those even if the visible veins look small. [1][3][6]

04

Why is the skin around my ankle changing?

When pressure stays high in the leg veins, fluid can collect in nearby tissues. That may cause swelling, especially around the ankle. Over time the skin can itch, become inflamed or darken. Some people develop a sore that takes a long time to heal, known as a venous leg ulcer. [1][3][5][7]

A hard, tender vein near the surface can be a sign of superficial vein thrombosis, sometimes called phlebitis. It deserves assessment, especially if symptoms spread or there is more general swelling. A sudden swollen, painful leg can also be a sign of deep vein thrombosis, a clot in the deeper veins. Those symptoms need urgent assessment. [3][4][8][9]

Bring up a new patch of darkened or itchy skin, a sore near the ankle, or any episode of bleeding from a vein. A clinician can help work out whether the changes are related to venous pressure and what assessment is needed. [3][5][7]

THE DEEP DIVEWhat a vein scan measures and which treatments can help
05

What does the vein scan show?

At the clinic, Lakshmi points to the vein that bothers her most. The clinician checks for swelling, changes to the skin and other possible causes of the symptoms. If she’s being assessed for a procedure, a duplex ultrasound can show both the veins and the direction of blood flow. [3][6]

During the scan, the examiner may ask her to stand or may squeeze and release part of the leg to check how well the valves work. The aim is to see which veins are affected and where reflux starts. The most obvious bulge may be fed by a vein running elsewhere in the leg. The scan helps the team plan treatment around that pattern. [5][6]

If compression is being considered, a clinician may also check circulation from the arteries, especially if there are signs it could be reduced. Strong compression can be unsuitable for some people with poor arterial blood flow. Ask whether stockings are appropriate for you and how to get them fitted. [3][5][10]

06

What can I do about the heaviness?

Lakshmi tries short walking breaks when the shop is quiet and puts her feet up when she gets home. Walking and calf movement can help blood return. Avoiding long spells of sitting or standing still and raising the legs when resting can ease symptoms for some people. If the skin is dry or itchy, ask about suitable skin care. [1][5][10]

Compression stockings apply pressure from the outside. When appropriate and fitted correctly, they can help with aching or swelling. They don’t repair faulty valves. They can be an option while waiting for treatment, when a procedure is unsuitable, or when you prefer to manage symptoms without a procedure. Local guidance can differ. [3][4][5][10]

Keep track of what the leg feels like before and after a shift. If heaviness still limits what you can do or the skin is changing, ask whether you need a vascular assessment. [1][3]

07

What can a procedure change?

If a scan finds reflux in a suitable surface vein, a treatment may close that vein so blood returns through other veins. Options include heat delivered from inside the vein, such as laser or radiofrequency, or foam injected to close it. Surgery remains an option in some cases. Which approach fits depends on the scan, your health and your preferences. [3][5][6]

In the UK CLASS randomised trial, people treated with laser or surgery reported better vein-specific quality of life at five years than those treated with foam. General quality-of-life measures were similar. Those results describe the treatments studied. They can inform a discussion about symptom relief, recovery, risks and the possibility of needing more treatment later. [11]

Venous ulcers can need specialist care too. In the EVRA trial, selected patients who had early treatment of reflux together with compression healed sooner on average than those whose vein treatment was deferred. An ulcer near your ankle deserves assessment so the team can confirm its cause and choose safe treatment. [3][7][12]

Care for an ulcer often includes a dressing and compression applied by a trained professional after checking arterial circulation. This is a different situation from buying stockings for tired legs. Ask who will change the dressing and what to do if the wound becomes more painful or you develop a fever. [5][7][15]

Lakshmi asks how soon she could return to standing at work if she chooses a procedure. She also asks whether more veins might appear later. Recovery and the chance of recurrence depend on the treatment and the underlying pattern of vein disease. [3][5]

08

When should I get help quickly?

Varicose veins usually allow time for a planned review. New symptoms or bleeding can change that. Tell your clinician about a sore that isn’t healing, new skin changes, or a hard, painful surface vein. [1][3][4]

If a varicose vein bleeds, lie down, raise the leg and press firmly on the spot with a clean cloth. Call your local emergency number if bleeding is heavy, does not stop with pressure, or you feel faint. Even if it stops, seek urgent medical assessment because the bleeding can recur. [3][4][13]

Seek urgent assessment for new swelling and pain in one leg. A deep vein clot is one possible cause. Call emergency services for sudden breathlessness, chest pain, coughing up blood or collapse, especially with leg symptoms. These can be signs of a clot in the lungs. [8][9][14]

Lakshmi now knows which changes to bring to her follow-up and which would make her seek help sooner. She also knows why the whole scan mattered more than the one vein she could see.

Mention aching, swelling and skin changes when you get your veins checked. Get urgent help for bleeding or sudden one-sided swelling. [3][8][13]

The fridge-door cheat sheet

Why do these veins bulge?

Weak valves can let blood flow backwards, raising pressure in veins near the skin. [1][2]

What can I do about heavy legs?

Try regular movement and put your legs up when you rest. Ask whether a vein scan or fitted stockings would help. [1][3][10]

When should I get help quickly?

Seek urgent assessment for bleeding from a vein or sudden swelling and pain in one leg. Call emergency services for heavy bleeding, sudden chest pain or breathlessness. [3][8][13][14]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Use this article to discuss leg symptoms, scan findings and treatment with your clinician. Ask them before changing any medicine. For severe symptoms, call your local emergency number immediately.
