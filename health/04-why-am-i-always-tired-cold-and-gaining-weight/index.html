HYPOTHYROIDISM

Why Am I Always Tired, Cold and Gaining Weight?

What an underactive thyroid can explain, how to read the blood tests and what treatment can change.

THE THYROIDTSH AND FREE T4WHAT TREATMENT CAN DO

01

The cardigan that stayed on

Nandini wakes after a full night’s sleep and still feels as if she could sleep another hour. At work, her colleagues turn on the fan. She keeps her cardigan on. Over several months she has gained weight, and going to the toilet has become less predictable. She wonders whether all of it could have one explanation.

An underactive thyroid, or hypothyroidism, is one possibility. The thyroid makes hormones that help regulate how the body uses energy. When levels are too low, you may feel colder and more tired than usual. Constipation, dry skin and changes in menstrual periods can occur too. Symptoms often come on gradually. [1][2]

The same symptoms can have other explanations. Anaemia or poor sleep can leave you exhausted, for instance. A clinician looks at the whole pattern and uses blood tests to find out whether the thyroid is involved. Nandini mentions how long she’s felt this way and brings a list of medicines and supplements to the appointment. [1][3][4]

02

What does this little gland actually do?

Place a finger at the lower front of your neck. Under the skin there’s a small gland shaped a little like a butterfly. That’s the thyroid. It releases hormones into the blood, mainly thyroxine, called T4. The body converts some T4 to the more active hormone T3. These hormones affect how cells use energy, how much heat the body makes and how fast the heart beats. [1][2][5]

A small gland at the base of the brain, the pituitary, helps regulate this system. It sends out thyroid-stimulating hormone, or TSH. When it senses too little thyroid hormone, it usually sends more TSH to ask the thyroid to work harder. As thyroid hormone levels rise, the signal normally eases. [2][5]

Think of it as a thermostat. TSH is the request to turn up the heat. T4 gives an idea of what the thyroid is supplying. A high TSH can be the signal of a thyroid that is struggling to respond. That is one reason a clinician may read the two numbers together. [4][5]

03

Why would the thyroid slow down?

One common cause is Hashimoto’s disease. The immune system normally helps protect you against infections. In Hashimoto’s, it targets the thyroid, and over time the gland may become less able to make enough hormone. Clinicians can sometimes find evidence of this in the blood by checking for antibodies against a thyroid enzyme called thyroid peroxidase, or TPO. [4][6]

An underactive thyroid can also follow thyroid surgery or radioactive iodine treatment. Some medicines, including lithium and amiodarone, can affect thyroid function. Tell your clinician what you take and why, so they can weigh the benefits of a medicine alongside its effect on your thyroid. Certain forms of thyroid inflammation, including after childbirth, can cause a temporary underactive phase. [1][7]

The gland needs iodine to make hormones, but both too little and too much can cause problems. Kelp or high-iodine supplements can be harmful in some thyroid conditions. Ask a clinician or pharmacist before using supplements marketed for thyroid support. [1][6][8]

04

Is the thyroid responsible for all the weight gain?

When Nandini steps on the scale, she wants to know how much of the change her thyroid could explain. Low thyroid hormone can contribute to weight gain. Some of the added weight comes from retaining salt and water. The amount varies, and a large weight change usually deserves a broader look. [1][9]

Treatment can help restore thyroid hormone levels. Some weight may come off as retained fluid settles, but restoring thyroid levels doesn’t guarantee a particular change on the scale. Taking extra thyroid hormone to lose weight can cause harm, including an irregular heartbeat and bone loss over time. [9][10]

It helps to tell your clinician what changed and when. If blood tests are back in range but tiredness or weight concerns continue, the clinician can review how the medicine is taken and consider other explanations. Nandini brings a short list of symptoms and dates so she can talk through the changes that matter most to her. [3][4][10]

05

How do TSH and free T4 fit together?

A clinician often starts with TSH when they suspect a problem in the thyroid gland itself. Free T4 is the portion of T4 circulating unbound in the blood. A high TSH with a low free T4 usually indicates overt primary hypothyroidism. If TSH is elevated but free T4 is still within range, the pattern is called subclinical hypothyroidism. A repeat test may help confirm that a mild change persists. [4][5]

There are exceptions. If the pituitary is the source of the problem, free T4 can be low while TSH is low or looks normal. Acute illness can also affect the results. And biotin, a vitamin present in some hair and nail supplements, can interfere with certain tests. Mention supplements, recent illnesses and pregnancy when you discuss your results. [4][5][11]

Nandini’s tests show high TSH and low free T4. The clinician explains what the combination means and reviews her history before discussing treatment. If you’re looking at your own report, ask how the two results fit together and whether either needs repeating. [4][5]

An antibody test answers a different question. It can help identify an autoimmune cause, while TSH and free T4 show how the gland is working. Some people have thyroid antibodies while their hormone levels are still in range. Their clinician may suggest checking thyroid function over time. Repeating the antibody test usually adds little once it has helped establish the cause. [4][5][6]

06

Does a slightly high TSH always need a tablet?

Subclinical hypothyroidism can prompt quite a different conversation from a low free T4. Clinicians may consider how far TSH is above the range, whether it stays there on repeat testing, symptoms, age and evidence of thyroid disease. Pregnancy calls for separate guidance. [4][12]

In the TRUST trial, 737 adults aged 65 and older with persistent subclinical hypothyroidism were assigned levothyroxine or a placebo. After one year, the treatment group’s TSH fell more, but the groups had similar changes in tiredness and thyroid-related symptoms. A review combining 21 randomised trials also found no meaningful average improvement in quality of life or thyroid-related symptoms in the adults studied. [13][14]

These findings help explain why a mild lab change may lead to monitoring first. The TRUST trial focused on older adults whose free T4 was in range. Nandini’s free T4 was low, so the clinician is answering a different question. She asks what treatment should improve and when she’ll have her next blood test. [4][13][14]

07

What happens once treatment starts?

For most people with confirmed primary hypothyroidism, the standard treatment is levothyroxine. It replaces T4, the hormone the thyroid would normally make. The clinician chooses a starting plan based on factors such as age and heart health, then uses follow-up blood tests to adjust it. Some symptoms take several weeks to improve. [2][4][15]

How consistently you take the medicine matters. Food and drinks such as tea or coffee can affect how much is absorbed. Iron, calcium and some antacids can also interfere. Nandini asks her pharmacist how to fit the tablet around breakfast and her iron supplement. If your routine or other medicines change, ask whether the timing needs to change too. [2][15]

Keep the follow-up appointment even if you feel better. Too much replacement can produce symptoms such as palpitations or tremor. If your thyroid results are stable and you still feel unwell, tell your clinician so they can review both the treatment and other potential causes. [4][10][15]

Before leaving, Nandini writes the date of her next blood test on the calendar.

08

When should I get help sooner?

Gradual tiredness, feeling cold and weight change are reasons to book a medical review. If you already have hypothyroidism and are planning a pregnancy or have just found out you’re pregnant, contact your clinician promptly. Thyroid hormone needs can change early, and blood tests and treatment may need a prompt review. [3][12][16]

Call your local emergency number if someone is difficult to wake, develops severe confusion, or is breathing very slowly or struggling to breathe. A rare emergency linked to severe hypothyroidism is called myxoedema crisis. It can involve marked drowsiness, low body temperature and slow breathing, sometimes during another illness. These signs require emergency help whatever the cause. [17][18]

Nandini still brings a cardigan to work. She has a clearer idea of what her thyroid can explain and what her clinician will recheck. She has also written down a question for the next visit: “If my numbers improve and I still feel tired, what else should we look at?” [4]

Ask what your TSH and free T4 mean together, why treatment or monitoring is recommended, and when to check again. [4][5]

The fridge-door cheat sheet

Could it be my thyroid?

Feeling cold and tired can have several causes. Blood tests help check whether your thyroid is involved. [1][4]

What do the two numbers tell me?

In primary hypothyroidism, a high TSH with low free T4 usually means the thyroid is underactive. A high TSH with free T4 in range may be rechecked. [4][5]

When should I act sooner?

Contact your clinician promptly about pregnancy if you have hypothyroidism. Call emergency services for severe confusion, very slow breathing or difficulty waking someone. [12][17][18]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Use this article to discuss thyroid symptoms, blood tests and treatment with your clinician. Ask them before changing any medicine. For severe symptoms, call your local emergency number immediately.
