ACID REFLUX (GERD)

Why Does My Chest Burn After Dinner or When I Lie Down?

Why stomach contents can rise after a meal, what changes when you lie down and when chest symptoms need urgent care.

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The burn that arrived after dinner

Kavita finishes dinner late, clears the kitchen and lies down to look at her phone. A warm feeling starts behind her breastbone. Then it climbs towards her throat. She sits up and notices a sour taste. “There it is again,” she tells her husband. At her appointment, she calls the feeling “acidity.” Her clinician asks where the burn starts, what brings it on and whether she ever feels it when walking or climbing stairs.

One possible explanation is reflux. That means stomach contents travel back into the oesophagus, the food pipe that connects your throat and stomach. The liquid can irritate the lining and cause heartburn. If it comes high enough, you may taste sour liquid or notice food coming back up. Doctors call this regurgitation. [1][2]

An occasional episode is common. When reflux repeatedly causes troublesome symptoms or leads to complications, doctors call it gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, or GERD. The timing and sour taste are useful clues. Chest discomfort can also come from the heart, so a new or concerning episode needs medical attention. [2][3][4]

02

What usually keeps stomach contents down?

When you swallow, waves of muscle move food down the oesophagus. At the bottom is a ring of muscle called the lower oesophageal sphincter. It relaxes to let food into the stomach and normally tightens again. The diaphragm, the broad muscle you use to breathe, also helps reinforce the barrier where the food pipe passes through it. [1][2]

Think of the join as a gate. It opens for traffic going down. It can also relax briefly when you aren’t swallowing. If the barrier opens at the wrong time or is weak, stomach contents can travel up. In a hiatus hernia, part of the stomach slides or bulges through the opening in the diaphragm. That can make the barrier less effective. [1][2][5]

The stomach makes acid as part of digestion. Reflux can happen even when the amount of acid is ordinary. The location matters. Acid and other stomach contents in the food pipe can cause irritation because the food pipe and stomach have different linings. [1][2][5]

03

Why is it worse when I lie down?

When Kavita is upright, gravity helps keep stomach contents down and helps the oesophagus clear liquid that comes back up. When she lies flat, she loses that advantage. During sleep, she also swallows less and makes less saliva. Both normally help clear or neutralise acid. An episode during sleep can therefore leave acid in contact with the food pipe for longer. [2][6]

A full stomach after a late meal can make the timing matter even more. The American College of Gastroenterology suggests leaving two to three hours between eating and bedtime if nighttime reflux is a problem. Raising the head end of the bed or using a wedge that lifts the upper body can also help some people. Piling pillows under your head may bend you at the waist. [2][6][7]

Sleeping on the left side may reduce nighttime reflux for some people. It changes how the stomach and its contents sit relative to the food pipe. Kavita starts by trying an earlier dinner and keeping track of whether she wakes with the same burn. [2][6]

04

Is “acidity” the same as an ulcer?

People use “acidity” for several kinds of discomfort. The burn behind the breastbone with sour fluid coming back up fits a reflux pattern. A peptic ulcer is a sore in the lining of the stomach or the first part of the small intestine, called the duodenum. Its most common symptom is upper abdominal pain, sometimes burning. Some ulcers cause no symptoms until a complication occurs. [1][8]

Two common causes of ulcers are infection with a bacterium called H. pylori and use of certain anti-inflammatory painkillers, such as ibuprofen or naproxen. Spicy food can bring on reflux symptoms for some people. Timing after a spicy meal tells you little about whether an ulcer is present. [2][8][9]

Acid-reducing medicines can relieve symptoms in both conditions. Feeling better after taking one doesn’t settle the diagnosis. An ulcer caused by H. pylori needs treatment aimed at the infection. If you keep getting symptoms, explain what you feel and where you feel it. Bring a list of the medicines you take, including painkillers. [2][9][10]

05

What does the research say about nighttime reflux?

Kavita wants to know whether lifting the bed is worth the trouble. In one small randomised crossover trial, 39 adults who were already receiving reflux treatment tried periods with and without the head of the bed raised. Symptom scores improved more often during the elevated period. Because the trial was small and the participants were already taking medication, it gives a reason to try the approach for nighttime symptoms with modest expectations. [11]

A review of studies on nighttime reflux also found support for raising the head of the bed, allowing more time between dinner and bed, and sleeping on the left side. Researchers studied different groups and measures, so there is no single prediction for how much each change will help an individual. The American College of Gastroenterology grades its recommendations on meal timing and bed elevation as conditional, based on low-quality evidence. [2][6]

Track a few details if the episodes keep recurring. Note when you ate, when you lay down and whether symptoms woke you. Also note whether they came on with exertion. That detail can help your clinician assess chest discomfort. [2][4][7]

06

Will I need a camera test?

When symptoms are typical and there are no warning signs, a clinician may start with a conversation and a trial of treatment. They’ll ask about the pattern, other health conditions and medicines. If you have trouble swallowing, unexplained weight loss or signs of bleeding, an upper endoscopy may be needed earlier. A thin flexible camera lets the clinician inspect the food pipe and stomach and take small samples when needed. [2][12][13]

An endoscopy can show inflammation or narrowing. Some people with reflux have a normal-looking food pipe. If the diagnosis is still unclear, a clinician may suggest reflux monitoring. A sensor measures acid exposure, and some tests can also detect the movement of liquid and gas. Recording symptoms during testing can help show whether they line up with reflux episodes. [2][12][13]

Long-term reflux can lead to complications in some people, such as scar-related narrowing or changes in the lining called Barrett’s oesophagus. Whether you need testing depends on symptoms and risk factors. Ask what question a proposed test would answer. [2][3]

07

What can I change, and how do medicines help?

Kavita works late twice a week. On those days, she tries eating earlier at work instead of having a large meal just before bed. At home, she looks for foods that reliably bring on symptoms. A diary can help identify a personal trigger. If excess weight is contributing, weight loss can improve symptoms. Avoiding tobacco may also help. [2][7][14]

Some products work quickly on symptoms. Antacids neutralise acid already in the stomach, while alginates form a barrier over stomach contents. Proton pump inhibitors, or PPIs, reduce acid production and can help an inflamed food pipe heal. The choice depends on how often symptoms occur and whether reflux has caused complications. Discuss recurring symptoms or repeated use of over-the-counter products with a clinician or pharmacist. [2][7][10][15]

If a treatment has helped, ask when to review it and what to do if symptoms return. If it hasn’t helped enough, tell your clinician what you tried and when you took it. The next step may be to review the diagnosis or check for a different cause. [2][10][13]

08

Which warning signs change what I should do?

The timing of a burn can be a clue, but a heart attack can also feel like burning or indigestion. Even a symptom that comes after dinner should be taken seriously if it is new or concerning. [4][16]

Call your local emergency number for possible heart attack symptoms. Seek emergency help for new chest pressure, pain or burning that persists or comes and goes, especially with shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, light-headedness or discomfort spreading to the arm, jaw or back. If you suspect a heart attack, call immediately. [4][16]

Get emergency medical care for vomiting blood or passing black, tarry stools. Vomit that looks like coffee grounds may also signal bleeding. Feeling faint, weak or clammy with these symptoms adds urgency. [8][10][17]

Arrange prompt medical assessment if food keeps sticking, swallowing hurts, you’re losing weight without trying, or you have repeated vomiting. Go to emergency care if food is stuck and you cannot swallow saliva. [1][2][18]

For recurring heartburn, note the timing and get it reviewed. For possible heart attack symptoms or signs of bleeding, get emergency help. [4][7][17]

The fridge-door cheat sheet

Why does lying down matter?

Gravity helps clear reflux when you’re upright. Lying down soon after dinner can make symptoms worse. [2][6]

Is this an ulcer?

Reflux reaches the food pipe. A peptic ulcer is a sore in the stomach or duodenum. Recurring symptoms deserve a review. [1][7][8]

When do I get emergency help?

Get emergency help for possible heart attack symptoms, vomiting blood or black, tarry stools. [4][16][17]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Use this article to discuss reflux symptoms, diagnosis and treatment with your clinician. Ask them before changing any medicine. For severe symptoms, call your local emergency number immediately.
