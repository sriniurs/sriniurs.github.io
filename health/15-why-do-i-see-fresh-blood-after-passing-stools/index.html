HAEMORRHOIDS (PILES)

Why Do I See Fresh Blood After Passing Stools?

What piles and fissures can look like, why the source of bleeding matters and when to get help.

PILES AND FISSURESFINDING THE SOURCEWHEN TO GET HELP

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The red streak she saw on the paper

After several days of constipation, Geeta passes a hard stool. On the toilet paper she sees a thin streak of bright red blood. She feels fine otherwise. A friend says it sounds like piles, and Geeta wonders whether to wait and see. When it happens again, she books an appointment and tells the clinician exactly what she saw.

Fresh red blood after a bowel movement can come from haemorrhoids, also called piles, or a small tear at the opening called an anal fissure. A hard stool and straining can contribute to either. The colour and timing are useful clues, but several conditions can cause rectal bleeding. [1][2][3]

Geeta’s clinician asks whether the blood was on the paper, on the outside of the stool or mixed through it. She asks whether passing the stool hurt and if Geeta’s bowel habits have changed. Those details help decide whether an examination near the opening is enough or whether the bowel needs a closer look. [2][4][5]

02

What are piles, and how do they bleed?

The anal canal contains soft cushions made of blood vessels and supporting tissue. They help seal the opening. If the cushions enlarge or shift down and cause symptoms, we call them haemorrhoids. Internal haemorrhoids are inside the canal. External haemorrhoids are under the skin around the opening. [4][6]

Internal haemorrhoids commonly cause bright red bleeding with little or no pain. Blood can appear on the paper, streak the stool or drip into the bowl. Sometimes the tissue slips out during a bowel movement, which is called prolapse. It may go back on its own or remain outside. Tell the clinician if you’ve noticed a lump and what happens to it afterwards. [1][4][6]

External haemorrhoids can itch or feel sore. If a clot forms in one, it may appear suddenly as a firm and very tender lump. That pattern feels quite different from a painless streak of blood. The clinician can distinguish it from other causes of a painful lump. [1][4][6]

03

How can constipation make it worse?

When a stool is hard or difficult to pass, you may bear down for longer. Straining and spending a long time on the toilet are associated with haemorrhoid symptoms. Pregnancy can also make them more likely. The pressure and repeated irritation can contribute to swelling or bleeding. [1][4][7]

Geeta says she often takes her phone into the bathroom and sits long after she has finished. She also puts off the urge when she’s at work. Her clinician talks through habits that can make a bowel movement less of an effort, such as responding to the urge when possible and getting up when finished. [4][7][8]

Hard stool can also tear the delicate skin at the opening. That is how a fissure can start. Two people may see the same red streak after constipation for different reasons, which is why it helps to describe how it felt as well as what it looked like. [2][3]

04

Is it a fissure or something farther up?

A fissure often causes a sharp, cutting pain as the stool passes. Burning pain can continue afterwards, and there may be a little fresh blood. Internal haemorrhoids often bleed without that sharp pain, although a prolapsed haemorrhoid or a clot in an external one can hurt. The patterns can overlap. [1][3][4]

Blood can also come from farther up the bowel. Inflammation, polyps, diverticular disease and bowel cancer are among the possible causes. Tell your clinician about blood mixed with stool, new diarrhoea or constipation, abdominal pain, unexplained weight loss or unusual tiredness. These details can change which tests are appropriate. [2][4][9][10]

Even when an examination finds haemorrhoids, the clinician may need to consider another source. If bleeding keeps coming back or continues after treatment, report it. Geeta asks what should prompt a return visit if her bleeding settles for a few days and then returns. [4][10]

Mention any past episodes and whether you ever had tests for them. Bleeding that looks familiar can still deserve a new assessment if it lasts longer, happens more often or comes with other symptoms. If the plan is to monitor, ask who to contact and how soon if the bleeding continues. [2][4][10]

05

What might the clinician check?

The visit starts with a description of the bleeding, how often it happens and what else you’ve noticed. Mention medicines such as aspirin or blood thinners and any family history of bowel cancer. Ask before changing a prescribed medicine. The clinician may inspect the area and, with your agreement, do a gloved finger examination. A small instrument called an anoscope can help them look inside the anal canal. [2][4][5]

Some people are offered a blood count to check for anaemia, a stool test such as a faecal immunochemical test or FIT, or a colonoscopy to examine the large bowel. The choice depends on your symptoms, age, previous tests and risk factors. A low FIT result may guide what happens next. Persistent unexplained symptoms still need follow-up. [4][10][11]

Geeta tells the clinician when the spotting started, how much she saw and whether the stool had changed. She asks what the examination found and what the plan is if she sees blood again. [4][5][10]

If an examination feels awkward to talk about, you can still ask what each step involves before it starts. You can describe symptoms in everyday words. Saying “a spot on the paper” or “enough to turn the water red” is often more practical than trying to estimate millilitres. [2][5]

06

Does fibre actually help?

The first steps for many people with confirmed haemorrhoids aim to make stools softer and easier to pass. A Cochrane review of randomised trials found that fibre supplements reduced the relative risk of ongoing bleeding by about half compared with control. The trials were small. Evidence for relief of pain, itching or prolapse was less clear. [4][12]

Geeta starts with changes that fit her meals. Lentils, vegetables, fruit and whole grains are ways to get more fibre. Add it gradually and drink enough fluid for your needs, unless your clinician has given you a fluid limit. If constipation continues, ask whether a fibre supplement or a suitable laxative would help. [7][8][13]

Try to avoid forcing a stool or sitting on the toilet longer than needed. The aim is to pass a soft, formed stool with less effort. If bowel habits change unexpectedly or bleeding persists, tell the clinician even if increasing fibre has helped the constipation. [4][7][10]

07

When might a procedure help?

For mild symptoms, a clinician or pharmacist may suggest a short course of a topical product for discomfort or itching. These products won’t establish why you’re bleeding. If bleeding or prolapse continues after changes to bowel habits, your clinician may discuss an office procedure for confirmed internal haemorrhoids. [4][7][8][14]

A common option is rubber-band ligation. A clinician puts a small band at the base of an internal haemorrhoid so the tissue shrinks and drops away. Other options include injection or heat-based treatment. Surgery may be considered for large external or combined haemorrhoids, or when symptoms persist despite other treatments. [4][6][8][14]

Ask which symptom a procedure is meant to improve, what recovery usually involves and what to do if bleeding returns. Tell the team if you take medicines that affect clotting so they can plan around them. [4][14]

Geeta asks what improvement would look like if she starts by changing her bowel habits. She and her clinician agree to review whether bowel movements are easier and whether the bleeding has stopped. Keeping track of both gives them a better basis for deciding what to do next. [4][8]

08

When should I get help quickly?

Arrange a medical review for unexplained rectal bleeding, particularly if it comes back. Mention any change in your bowel habits, weight loss or tiredness. If you already have haemorrhoids, a new pattern still deserves attention. [2][4][10]

Go to emergency care if bleeding is heavy, won’t stop, or there are large clots. Call your local emergency number if you feel faint or clammy, collapse, or are too unwell to travel safely. These can be signs of significant blood loss. [2][9][15]

Seek urgent assessment for black, tarry stools, dark red stool, bloody diarrhoea, or severe anal pain with fever. If you also have heavy bleeding, feel faint or become severely unwell, get emergency help. [2][7][8][9][15]

Geeta still remembers that first red streak. She now has a clearer way to describe it and knows which changes would make her seek help sooner.

Get unexplained bleeding checked. Once the cause is clear, ask how to make stools easier to pass and when to come back. [2][4][7]

The fridge-door cheat sheet

Does fresh blood mean piles?

Piles and fissures can both cause fresh blood. A clinician can check for other causes. [1][2][3]

What details should I share?

Say how much blood you saw, where it appeared, whether it hurt and whether bowel habits changed. [2][4][5]

When should I get emergency help?

Get emergency help for heavy or ongoing bleeding, large clots, faintness or collapse. [2][9][15]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Use this article to discuss rectal bleeding, examination and follow-up with your clinician. Ask them before changing any medicine. For severe symptoms, call your local emergency number immediately.
