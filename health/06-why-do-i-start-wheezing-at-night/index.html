ASTHMA

Why Do I Start Wheezing at Night or When the Weather Changes?

What makes the breathing tubes narrow, why symptoms come and go, and how an asthma plan helps.

NARROWED AIRWAYSNIGHT AND WEATHERWHEN TO GET HELP

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The whistle that woke him up

Arun wakes before dawn with a dry cough. When he breathes out, he hears a faint whistle. It happened last week after he walked home in cool air. By breakfast he feels well enough to go to work. When the clinician listens to his chest that afternoon, the whistle has gone.

That changing pattern can be a clue to asthma. Asthma affects the airways, the branching tubes that carry air into and out of your lungs. These tubes can become inflamed and unusually sensitive. At times they narrow, making breathing out harder and causing a wheeze, cough, chest tightness or shortness of breath. Later, the airways can open up and symptoms may ease. [1][2]

Other conditions can also cause wheezing or a persistent cough, so the clinician asks what happened before each episode and arranges breathing tests. Arun remembers the nights that woke him and the walk in cold air. Feeling better in the clinic still leaves an important story to tell. [1][3]

02

What happens inside a wheezing airway?

Imagine looking straight down a breathing tube. On a good day there’s room for air to move. When asthma flares, the muscle wrapped around the tube can tighten, the lining can swell, and extra mucus can collect inside. All three can leave less space for air. The tubes also naturally get a little narrower as you breathe out, which helps explain why an asthma wheeze is often easier to hear then. [1][4]

Wheeze is only one possible symptom. Some people mainly cough at night. Others notice a tight chest or get breathless when they run. The same person can feel different from one episode to the next. In a very severe attack there may be little air movement and less wheezing. How hard it is to breathe matters more than how loud the whistle sounds. [1][2][5]

The swelling and the muscle squeeze help explain why inhalers can have different jobs. One can help open the narrowed tubes quickly. Another reduces inflammation, and some inhalers combine both jobs. [1][6]

03

Why does it happen at night?

The body runs on an internal clock. In a laboratory study, researchers varied sleep and activity schedules to help separate that clock from what people were doing. Lung function in participants with asthma was lowest during their biological night. The study suggests the clock itself can contribute to nighttime symptoms. It doesn’t tell us why Arun woke coughing on any particular night. [7]

The bedroom can add other factors. Bedding can harbour dust mites. Damp rooms can have mould. Whether either matters depends on what a person is exposed to and what sets off their symptoms. Mention other nighttime symptoms too, such as a sour taste after lying down, because reflux and other conditions can contribute to cough. [1][8][9]

Repeated waking is worth bringing up even when you feel fine during the day. In someone with asthma, waking with cough, wheeze or breathlessness can be a sign that the current plan needs review. Arun starts noting how often he wakes and whether he needs his reliever. [1][3][6]

04

What does a change in weather have to do with it?

Cold, dry air can trigger symptoms in sensitive airways. Windy days can change what you breathe in, and pollen levels vary with the season and weather. For some people with pollen allergy, certain thunderstorms can trigger severe symptoms. Not every storm carries that risk, and not everyone with asthma responds to the same conditions. [1][8][10]

Other triggers can overlap with the weather. A viral cold, smoke, air pollution or exercise may be part of the same day’s story. If you wheeze outdoors and again when cleaning a dusty room, note both situations. A short record of what you were doing and where you were can be more informative than the temperature alone. [2][8]

Arun wants to keep walking, so he asks how to do it safely. With well-managed asthma, most people can stay active. Symptoms that repeatedly force you to slow down or stop deserve review. Ask about a plan that fits your normal activity and the conditions that bring symptoms on. [1][6]

05

How do the tests catch something that comes and goes?

The history is the starting point. The clinician asks about nighttime symptoms, exercise, illnesses and exposures at home or work. Then they look for objective evidence. In spirometry you take a full breath and blow into a machine that measures how much air you breathe out and how quickly. The test may be repeated after a medicine that opens the airways. A clear change can support an asthma diagnosis. [1][3][11]

An exam or breathing test can look normal on a good day. If asthma is still suspected, the clinician may repeat testing, ask you to record peak flow at home, or arrange other tests. Peak flow measures how fast you can blow out. A breath test called FeNO can help identify a type of airway inflammation, but the result needs to be interpreted with your other findings. [1][3][11]

If you already use an inhaler, bring it and explain when you last used it. Treatment can change test results. Follow the clinic’s preparation instructions. [1][3]

06

Why do inhalers have different jobs?

A reliever is used when symptoms flare. A bronchodilator in the reliever helps relax the muscle around the airways. An inhaled corticosteroid, often called an ICS, reduces inflammation and helps prevent attacks. Some inhalers combine an ICS with a fast-acting bronchodilator. Depending on the prescription, one combination inhaler can sometimes serve as both regular treatment and a reliever. [1][6][12]

There’s evidence behind treating the inflammation as well as the squeeze. In the 2019 Novel START trial, adults with mild asthma using an as-needed anti-inflammatory combination inhaler had about half the annual rate of asthma flare-ups as those using a quick-relief bronchodilator alone. The trial compared specific medicines in adults over one year. A clinician chooses the plan that fits your age, diagnosis and circumstances. [1][13]

Bring your inhalers to a visit and show how you use them. Different devices require different techniques. A spacer can help with some pressurised inhalers. Ask which device to reach for during symptoms, since the colour alone can be misleading. [3][6][12]

If symptoms keep breaking through, your clinician can check how often you are able to take your treatment, whether the device suits you, and whether something else is contributing. If cost or access makes the plan hard to follow, say so at the visit. [1][3]

07

What belongs in my asthma action plan?

Arun asks for a written plan he can keep on his phone. It says what he uses on ordinary days, what to do when symptoms start worsening, and when to get urgent help. Some plans use symptoms alone. Others also use peak flow compared with the person’s own best reading. Ask your clinician to walk through an example, such as waking wheezy at two in the morning. [1][6][14]

Make the plan specific to your inhalers. Write down the names and check which one is the reliever. Keep track of waking at night, needing more reliever than usual, or finding it harder to do everyday activities. Those changes can be a reason to contact your care team before an attack gets worse. [3][6][14]

At his next visit, Arun brings his inhalers and the notes he’s made. That gives them something concrete to review, including whether he can walk his usual route and whether he’s sleeping through the night. [3][6]

08

When is breathing trouble an emergency?

If symptoms are getting worse, follow your personal action plan and use your prescribed reliever as directed. A severe attack can develop quickly. Know the signs that mean it’s time to call for help. [5][15]

Call your local emergency number immediately if breathing is very hard, you can’t speak in full sentences, lips look blue or grey, or you become drowsy or confused. Get emergency help if symptoms worsen quickly or the prescribed reliever isn’t helping. Sit upright if you can and follow your action plan and the dispatcher’s instructions while help is on the way. [5][15][16]

Arrange a prompt review after an attack, even if you feel better. Bring your inhalers and tell your care team what happened, what you took, and how quickly it helped. They can check your technique and update the plan with you. [1][15]

Arun keeps the plan easy to find. He and his family now know what to do about an ordinary bad night and which signs mean they should call for help.

Night waking, wheeze with activity and needing more reliever are worth reviewing. Keep a written plan and act quickly if breathing becomes severe. [1][3][5]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Use this article to discuss breathing symptoms, diagnosis and treatment with your clinician. Ask them before changing any medicine. For severe symptoms, call your local emergency number immediately.
