RECURRENT MOUTH ULCERS

Why Do I Keep Getting Mouth Ulcers?

Why small sores can keep returning, what can ease the pain and when a sore needs checking.

CANKER SORESWHY THEY RETURNWHEN TO GET HELP

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The tiny sore that spoiled breakfast

Meera takes the first bite of her morning toast and feels a sharp sting inside her cheek. In the mirror she sees a small round sore with a pale centre. She had something similar last month, but it was on the inside of her lower lip and healed. She wonders why these spots keep appearing and whether it means she’s missing a vitamin.

One common possibility is a recurrent aphthous ulcer, sometimes called a canker sore. These painful sores tend to appear inside the mouth and heal between episodes. Most small ones settle within one to two weeks. A sore that never fully heals, especially one that stays in the same place, needs a different assessment. [1][2][3]

At her dental visit, Meera explains where the last sore was and how long it took to heal. Her dentist asks whether there was a period when her mouth felt completely normal. That detail helps distinguish several separate episodes from one persistent sore. [2][3][4]

02

What is the pale patch in the middle?

The lining of your mouth is normally a continuous surface. An ulcer is a small break in that lining. A typical small aphthous ulcer is shallow and round or oval, often with a pale grey or yellow centre and a red rim. That pale coating can be part of the ulcer itself. Its colour alone doesn’t establish that there’s an infection. [2][3][4]

Small aphthous ulcers often appear on softer areas such as the inside of the lips and cheeks or under the tongue. They can hurt out of proportion to their size, especially when food brushes against them or something acidic reaches the sore. Some people get larger or multiple sores, which may take longer to heal and deserve assessment. [1][2][3][5]

Meera asks whether she can give the sore to someone else. A typical aphthous ulcer isn’t contagious. Cold sores are caused by a herpes virus and often appear as grouped blisters on or around the lip, though herpes can affect the inside of the mouth too. Ask if it’s unclear which you have. [1][4][6]

03

Did I bite my cheek, or is this something different?

An accidental bite, a sharp tooth, a rough filling or a denture that rubs can leave a sore. The location can be a clue. If a sore consistently develops next to the same sharp edge, a dentist can look for a source of repeated injury. If the sore persists after the rubbing has been addressed, it needs to be checked again. [1][4][7]

Recurrent aphthous ulcers can appear with no obvious injury. Some people report episodes during stress or after minor injury, and family tendency may play a part. Often there is no single cause to identify. A spicy or sharp food can sting a sore that is already there, so pain at a meal isn’t enough to pin down what started it. [1][2][3][5]

Write down anything that repeats across episodes, including a dental change, a new medicine or where the sore appeared. Meera takes a photo on the first day and another several days later. The two pictures can help show whether it shrank and healed. [4][5]

04

Could it be iron, vitamin B12 or something else?

Some people with recurring ulcers have low iron, vitamin B12 or folate. A clinician can decide whether blood tests are appropriate based on how often the sores occur, how they look and whether there are other clues. Tiredness, dietary limitations and symptoms of a gut condition are worth mentioning. Many people with typical aphthous ulcers have no identifiable deficiency. [2][3][5]

A mouth ulcer can occasionally be part of a wider condition. Tell the clinician about persistent diarrhoea, unexplained weight loss, ulcers elsewhere on the body, joint symptoms or a painful red eye. These details can change what needs to be checked. Conditions such as coeliac disease, Crohn’s disease and Behçet’s disease are among the possibilities in the right setting. [3][5][8][9]

If you take a supplement, bring its name and explain why you started it. A clinician can advise whether there is a deficiency to treat and whether you need tests for its underlying cause. [3][5]

05

What will my dentist or clinician look for?

The visit usually starts with how the sores behave. Say how often they appear, how many you get, how long each lasts and whether it heals completely. Bring photos if the sore has gone. The clinician may check the teeth, tongue, cheeks and gums and, if appropriate, feel for a lump in the neck. [4][5][7]

A typical short-lived aphthous ulcer can often be recognised from the history and examination. Frequent or unusually severe episodes may prompt selected blood tests or a referral to oral medicine. A persistent or unusual sore may need a specialist assessment and sometimes a small tissue sample, called a biopsy, to work out what it is. [3][4][5][7]

Tell the dentist if you use tobacco or chew areca nut, also called supari, including in paan or paan masala. These exposures can increase the risk of mouth cancer. A persistent sore needs checking whatever your history. [10][11]

06

What can make eating less painful?

While a typical small ulcer is healing, try softer foods and cool drinks if they feel comfortable. Very spicy, acidic, salty or sharp foods can sting, so take a break from anything that makes the sore hurt. A soft toothbrush may be easier to use. Keep drinking, and seek help if pain is making that difficult. [1][2][12]

A dentist or pharmacist can advise on products for pain or irritation. For diagnosed aphthous ulcers, a clinician may recommend a topical corticosteroid in some cases. Treatment studies suggest some topical options can improve pain or healing, but the comparisons and results vary. Ask what the product is meant to improve and whether it suits the type of sore you have. [2][5][13]

Meera’s dentist says to come back if this sore behaves differently from the others. A product that makes it hurt less can be helpful while it heals. It doesn’t tell her what caused it or whether a persistent ulcer needs further assessment. [1][4][7]

07

What if they keep interrupting my meals?

Meera notices she’s had several episodes close together. She writes down how many days each sore lasted and which meals she skipped. A short record can show how much trouble the ulcers cause. It can also help your clinician distinguish occasional small ulcers from frequent episodes that warrant a closer look. [2][5]

If a tooth or denture is rubbing, ask whether it can be adjusted. If the cause appears to be recurrent aphthous ulcers, ask what to do at the start of an episode and when to review the plan. Some people with severe or very frequent episodes benefit from seeing an oral medicine specialist. [2][5][7]

Tell the dentist if you’re avoiding most meals or struggling to drink. Also mention if you have several ulcers at once or new ones appear before the earlier ones heal. Those details can change how soon you’re seen and whether you need more help with pain or further investigation. [2][5][7]

Meera and her dentist agree on what to track next time. She’s looking for fewer days with pain when eating, and a clear return to normal between episodes. She’ll contact the clinic sooner if a sore looks different or fails to heal. [2][4][5]

08

When should I get help sooner?

A small familiar ulcer that heals is different from a spot that stays put. If a sore is getting larger, bleeds repeatedly or comes with a lump or a red or white patch that persists, arrange a prompt dental or medical assessment. [1][7][10]

Get an unexplained mouth ulcer checked promptly if it has lasted more than three weeks. A long-lasting sore can sometimes be a sign of mouth cancer, so it may need an urgent referral. Seek assessment earlier if it is worrying you or is clearly getting worse. [1][7][10]

Seek urgent help if mouth pain stops you drinking enough. Passing much less urine or feeling dizzy on standing can be signs of dehydration. Call your local emergency number if you cannot swallow saliva, have sudden swelling of the tongue or throat, or are struggling to breathe. [12][14][15]
