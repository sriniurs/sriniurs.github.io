ANAEMIA

Why Do I Feel Exhausted After Climbing Just One Flight of Stairs?

How blood carries oxygen, why haemoglobin can fall and what finding the cause can change.

CARRYING OXYGENFINDING THE CAUSEWHEN TO GET HELP

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The landing where she had to stop

Meena usually takes the stairs to her flat. Lately she has been pausing halfway up, grocery bag in one hand, waiting for her breathing to settle. It happens even when the bag is light. At her appointment, she says she feels exhausted after one flight. Her clinician orders a blood count along with an assessment of her symptoms. The result shows a low haemoglobin level.

Haemoglobin is the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. When there is too little haemoglobin in your blood, you have anaemia. Your muscles need more oxygen when you climb stairs, and anaemia can make that effort feel harder than it used to. You may notice tiredness, breathlessness, a fast heartbeat or dizziness. Some people with mild anaemia notice very little. [1][2][3]

The result gives Meena a clue and opens a second question. Why is her haemoglobin low? Breathlessness can also come from the heart, lungs or other causes, so the clinician considers the blood count alongside her examination and history. [2][4]

02

What does haemoglobin do on the way upstairs?

When you breathe in, oxygen moves from your lungs into your blood and binds to haemoglobin. Red blood cells carry it around the body. In tissues such as your leg muscles, haemoglobin releases oxygen that the cells can use. Iron is part of the structure that allows haemoglobin to bind oxygen. [1][2][5]

Think of the red cells as delivery vehicles. When you sit still, your muscles need less oxygen than when you climb stairs. With anaemia, the blood has less capacity to carry oxygen, so exertion may bring out symptoms first. How quickly anaemia develops matters too. Someone whose haemoglobin fell gradually may feel quite different from a person whose level fell suddenly. [1][3]

The number to look for on a full blood count is haemoglobin, often written Hb. Clinicians interpret it using factors such as age, sex and pregnancy. A value outside the expected range helps identify anaemia. Its cause still needs to be worked out. [2][7][8]

03

Where can all that iron go?

Iron deficiency is a common cause of anaemia. It can develop when you lose iron through bleeding, get too little over time, need more than usual, or have difficulty absorbing it. Heavy periods can gradually drain iron stores. Pregnancy increases iron needs. Some people lose small amounts of blood from the digestive tract and never see it. [1][5][9]

Meena mentions that her periods have become heavier. She had treated that as a separate nuisance. The clinician asks how often she has to change protection, how long bleeding lasts and whether it is affecting her usual activities. They also discuss whether she takes anti-inflammatory painkillers, which can contribute to bleeding in the digestive tract. [5][9][10]

Iron can also be harder to absorb in conditions such as coeliac disease or after certain stomach or bowel operations. The same low haemoglobin can come from very different circumstances. A useful history includes changes in bleeding, stomach or bowel symptoms, medicines, diet and past operations. [5][9][11]

04

Can anaemia come from something other than low iron?

Yes. Vitamin B12 and folate help the bone marrow make healthy red blood cells. A shortage of either can lead to anaemia, often with larger-than-usual red cells. Vitamin B12 deficiency can also cause tingling, numbness or problems with balance. Those nerve symptoms can appear even when a blood count shows no anaemia. Tell your clinician about them early. [2][12][13]

Long-term inflammation can make it harder for the body to use stored iron. The kidneys also make a hormone that tells the bone marrow to produce red cells. When kidney function is impaired, there may be less of that signal. Inherited conditions such as thalassaemia can affect how haemoglobin is made. These problems need different assessments and treatment. [1][2][14]

This is why two people with similar haemoglobin levels can leave the clinic with different plans. One may need iron and an investigation for bleeding. Another may need tests for vitamin deficiency or a long-term condition. [2][9][12][14]

THE DEEP DIVEWhat the blood tests show and why the cause matters
05

What do ferritin and red-cell size tell me?

The full blood count can show the average size of your red cells, called the mean corpuscular volume or MCV. Small cells can point towards iron deficiency, but they can also occur in thalassaemia. Larger cells may prompt a check for vitamin B12 or folate deficiency. A cell size within range can occur with several causes, especially early on or when more than one problem is present. [2][8][9][12]

Ferritin is a protein that stores iron. A low blood ferritin is a useful clue that stores are depleted. Inflammation can raise ferritin, so a result in range may need a closer look. Recent American Society of Hematology guidance suggests using ferritin with another measure, transferrin saturation, when assessing iron deficiency in adults with anaemia of inflammation. [9][15]

Meena’s haemoglobin and ferritin are both low. That supports iron-deficiency anaemia. Her clinician still asks what could have depleted her iron and whether her heavier periods explain the whole picture. [5][9]

Iron stores can run low before haemoglobin drops below range. That’s called iron deficiency without anaemia. The two results answer different questions, so it helps to ask which one is low and what follow-up is appropriate. Your clinician may interpret the same ferritin result differently if you have an illness that causes inflammation. [9][15]

06

Why look for bleeding if iron can replace what I lost?

Replacing iron can help rebuild haemoglobin and iron stores. If bleeding continues, though, iron can keep being lost. Finding and treating the source matters. For Meena, that includes a review of her heavy periods and a discussion about what has changed. In other people, the history may point towards the stomach or bowel. [9][10][11]

Digestive tract bleeding can be slow and hidden. In adults with confirmed iron-deficiency anaemia and no clear cause, clinicians may investigate the stomach and bowel. This is especially important in men and people who have gone through menopause. The choice of tests depends on age, symptoms, risk and other likely explanations. Some people need a camera test or assessment for coeliac disease. [9][11]

At the visit, ask what the clinician thinks caused the deficiency, what would make them look further and when to repeat the blood test. A plan for replacing iron and a plan for investigating the cause can run in parallel. [9][11]

Meena takes a short record of her periods to the next appointment. She notes the change from her usual cycle and when the breathlessness began. Her clinician asks about symptoms between periods as well. If you can describe what changed and when, it helps your clinician decide which questions or tests to start with. [9][10]

07

What does recovery look like?

If iron deficiency is confirmed, a clinician may suggest iron tablets. Some people need iron through a vein, for example if tablets aren’t tolerated, aren’t absorbed well or don’t work adequately. Follow-up blood tests help check that haemoglobin is rising. It can take longer to refill iron stores, so ask how long the treatment is expected to continue. [5][9][16]

Food can support recovery. Beans, lentils and other pulses provide iron, as do meat and fish. Vitamin C from foods such as guava or citrus can help you absorb iron from plant foods. When anaemia is established, food changes alone may be insufficient. The clinician can advise on replacement and any tests for ongoing loss. [1][5][9]

Meena and her clinician agree to check her response and review the bleeding. If she is still breathless after her haemoglobin improves, she’ll bring that up too. It helps to track both the lab results and what she can comfortably do. [4][9]

08

When should I get help sooner?

Book a review if tiredness and breathlessness are new, persistent or affecting ordinary activity. Tell your clinician about heavy periods, bleeding, changes in bowel habits or numbness and balance problems. If you’re pregnant or recently gave birth, report new symptoms promptly to your maternity team. [4][9][10][12][17]

Call your local emergency number for severe difficulty breathing, new chest pressure or pain, or collapse with failure to recover quickly. These symptoms have several possible causes and need urgent assessment. [4][18]

Get emergency medical help for vomiting blood, passing black, tarry stools, or heavy bleeding with faintness, clammy skin or severe weakness. If you’re pregnant or recently gave birth, heavy bleeding, chest pain or significant difficulty breathing needs immediate medical care. [6][17]

Meena still remembers the landing where she stopped. At her follow-up she can describe what the stairs feel like now, and her clinician can compare that with the blood tests.

Ask what caused the anaemia, what treatment fits the cause and when to check that you’re recovering. [2][5][9]

The fridge-door cheat sheet

Why are the stairs harder?

Low haemoglobin means the blood carries less oxygen. Other conditions can also cause breathlessness. [1][3][4]

What should I ask about my blood test?

Ask what caused the anaemia, how it will be treated, and when to recheck your blood count. [8][9]

Which symptoms need emergency help?

Call emergency services for severe breathing trouble, new chest pain, collapse without quick recovery, or signs of serious bleeding. [4][6][18]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Use this article to discuss blood count results, symptoms and care with your clinician. Ask them before changing any medicine. For severe symptoms, call your local emergency number immediately.
