TYPE 2 DIABETES

Why Is My Sugar High Without Sweets?

What a chapati, your liver and insulin can tell you about a high blood glucose reading.

FOOD AND GLUCOSETHE LIVER AND INSULINWHAT THE TESTS SAY

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

“But I haven’t eaten anything sweet”

Ravi has come for a routine check before work. He skipped breakfast for the blood test and has been taking his tea without sugar for months. When the clinician says his fasting glucose is 132 mg/dL, he thinks back to yesterday’s dinner. Two chapatis, dal and vegetables. “There wasn’t even a dessert,” he says.

His clinician explains that glucose is the sugar circulating in the blood. Your body gets it from food and can also release it from the liver between meals. How much stays in the blood depends partly on insulin, a hormone that helps regulate glucose. The test gives a clue about how those systems are working together. [1][2]

Ravi feels well, which is common in the early stages of type 2 diabetes. His clinician arranges another lab test to confirm the finding. One fasting result can start an important conversation, and the next test can help decide what to do. [3][4]

As Ravi leaves for work, he has a better question for his next visit. “If I was fasting, where did the sugar come from?”

Prof. BoltIt could measure the glucose his body was already carrying.
02

What happens to a chapati after I eat it?

Picture a piece of chapati on Ravi’s plate. Much of its carbohydrate comes as starch, which is made of glucose units joined together. As the starch is digested, glucose is released and absorbed into the bloodstream. Rice, idli and potatoes also contain starch. Sweets and sugary drinks can deliver carbohydrate too, even though they taste quite different. [2][5]

After a meal, the pancreas usually releases insulin. The signal helps muscle and other tissues take up glucose from the blood. They can use it for energy or store it for later. Insulin also tells the liver to slow its own supply of glucose when food is coming in. [1][2]

Think about a meal as a delivery. Digestion brings glucose into the blood, and insulin helps direct where it goes. The amount and type of carbohydrate and the other foods eaten with it can change how quickly glucose rises. A helping of rice alongside vegetables and protein can produce a different response from rice eaten on its own. [2][5]

03

Why can glucose rise before breakfast?

Go back to the morning of Ravi’s test. His last meal was the night before. During the gap between meals, the body still needs a supply of energy. The liver helps by releasing stored glucose and making fresh glucose. That steady supply is one reason there can be glucose in the blood before the first bite of the day. [2][6]

In the early morning, hormones can signal the liver to release more glucose as the body prepares to wake. In some people with diabetes, that rise is more visible. It’s often called the dawn phenomenon. Other factors can affect morning readings too, including changes in insulin levels overnight. A clinician can use the timing and pattern of readings to work out what’s happening. [6]

Think of the liver as a kitchen that operates overnight. Even with the dining table clear, glucose can still be served. When insulin’s signal is weaker, the liver may release more than the body needs. That helps explain why fasting glucose can stay high even when someone has eaten no sweets. [2][6]

ZappySo there’s an overnight kitchen?
Prof. BoltYes. The liver keeps a fuel supply going between meals.
04

What does “insulin resistance” mean?

Imagine giving a familiar instruction and having to repeat it louder to get the same response. With insulin resistance, tissues such as muscle, fat and liver respond less well to insulin. Muscle can take up less glucose, and the liver can keep releasing it when insulin would usually tell it to slow down. [2]

Early on, the pancreas may make extra insulin to keep glucose in range. Over time, it may be unable to keep up with what the body needs. Glucose then rises more often or stays high for longer. That combination of insulin resistance and insufficient insulin for the body’s needs is typical of type 2 diabetes. [1][2]

Family history, age, a history of diabetes in pregnancy and physical inactivity are among the factors that influence risk. People can have several of these at once. Type 1 diabetes is a different process: the immune system attacks the cells that make insulin. It can start in adults as well as children. A clinician may need extra tests to work out which kind of diabetes someone has. [1][3][4][7]

THE DEEP DIVEHow to read the tests, what you can do and when to get help
05

Which test tells me what’s going on?

A fasting lab glucose shows the level after at least eight hours without food or caloric drinks. An HbA1c, often shortened to A1c, measures how much glucose has attached to haemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. It reflects average glucose over roughly three months and can usually be taken without fasting. One is a snapshot. The other summarises a longer stretch of time. [4][8]

For most nonpregnant adults, a fasting lab glucose of 126 mg/dL or higher, or a lab A1c of 6.5% or higher, is in the diabetes range. If high glucose is not already clear from the clinical picture, a second abnormal result is needed. In someone with classic symptoms such as marked thirst, frequent urination or unintentional weight loss, a random lab glucose of at least 200 mg/dL can establish the diagnosis. [4][9]

Tell your clinician about anaemia, known haemoglobin variants or a recent blood transfusion. These can affect how an A1c result is interpreted. Home meter readings are useful for tracking patterns, and lab testing is used to establish the diagnosis. Pregnancy has separate testing pathways. [4][8][9]

Prof. BoltMore like a summary of the album. It can miss the individual highs and lows.
06

If I feel fine, why does the result matter?

Ravi’s repeat test is also in the diabetes range. He hasn’t noticed extra thirst, more trips to the toilet or blurred vision. Type 2 diabetes can develop gradually, and some people have few or no symptoms for years. Blood tests can show a change long before a person notices one. [1][3]

Over time, high glucose can damage small blood vessels and nerves. It can affect the retina, the light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye, and the kidneys’ ability to filter blood. Nerve damage can make a sore on the foot harder to feel. Diabetes also raises the risk of heart disease and stroke, so your care team may talk about blood pressure and cholesterol alongside glucose. [10][11]

This is where follow-up can make a difference. Eye checks can pick up damage before vision changes. Blood and urine tests can help assess kidney health, and foot checks can spot problems that are easy to miss. Ask when these checks are due and how you’ll review the results. [10][11][12]

07

What can I change at my next meal?

At lunch, Ravi tries a version of the plate method. He fills half his thali with non-starchy vegetables such as beans and cabbage. He uses about a quarter for a protein food such as fish or tofu, and about a quarter for a starch such as chapati or rice. Dal can contribute both carbohydrate and protein. A dietitian can help you adapt the idea to a meal you already enjoy. [5][13]

Regular movement helps muscles use glucose. A walk you can fit around work is one possible starting point. Ask what activity fits your health and treatment. If you use insulin or other medicines that can cause low glucose, ask how to plan activity and recognise a low. [13][14]

Some people with type 2 diabetes need medicine along with food and activity changes. The plan depends on glucose levels and other factors such as heart or kidney health. For people with prediabetes, there is also evidence for prevention. In a US trial of high-risk adults, an intensive lifestyle programme reduced new diagnoses of type 2 diabetes by 58% relative to placebo over an average of 2.8 years. The figure describes the trial groups. [11][15][16]

08

When should I get urgent help?

Glucose that keeps running high is a reason to contact your care team, especially during an illness. If you already have diabetes, ask for a written sick-day plan that says when to check glucose or ketones and who to call. Ketones are chemicals made when the body breaks down fat for fuel. High ketones can signal a dangerous problem called diabetic ketoacidosis, or DKA. [17][18]

Get emergency help now if you have signs of a serious glucose emergency, such as repeated vomiting with inability to keep fluids down, fast or difficult breathing, fruity-smelling breath, severe confusion or unusual drowsiness. DKA can be the first sign of diabetes. Severe dehydration and confusion can also occur in a different high-glucose emergency. Call your local emergency number for severe symptoms. [17][18]

If someone using diabetes medicine becomes unconscious, has a seizure or cannot safely swallow, call emergency services immediately. Severe low glucose can cause these symptoms. Give nothing by mouth to someone who cannot swallow safely. [14][18]

Ravi still takes his tea without sugar. At his next appointment, he’s more interested in what the tests show over time and which changes he can keep up.

If a glucose reading surprises you, ask which test it came from, whether it needs confirmation and what your next step is. [4][11]

The fridge-door cheat sheet

Where does the glucose come from?

Starchy foods break down into glucose. Your liver can also release glucose between meals. [2][5][6]

What if a test comes back high?

Ask what the test measured, whether it needs confirmation and when to follow up. [4][9]

What symptoms need quick action?

Get emergency help for serious symptoms such as persistent vomiting with inability to drink, trouble breathing or severe confusion. [17][18]

Important patient-education notice
