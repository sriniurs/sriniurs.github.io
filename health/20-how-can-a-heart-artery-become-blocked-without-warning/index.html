CORONARY ARTERY DISEASE

How Can a Heart Artery Become Blocked Without Any Warning?

How plaque can build quietly, why a clot can block blood flow suddenly and when to call for help.

PLAQUE BUILD-UPSUDDEN BLOCKAGEWHEN TO CALL FOR HELP

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The morning walk that ended with a call for help

Mohan usually walks to the market before breakfast. One morning, pressure builds in the centre of his chest and spreads to his arm. He stops, but the pressure stays. He feels sweaty and sick. His daughter calls for emergency help. Later, after the hospital team finds a blockage in a heart artery, Mohan asks how it could have happened when he felt well the day before.

Coronary artery disease can build up quietly over years. A heart attack can start suddenly if the surface of a fatty deposit, called plaque, breaks or wears away and a clot forms on it. The clot can reduce or cut off blood flow to part of the heart muscle within a short time. The long build-up and the sudden blockage are two parts of the same story. [1][2][3]

Some people have warning symptoms before a heart attack. For others, the attack is the first symptom they recognise. The absence of chest pain in the past doesn’t tell you how much plaque is present. [1][3][4]

02

Where does plaque begin?

Your heart pumps blood to the body, and the heart muscle needs its own blood supply. The coronary arteries bring it oxygen. Coronary artery disease often starts when cholesterol-carrying particles, including LDL, enter and become trapped in the artery wall. This can trigger inflammation. Over time, cholesterol, cells and other material collect in a plaque. [1][2][5]

Plaque grows within the wall. Early on, the artery may expand outward, so there can be plaque even while the passage for blood still looks fairly open. As disease progresses, some areas narrow enough to limit the extra blood the heart needs during activity. [2][5][6]

Think of the artery as a living tube, with a wall that can change shape. This helps explain why feeling fine on a usual walk doesn’t prove the artery wall is clear. [1][5]

03

How can blood flow change so quickly?

Many plaques have a surface layer over the material inside. If that layer ruptures, or if the surface erodes, the blood can react as it would to an injury. Platelets gather and a clot can form. The clot may suddenly narrow or block the artery, even where the narrowing hadn’t caused symptoms before. [2][3][5]

When the heart muscle gets too little oxygen, it is called ischaemia. If blood flow remains inadequate long enough that heart muscle is injured and dies, it is a heart attack, also called a myocardial infarction. There are other ways a heart attack can occur. Plaque disruption with a clot is a common one. [3][4]

Mohan asks if the hospital could have guessed the exact day it would happen. His team explains that tests can assess risk or look for disease, while predicting exactly which plaque will cause an event at a particular time is much harder. [5][6][7]

Prof. BoltYes. That’s why new warning symptoms need a quick response.
04

Would I always feel chest pain first?

Some people notice pressure, heaviness or discomfort mainly with exertion, such as when climbing stairs. If it settles with rest and follows a predictable pattern, clinicians may consider stable angina. New symptoms, symptoms at rest, or a familiar pattern that suddenly gets worse can need emergency assessment. [1][3][7]

A heart attack can feel like pressure, squeezing, fullness or pain in the chest. Discomfort can spread to the arm, jaw, back or upper abdomen. Shortness of breath, nausea, sweating or light-headedness can also occur. Some people have little or no chest discomfort. A symptom that feels like indigestion can still be heart-related. [4][7][8]

Tell a clinician about changes in what you can do, including getting unusually breathless on a route you used to manage. For possible heart attack symptoms, follow the emergency guidance below. [1][7][8]

05

What can the tests actually tell me?

In an emergency, the team records the heart’s electrical activity with an ECG and checks blood for troponin, a marker of heart muscle injury. Depending on how long symptoms have been present and what the first results show, tests may be repeated. An early normal result can be only one part of the emergency assessment. [3][7][9]

When symptoms are stable, clinicians choose tests according to the history and likelihood of disease. A CT coronary angiogram can show the arteries and look for plaque and narrowing. A stress test can help assess whether part of the heart is getting too little blood during exertion or when a medicine simulates the effect. The tests answer different questions. [6][7][10]

If you feel well, a clinician may start by reviewing factors such as blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, tobacco use and family history. In some cases, a coronary calcium scan helps guide a prevention decision. It measures calcified plaque and can miss plaque that hasn’t calcified. Ask which decision a proposed test would help with. [6][11]

The same test can be useful in one situation and unnecessary in another. If you’re being assessed for chest discomfort today, the immediate question is whether there is a dangerous cause. At a routine visit when you feel well, the question may be what you can do to lower your future risk. Tell the clinician which concern brought you in. [6][7][11]

06

Can I do anything before symptoms appear?

High LDL cholesterol, high blood pressure, diabetes and tobacco use can contribute to coronary disease. Age and a family history of early heart disease also affect risk. A clinician can look at these factors together and help decide which changes or treatments would make the biggest difference for you. [1][6][11]

Being active in a way that fits your health, avoiding tobacco, and eating more vegetables, pulses and whole grains are practical parts of prevention. If symptoms are new or you’re recovering from a heart attack, ask when and how to increase activity. Cardiac rehabilitation can help eligible people regain confidence and work on risk factors after a heart event. [6][12]

Some people benefit from a cholesterol-lowering medicine such as a statin. Aspirin isn’t suitable for everyone because of bleeding risk. Ask before starting it for prevention. If you already have a prescription for it, discuss changes with the prescribing team. [6][11][13]

At the first follow-up, Mohan brings his discharge summary and medicine list. He asks which medicines he needs to keep taking, what symptoms or side effects to report and when to have his blood pressure and cholesterol reviewed. This gives him a set of decisions to make with the team over time, even when he feels back to normal. [6][12][13]

07

Does every narrowing need a stent?

During a heart attack, urgently restoring blood flow can protect heart muscle. A stent is a tiny support that helps hold an artery open after treatment. In long-term stable disease, the choice depends on symptoms, the location and extent of disease, the heart’s pumping function and your overall health. Medicines, a stent or bypass surgery may be considered in different situations. [3][6][10]

The ISCHEMIA trial compared an initial invasive strategy with an initial medicine-based strategy in selected people with stable coronary disease. It found no clear reduction in the main combined outcome over a median of about three years. A related analysis found greater symptom and quality-of-life improvements in some people with frequent angina. These findings concern stable disease. A suspected heart attack needs emergency assessment. [3][14][15]

Mohan asks what each part of his treatment is for. His team explains which steps address a particular blockage and which reduce his overall risk over time. [3][6]

08

When should I call for emergency help?

If symptoms are new, prolonged or happening at rest, take them seriously. A heart attack can start gradually with mild discomfort or begin suddenly. How severe the pain feels is not a reliable guide to whether you need help. [3][8]

Call your local emergency number immediately for possible heart attack symptoms. These include new chest pressure or discomfort that lasts more than a few minutes or comes and goes, especially with shortness of breath, a cold sweat, nausea or discomfort in the arm, jaw or back. Some people have shortness of breath or other warning signs without chest pain. [3][8]

Use emergency medical transport when available. Don’t drive yourself. Follow the dispatcher’s instructions. If someone collapses, is unresponsive and isn’t breathing normally, call emergency services and follow their instructions for CPR. [3][8][16]

At follow-up, Mohan makes a list of questions about his medicines, activity and risk factors. His daughter keeps the emergency number easy to find. The family now has a plan for ordinary days and knows what to do if symptoms return. [6][8][12]

Know your heart risk and get changes in exertion checked. If you suspect a heart attack, call emergency services immediately. [6][7][8]

How can it happen suddenly?

Plaque can build for years. A clot can form quickly if its surface is disrupted. [1][2][3]

What can I do before there are symptoms?

Ask about your blood pressure, LDL cholesterol, blood sugar and family history, and what changes fit your risk. [6][11]

When should I call for help?

Call emergency services immediately for possible heart attack symptoms. Use emergency transport when available. [3][8]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Use this article to discuss heart symptoms, risk, tests and follow-up with your clinician. Ask them before changing any medicine. For severe symptoms, call your local emergency number immediately.
