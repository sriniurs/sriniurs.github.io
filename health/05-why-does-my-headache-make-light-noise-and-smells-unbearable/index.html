MIGRAINE

Why Does My Headache Make Light, Noise and Even Smells Unbearable?

Why everyday sensations can feel overwhelming during a migraine, and when a headache needs urgent help.

SENSORY SENSITIVITYUNDERSTANDING AURAWHEN TO GET HELP

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

Suddenly, the whole kitchen felt too loud

Leela is slicing an onion when the kitchen light starts to hurt. She turns it off. In the next room, a television she barely noticed ten minutes ago sounds painfully loud. The smell of onions turns her stomach. Soon there’s a throbbing pain near her temple. Her husband asks whether he should bring dinner. She asks him to turn down the television first.

At her appointment, her clinician asks about all three senses as well as the pain. That combination can be a clue to migraine, a neurological condition that causes recurring attacks. During an attack, ordinary light, sound or smells can become uncomfortable or worsen how you feel. Nausea may accompany the pain. The symptoms can interfere with a meal, a commute or an entire day at work. [1][2]

Leela has noticed a similar pattern on several occasions. She starts putting the whole episode into words, including when the room first felt too bright. The details help her clinician assess whether the episodes fit migraine and whether something else needs checking. [2][3]

02

Why can light, sound and smells feel unbearable?

During migraine, the nervous system can become more sensitive to signals it usually handles comfortably. Clinicians call sensitivity to light photophobia, sensitivity to sound phonophobia, and aversion or sensitivity to smells osmophobia. A fluorescent light, a mixer grinder or a familiar perfume can feel overwhelming during an attack. These symptoms vary from person to person and can begin before the pain. [1][2][4]

Researchers have looked at how light might make migraine pain worse. One study combined observations in people with migraine and vision loss with experiments in rats. In the animal work, researchers found brain cells that received input linked to both light and pain from tissues around the brain. That provides one possible route for light to amplify headache. Sound and smell sensitivity have their own open questions. [4]

Think of several input channels becoming harder to tolerate at once. Leela’s wish to close the curtains and sit somewhere quiet is part of the illness she’s describing. It gives the clinician information that the pain score alone would miss. [1][2][3]

03

How is migraine different from a tension-type headache?

Clinicians look for a pattern across attacks. Migraine pain often throbs, can be moderate to severe, and may worsen when you climb stairs or bend down. It can be on one side or both. Nausea or vomiting and sensitivity to light and sound help distinguish it. In adults, the headache phase commonly lasts four hours to three days if untreated or if treatment doesn’t work. [2][3][5]

Tension-type headache often feels like pressure or tightening on both sides. Routine movement usually doesn’t make the pain worse, and vomiting is uncharacteristic. A person can experience more than one kind of headache, so describe what happens on each type of day. [3][6]

Some attacks begin with tiredness, yawning or food cravings before the headache. Some people feel drained after the pain has passed. A diary can capture the change from the first clue through recovery, along with the days you had to stop work or cancel plans. [1][3][7]

04

What is aura, and does everyone get it?

Aura is a temporary change in nervous system function that happens with some migraine attacks. A typical visual aura can look like a shimmering zigzag or a blind patch that gradually spreads. Some people get pins and needles that move across a hand or face, or have trouble finding words. A typical individual aura symptom usually lasts five to sixty minutes. Aura can occur before or during the headache, or sometimes without headache. [3][8]

Many people have migraine attacks without aura, and some people experience both kinds. Tiredness or a food craving before a headache belongs to a different part of the attack, often called the prodrome. Leela’s light sensitivity is a clue to migraine on its own. A separate visual change such as an expanding zigzag would be worth describing in detail. [1][5][7][8]

New weakness, sudden trouble speaking or sudden vision loss can be signs of stroke. Call emergency services for those symptoms, even if you have had migraine before or the symptoms improve. [3][9]

05

Was it the perfume, or had the attack already begun?

Leela suspects the onion. On another day, she suspects a colleague’s perfume. Later she remembers that she had been yawning repeatedly and had already dimmed her screen before she smelled either. That sequence raises a question researchers have asked too. Sometimes something that feels like a trigger may become noticeable because an attack is already beginning. [7][10]

In one study, researchers found overlap between things people reported as triggers and their early symptoms. For example, people who described light as a trigger were more likely to report light sensitivity before the pain. That association cannot tell you what started a particular attack. It does give a good reason to write down the order of events. [10]

Sleep changes, skipped meals, stress and the menstrual cycle can be relevant for some people. Record usual habits alongside possible triggers and look for patterns across several attacks. A diary can help you and your clinician decide which change is worth trying first. [1][3][11]

06

What will the clinician check?

There is usually no single test that confirms migraine. The clinician asks how often attacks occur, how long they last and what happens before, during and after them. They may check vision, strength, sensation and reflexes. Bring a list of the medicines you use, including over-the-counter painkillers, and count how many days a month you take them. [1][3][12]

Brain imaging is usually unnecessary when attacks fit a typical migraine pattern, the neurological exam is normal and there are no warning signs. A sudden onset, a substantial change in pattern or an abnormal examination can change that decision. Tell the clinician about those changes even if a previous headache was diagnosed as migraine. [3][12]

The clinic asks Leela to track headache days, migraine symptoms and medicine days separately. She also records how often she leaves work early. Treatment decisions can take account of both attack frequency and how much the attacks disrupt daily life. [3][13]

If you can, bring a concrete example. Leela describes one Tuesday when she had to turn off her screen, leave a meeting and lie down. She notes when the pain started, what she took and when she could get back to her usual activities. A description like that makes it easier to discuss whether your current plan is doing enough. [3]

07

What can help during an attack, and between attacks?

Leela and her clinician agree on a plan for the next attack. Resting somewhere dark and quiet may help with sensory discomfort. Depending on your health history, treatment can include a suitable pain reliever, a migraine-specific medicine such as a triptan, or something to help with nausea. Ask what to take, when to take it and what to do if vomiting makes tablets difficult. [2][3]

If attacks are frequent or disruptive, ask about preventive treatment. Options can include daily tablets and medicines targeting CGRP, a signalling molecule involved in migraine. The choice depends on your health, other medicines, pregnancy plans and which options are available where you live. A diary can help you judge whether a prevention plan is cutting down the number or impact of attacks. [3][13][14]

Regular sleep, meals and hydration are practical places to start. Using acute medicines too often can itself make headaches more frequent. Ask your clinician about the limits for the medicines you use and how to get help if you’re already relying on them frequently. [2][3][11][15]

08

When does a headache need urgent help?

Leela knows what her usual attacks feel like. Her care plan also says what would change the response. A new headache can need urgent assessment even in someone who has had migraine for years. [2][3]

Call your local emergency number for a sudden severe headache that reaches full intensity within minutes, or a headache with sudden weakness, trouble speaking, sudden vision loss, a seizure, confusion, or fever with a stiff neck. Stroke symptoms need emergency help even if they pass. [2][3][9]

Seek urgent medical assessment for new or unusual aura, or aura lasting longer than an hour. Sudden stroke-like symptoms need emergency help immediately. During pregnancy or after delivery, get immediate medical care for a severe, persistent or worsening headache, especially with vision changes. [2][9][16][17]

Leela keeps her diary beside the TV remote. Her next appointment can cover what she’s learning from the pattern and whether the plan is helping.

Track the full attack, including light, sound, smell and nausea. Bring the pattern and medicine days to your review. Act immediately on emergency warning signs. [1][3][9]

KEEP

The fridge-door cheat sheet

What belongs in the diary?

Record when symptoms begin, light or sound sensitivity, nausea, any aura, headache days and medicine days. [1][3][8]

When should I ask about prevention?

Talk to your clinician when attacks recur often or disrupt work, sleep or daily life. [3][13]

When is it an emergency?

Call emergency services for a sudden severe headache or sudden weakness, speech trouble or vision loss. [2][3][9]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Use this article to discuss headache patterns, diagnosis and treatment with your clinician. Ask them before changing any medicine. For severe symptoms, call your local emergency number immediately.
