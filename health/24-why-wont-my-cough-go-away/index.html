PERSISTENT COUGH

Why Won’t My Cough Go Away?

Why a cough can linger after a cold, what clinicians check and when to seek help.

AFTER A COLDFINDING THE CAUSEWHEN TO GET HELP

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The cold went away. The cough stayed.

Sameer catches a cold and spends a few days with a runny nose and sore throat. Those symptoms clear. The cough stays. It interrupts calls at work and wakes him at night. When he gets to the clinic, he’s lost track of how many weeks have passed. He asks whether he needs another bottle of cough syrup or a closer look.

A cough can linger after a respiratory infection. In adults, clinicians often call a cough chronic when it has lasted more than eight weeks. That term describes duration. You may need an assessment earlier, depending on symptoms, exposures and where you live. [1][2][3][4]

The clinician asks Sameer whether the cough is improving, whether it brings up mucus and whether there is fever, blood or breathing difficulty. Those details matter more than the sound of a cough heard once in the clinic. [1][2][5]

02

Why can a cough outlast an infection?

Coughing is a protective reflex. It helps clear mucus and material that irritates the airways. During and after an infection, the airways can stay irritated and the reflex can remain sensitive. A deep breath, talking or cold air may set it off. For some people, a cough after infection improves gradually over several weeks. [1][3][6]

Imagine a smoke alarm that reacts to a little steam after it has been set off. A sensitive cough reflex can make everyday triggers feel like a tickle you have to clear. That can be one part of the explanation, especially when the cough is dry. A persistent cough with lots of phlegm may need a different assessment. [1][6]

Sameer thinks his cough is improving, then remembers that he still wakes several nights a week. His clinician asks him to note whether the bouts are becoming less frequent or less intense. The direction of change can help decide how soon to review it. [1][2][5]

03

What else can keep it going?

Asthma can cause cough, particularly at night or with exercise or cold air. Wheezing, chest tightness or breathlessness are useful clues to mention. Nasal problems can occur with a blocked or runny nose, sneezing or a feeling of mucus in the throat. Heartburn or sour liquid coming up may suggest reflux, though a cough alone doesn’t establish that reflux is the cause. [1][6][7]

Smoke, dust, fumes and some medicines can also matter. A type of blood pressure medicine called an ACE inhibitor can cause a dry cough. Bring your medicine list and tell the clinician when you started each medicine. Ask the prescriber before changing anything. [1][6][8]

Tell the clinician if you bring up phlegm most days, get repeated chest infections or have symptoms around certain work exposures. There may be several contributors. Sameer mentions that cleaning spray sometimes starts a coughing spell and that his symptoms are different after a brisk walk. [1][6][7]

04

When should tuberculosis be considered?

Tuberculosis, or TB, is an infection that can affect the lungs. Possible symptoms include a lasting cough, fever, night sweats, unexplained weight loss and sometimes blood in the phlegm. Tell the clinician if you’ve spent time with someone with TB. They can decide what testing is appropriate even if you don’t have every symptom. [4][9]

India’s Central Tuberculosis Division lists a persistent cough for two weeks or more as a symptom that should prompt TB evaluation. If you’re in India and have reached that point, contact a clinician or health centre. The eight-week label for chronic cough is for classifying symptoms, and there is no reason to wait that long to get checked for TB. [1][4][9]

A clinician may arrange a chest X-ray and tests on sputum, the phlegm you cough up. The tests needed depend on the situation. If TB is suspected, follow the care team’s advice about testing and steps to reduce exposure to others. [4][9]

05

What will the clinician ask and test?

Bring an approximate start date, whether the cough is dry or brings up phlegm, and what tends to set it off. Mention any shortness of breath, chest pain, fever, weight loss, TB exposure or blood. Tell the clinician about smoking, vaping and anything you breathe in regularly at work or home. [1][5][9]

For chronic cough, a chest X-ray and a breathing test called spirometry are common starting points. Spirometry measures how much and how fast you can breathe out. A clinician may request a phlegm sample, or other tests if the history points towards an infection or a particular airway problem. [1][5][6]

A reassuring X-ray helps with some questions, but a persistent cough may still need follow-up. The clinician can decide whether to investigate further or try a treatment aimed at a specific cause. Ask what the test checked and what would change the next step. [1][6]

Sameer remembers being asked whether he coughed on holidays as well as at work. The answer helps put his exposure to cleaning sprays in context. If your cough follows a pattern, such as recurring after a particular task or waking you soon after lying down, bring a few examples to the visit. [1][5][6]

06

Why doesn’t every cough need an antibiotic?

Many short coughs are caused by viruses. Antibiotics do not treat viruses and generally offer little benefit for uncomplicated acute cough or bronchitis in people who aren’t very unwell or at higher risk of complications. A clinician may consider them when there is evidence of a bacterial infection or a specific reason to treat. [2][10]

The useful treatment depends on the cause. An inhaler can be helpful when asthma or certain kinds of airway inflammation are involved. If the main clues point to a nasal condition, treating that may be more useful. Acid-suppressing medicines may help some people with reflux symptoms, but they’re generally not used just because someone coughs. [1][6][7]

Sameer asks whether the next treatment is meant to ease symptoms while he recovers or address a specific cause. The clinician explains how long to try it and how to tell if it’s helping. [1][2][6]

Bring bottles or photos of any products you’ve tried. Combination cold remedies can have overlapping ingredients, and some may be unsuitable with your regular medicines. Ask a pharmacist or clinician what is safe for you and whether there is a reason to continue it. [2][10]

07

What if the cough stays after the usual checks?

Some people continue to cough despite assessment and treatment of likely causes. They may have a very sensitive cough reflex. A clinician can review earlier tests, reconsider exposures or other conditions, and decide whether referral is needed. The goal is to understand what has already been checked and what could still change care. [1][6]

Specialist cough-control therapy can help selected people with a persistent cough. It may include breathing techniques and ways to handle the urge to cough. In a small randomised trial, adults with chronic cough that persisted despite medical treatment had better symptom scores with a speech pathology programme than with a comparison programme. The result applies to the group studied. [6][11]

Sameer keeps a short record of waking, coughing during conversations and what he has tried. At follow-up, that gives the clinician a way to compare the cough with how it behaved at the first visit. [1][6]

He asks which improvement would count first. Fewer nights waking up and being able to finish a conversation without stopping are changes he can describe. If he develops new symptoms in the meantime, he knows to contact the clinic sooner. [1][5]

08

When should I get help sooner?

Seek assessment if the cough is lingering, worsening, or repeatedly disturbing sleep or activity. Mention fever, drenching night sweats, unexplained weight loss or known TB exposure. In India, ask about TB evaluation for a cough that lasts two weeks or more. [1][4][5][9]

Get urgent same-day care if you cough up even a few spots or streaks of blood, or if your breathing is getting worse. Tell the clinician how much blood there was and whether you also have fever, chest pain or breathlessness. [5][12][13]

Call your local emergency number for severe breathing difficulty, new chest pain with shortness of breath, confusion or collapse. Get emergency help if you cough up more than a few spots or streaks of blood, especially with breathing difficulty or chest pain. [12][13][14]

Sameer leaves with a plan that includes what to watch for and when to return. The cough has a clearer timeline now, and so does his follow-up.

Track duration, phlegm, triggers and warning signs. Get a lingering cough assessed rather than waiting for it to fit a label. [1][4][5]

The fridge-door cheat sheet

Can a cough linger after a cold?

Yes. A cough can outlast an infection. Mention its duration, whether it is improving and what else has changed. [1][2][3]

When should I ask about TB?

In India, contact a clinician or health centre if your cough lasts two weeks or more. Mention known exposure and any weight loss or night sweats. [4][9]

When should I get emergency help?

Call emergency services for severe breathing trouble or new chest pain with breathlessness. Get emergency help for more than a few streaks of coughed-up blood. [12][13]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Use this article to discuss cough symptoms, testing and treatment with your clinician. Ask them before changing any medicine. For severe symptoms, call your local emergency number immediately.
