GALLSTONES

Why Does My Stomach Hurt After a Heavy or Oily Meal?

Why a meal can bring on gallstone pain, what ultrasound can show and when to get urgent help.

THE GALLBLADDERWHY PAIN FOLLOWS MEALSWHEN TO GET HELP

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The pain that stayed after dinner

Anjali enjoys a family dinner and helps clear the table. An hour later, a strong ache settles under her right ribs. She feels sick, and the discomfort reaches towards her right shoulder blade. It lasts long enough for her to wonder whether to ask for help, then eases. When it happens again, she tells her clinician about both episodes.

Pain from the gallbladder is one possibility. After a meal, the gallbladder squeezes to release bile. If a stone temporarily blocks its outlet, the pressure can cause a steady ache in the upper abdomen. Doctors call this biliary colic. It can follow a heavy meal and may last from half an hour to several hours. [1][2][3]

People often say “stomach pain” for anything in the upper abdomen. The clinician asks Anjali to point to where it starts and describe how long it lasts. Indigestion, reflux and other conditions can overlap with parts of the story, so the meal is one clue among several. [2][3][4]

02

What is the gallbladder squeezing out?

Your liver makes bile, a digestive fluid. The gallbladder, a small pouch tucked under the liver, stores and concentrates some of it between meals. When you eat, signals from the gut tell the gallbladder to contract. Bile travels along small tubes called bile ducts and enters the first part of the small intestine, where it helps with fat digestion. [1][5]

Think of the gallbladder as a reservoir beside a road. Its own small outlet, the cystic duct, joins the route carrying bile from the liver. A stone can block the outlet of the reservoir or move farther along into the main route, called the common bile duct. Where it gets stuck matters. [3][5][6]

In Anjali’s case, the clinician suspects the outlet was blocked for a time. If a stone moves away from the opening, the pain can settle. If the blockage persists or a stone travels farther, the problem can become more serious. [1][3][6]

03

Where do gallstones come from?

Gallstones can form when substances in bile harden into crystals. Many stones contain cholesterol. Others are pigment stones, made mainly from compounds related to bilirubin, a substance formed as the body breaks down old red blood cells. Bile composition and how well the gallbladder empties can contribute to stone formation. [1][5][7]

Family history, age, pregnancy, obesity and rapid weight loss can increase the chance of gallstones. A person can have stones even if they rarely eat oily food. One particular dinner may set off symptoms by prompting the gallbladder to squeeze, while the stones formed long before that meal. [1][5][7]

Gallstones sometimes turn up during a scan for another reason. Many never cause symptoms or require treatment. When a scan finds a stone, the clinician asks whether it fits the pain pattern and whether the bile ducts or gallbladder show signs of a complication. [4][5][8]

04

What changes if a stone stays stuck?

Despite the word “colic,” gallstone pain often feels steady once it starts. It can sit under the right ribs or in the centre of the upper abdomen and spread to the back or shoulder. A temporary blockage can cause an attack that eventually eases. Persistent pain with fever or marked tenderness under the right ribs may mean the gallbladder is inflamed. This is called acute cholecystitis. [2][3][6]

A stone in the main bile duct can cause jaundice, which may show as yellowing in the whites of the eyes or skin, dark urine or pale stools. A blocked bile duct can also become infected. If a stone interferes with drainage from the pancreas, it can trigger pancreatitis, which can cause severe upper abdominal pain spreading to the back, often with vomiting. [2][3][6][9]

These problems need prompt assessment. A previous attack settling on its own doesn’t tell you how a new episode will behave. [2][3]

The location of the blockage helps explain the difference. The gallbladder’s own outlet can be blocked while bile from the liver still reaches the intestine. A blockage farther downstream, in the common bile duct, can interfere with drainage from the liver as well. That is one reason the clinician asks specifically about yellow eyes, dark urine and pale stools. [3][6]

THE DEEP DIVEWhat scans show and how treatment decisions are made
05

Can ultrasound show what caused my pain?

A clinician will usually ask about where it hurts, whether the pain spreads, how long it lasts and whether there has been fever or jaundice. Ultrasound can often find gallbladder stones and may show signs of inflammation. Blood tests can give clues about infection, a blockage in the bile ducts or inflammation of the pancreas. [4][10]

An ultrasound may miss a stone in the common bile duct. If blood tests or the size of the duct raise concern, the team may use an MRI-based scan called MRCP to look at the ducts. Another option in some situations is an ultrasound probe on a flexible tube, called endoscopic ultrasound. If a stone needs removing from the main bile duct, a specialist may use an endoscopic procedure called ERCP. [4][10][11]

Anjali’s clinician explains why the pain pattern matters even with a scan in hand. A person can have gallstones alongside reflux or another cause of abdominal pain. Ask how well the finding explains your symptoms. [3][4][12]

06

Does everyone with gallstones need surgery?

If gallstones are found by chance in an otherwise normal gallbladder and bile ducts, treatment is usually unnecessary. When stones cause repeated typical attacks, removal of the gallbladder is a common option. The procedure, typically done through small cuts, is called laparoscopic cholecystectomy. A complication can change how urgently treatment is needed. [4][8][13]

In the UK C-GALL randomised trial, adults with uncomplicated symptomatic gallstones assigned to conservative care had similar overall bodily pain scores over 18 months to those assigned to surgery. Some people allocated to conservative care later had surgery, and people with important complications such as known bile duct stones or previous pancreatitis were excluded. These findings support discussing options when the case is uncomplicated. [12]

Anjali wants to know how likely surgery is to help the pain she has, what waiting would involve and what should send her back to hospital. The answers depend on her pattern of attacks, test results and preferences. [4][12][14]

Clearing a stone from the main bile duct and removing the gallbladder are different steps. If testing shows a duct stone, the team may remove it with ERCP or clear the duct during surgery. Ask which problem each procedure addresses and whether you’re likely to need more than one visit. [4][8][11]

07

What can I do about meals while I wait?

If a particular meal repeatedly brings on pain, it may make sense to avoid it while waiting for assessment or treatment. Smaller meals or less of a food that reliably triggers symptoms may be easier to manage than a long list of foods to avoid. These changes can help manage symptoms for some people. They don’t remove a stone or treat a blocked duct. [4][14]

After gallbladder removal, the liver still makes bile. It flows into the intestine without first being stored in the gallbladder. Most people can return to a normal, balanced diet. Ask the surgical team about recovery, possible risks such as infection or bile duct injury, and which symptoms should prompt a call after surgery. [8][13][15]

Anjali writes down what she ate, where it hurt and how long it lasted. If the attacks recur, that record can help her describe the pattern clearly. She also keeps the emergency advice handy. [3][14]

08

When is the pain an emergency?

Arrange urgent medical assessment for a new episode of upper abdominal pain lasting more than half an hour, even if you suspect gallstones. Tell the clinician if the pain is different from earlier attacks, or if you’ve noticed dark urine or pale stools. [2][3]

Go to emergency care now for sudden severe abdominal pain, especially if it persists, or for upper abdominal pain with fever, shaking chills, yellowing of the eyes or skin, or pain spreading to the back with vomiting. These can be signs of an infected or blocked bile duct, an inflamed gallbladder or pancreatitis. [2][3][9]

Call your local emergency number if someone with possible infection becomes confused, collapses or is too unwell to travel safely. [16]

Anjali remembers the first dinner because that was when the pain began. At her next visit, she’s ready to talk about the timing, where it hurt and what the tests showed.

Tell your clinician where the pain starts, how long it lasts and whether there’s fever, vomiting or yellowing of the eyes. Seek emergency care for severe pain or warning symptoms. [2][3][4]

The fridge-door cheat sheet

Why can pain follow a meal?

The gallbladder squeezes to release bile. A stone can temporarily block its outlet. [1][2]

Do gallstones always need treatment?

Stones found by chance often need no treatment. Recurrent pain or complications can change the plan. [4][8][12]

When should I get emergency help?

Get emergency care for sudden severe pain or upper abdominal pain with fever, jaundice or pain spreading to the back with vomiting. [2][3][9]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Use this article to discuss abdominal symptoms, scan findings and treatment with your clinician. Ask them before changing any medicine. For severe symptoms, call your local emergency number immediately.
