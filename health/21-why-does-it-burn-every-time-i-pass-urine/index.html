URINARY TRACT INFECTION (UTI)

Why Does It Burn Every Time I Pass Urine?

How bacteria reach the bladder, why symptoms can come back and when to get urgent help.

BLADDER OR KIDNEY?WHEN IT KEEPS COMING BACKWHEN TO GET HELP

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The bathroom trip that stung

Deepa is getting ready for work when passing urine starts to sting. Twenty minutes later she feels she needs to go again, but only a little comes out. By lunchtime the urge is interrupting her meetings. At the clinic she asks whether the burning means she has a urinary tract infection, or UTI.

A bladder infection is one common explanation for burning and needing to pass urine often or urgently. It happens when bacteria reach the bladder and cause inflammation. Other conditions can cause burning too, so a clinician looks at the combination of symptoms and checks for signs that the infection might have reached a kidney. [1][2][3]

Deepa tells the clinician when the symptoms started and whether she has fever, pain in her side, vaginal irritation or discharge. Those details can change which tests are useful and how quickly she needs treatment. [2][4][5]

02

How do bacteria get into the bladder?

The kidneys make urine. Two tubes called ureters carry it to the bladder, where it is stored. The urethra is the separate tube that carries urine out. Bacteria that normally live near the bowel or on nearby skin can enter the urethra and travel up to the bladder. A bladder infection is also called cystitis. [1][3][6]

When the bladder lining is inflamed, it can feel as though you need to pass urine again even after a recent visit. Passing urine can sting. Some people feel discomfort low in the abdomen or notice blood in the urine. Cloudiness or a stronger smell can happen too, but smell alone has other possible explanations. [1][2][3]

Think of Deepa’s complaint as a combination of changes. The burning, urgency and frequent small trips matter more together than a single change in the smell of urine. [2][4]

03

Why are bladder infections more common in women?

In most women the urethra is shorter and its opening is closer to the anus. That gives bacteria a shorter route to the bladder. Sexual activity and some contraceptives, such as spermicides, can increase the chance of infection in some people. After menopause, changes related to lower oestrogen can also make repeated infections more likely. [2][3][5][7]

People of any sex can get a UTI. A catheter, a stone or a prostate problem that makes it hard to empty the bladder can change the risk. Pregnancy also affects how urinary symptoms and test results should be managed. Tell the clinician if any of these apply to you. [2][5][6]

Deepa asks whether she should mention a possible link with sex. Her clinician says the timing is useful, especially if the same pattern keeps recurring. It can guide a conversation about prevention and other possible causes of burning. [4][5][7]

04

What else can cause burning?

Burning can come from irritation of the skin around the opening, vaginal inflammation or an inflamed urethra. Some sexually transmitted infections can cause urethral symptoms. A stone can cause urinary discomfort or bleeding as well. The right test depends on the rest of the story. [2][4][5][6]

Tell the clinician whether you have itching, discharge, sores, pelvic pain or a new sexual partner. Also mention whether the sting feels on the outside when urine touches the skin, or whether it comes with an urgent need to empty the bladder. These observations are clues a clinician can use alongside any tests. [4][5]

If urine tests keep coming back negative or treatment hasn’t helped, go back for a review. Repeatedly treating presumed infection can delay finding another cause. Bring any earlier results and the names of medicines you took. [4][8][9]

THE DEEP DIVEWhat urine tests show and what changes the treatment plan
05

Do I need a urine test or a culture?

A clinician may be able to diagnose a straightforward bladder infection from a typical symptom pattern. In other situations, they may check a urine sample. A dipstick gives quick clues about signs of infection. A urine culture checks which bacteria grow and can help show which antibiotics are likely to work. Culture is particularly useful when symptoms recur, an infection is suspected in pregnancy or the kidneys may be involved. [2][4][5][8][10]

If your clinic asks for a sample, follow its instructions. The timing can matter, especially if you’ve already taken an antibiotic. Tell the clinician about recent medicines and any allergies or previous resistant infections. [4][8][10]

A culture result usually takes longer than a dipstick. Depending on how unwell you are, the clinician may begin treatment before the result is back and review it when the lab reports which antibiotics are likely to work. Ask who will check the result and how you’ll be told if the plan changes. [8][9][10]

Sometimes bacteria are found in urine in someone who feels well and has no urinary symptoms. Clinicians usually avoid treating that finding alone. Pregnancy and certain urological procedures are important exceptions. The sample needs to be interpreted in context. [4][5][11]

06

How would I know if the infection reached a kidney?

A bladder infection usually causes symptoms around urination and sometimes discomfort low in the abdomen. A kidney infection can cause fever, shaking chills, pain in the side or back under the ribs, nausea or vomiting. It can occur with burning or frequent urination, but those symptoms may be absent. Kidney infection needs prompt assessment. [2][3][10][12]

Clinicians choose treatment based on the likely site of infection, your symptoms, pregnancy status if relevant, allergies and local antibiotic resistance. A medicine suitable for the bladder may be unsuitable for infection in a kidney. If you’re given treatment, follow the instructions and ask what to do if you aren’t improving or symptoms worsen. [8][10][12]

Deepa asks how long to wait before checking back. Her clinician tells her what to expect and gives her specific signs that mean she should seek help sooner. Taking leftover antibiotics can complicate that assessment, so tell the team if you’ve already taken something. [4][8]

If you’ve started antibiotics and symptoms haven’t begun to improve within about two days, contact the treating team for reassessment. Get help sooner if they worsen, or if fever, side pain or vomiting develops. A clinician may review the culture, check for complications or consider whether another condition is causing the symptoms. [8][10]

07

What if the infections keep coming back?

Tell your clinician if you’ve had two UTIs in six months or three in a year. Bring the dates and any culture results. The plan may include checking for factors that make infections more likely or for other causes of similar symptoms. Prevention depends on the person and can include prescription options, such as vaginal oestrogen after menopause when appropriate. [2][7]

In one year-long randomised trial, premenopausal women with repeated bladder infections and low usual fluid intake who drank more water had an average of 1.7 infections, compared with 3.2 in the group that kept its usual intake. The study included a specific group. Ask what is appropriate for you, especially if you have a prescribed fluid limit. [7][13]

A clinician can discuss other preventive approaches if infections continue. The aim is to reduce future episodes and avoid unnecessary antibiotic use. Ask how your next episode should be assessed and how you’ll know whether a prevention plan is helping. [7][14]

For some people with recurrent infection, a prescription preventive medicine that is not an antibiotic may be an option. It has been compared with daily preventive antibiotics in a randomised study. It’s a decision for a prevention visit. Current infection symptoms still need their own assessment and treatment plan. [7][14]

08

When should I get help quickly?

Arrange a medical review for burning that persists, comes back or comes with blood in the urine. Seek urgent advice if you are pregnant or if symptoms worsen despite treatment. Visible blood deserves assessment, including if it continues after an infection is treated. [2][5][8][15]

Seek urgent same-day care for urinary symptoms with fever or shaking chills, pain in the side or back under the ribs, or vomiting. Get emergency help if you can’t keep fluids down and are becoming very unwell. These symptoms can signal a kidney infection or another problem needing prompt treatment. [2][10][12]

Call your local emergency number if someone with a possible infection becomes confused, is difficult to wake, has severe breathing difficulty or collapses. These can be signs of sepsis, a life-threatening response to infection. [12][16]

Deepa keeps her clinic’s instructions handy. She knows what to report if the symptoms return and which changes would make the situation more urgent.

Mention burning, urgency, fever and the location of any pain. Get same-day care if symptoms suggest a kidney infection. [2][4][10][12]

The fridge-door cheat sheet

Does burning always mean a UTI?

Burning with urgency or frequency can suggest a bladder infection. Other conditions can cause burning too. [1][2][4]

What should I tell the clinician?

Mention when it started, any fever, where it hurts, previous infections and whether you are pregnant. [2][4][5]

When do I get help quickly?

Seek same-day care for urinary symptoms with fever, shaking chills, side pain or vomiting. Call emergency services for confusion or collapse with possible infection. [10][12][16]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Use this article to discuss urinary symptoms, test results and treatment with your clinician. Ask them before changing any medicine. For severe symptoms, call your local emergency number immediately.
