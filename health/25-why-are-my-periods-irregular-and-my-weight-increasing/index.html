PCOS / PMOS

Why Are My Periods Irregular and My Weight Increasing?

How ovulation, androgens and insulin can affect your cycle, and what a clinician can check.

IRREGULAR OVULATIONHORMONES AND INSULINCARE AND FERTILITY

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The calendar that stopped making sense

Ananya opens her period tracker and counts back. The last gap was nearly two months. She has also noticed more acne, a few coarse hairs on her chin and a change in weight. She wonders whether these things could be connected. At her appointment, she asks if a scan will tell her whether she has PCOS.

Polycystic ovary syndrome, long known as PCOS, can affect ovulation, androgen levels and metabolic health. In 2026 an international initiative introduced a new name, polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome, or PMOS. You may see either name on reports or patient leaflets. The new name reflects that the condition can affect several hormone systems and the way the body handles energy. [1][2][3]

Ananya’s symptoms are a reason to look more closely. Irregular periods and weight gain can have several causes. The clinician asks about pregnancy possibility, her cycle history and other symptoms before deciding what to test. [4][5]

02

Why might my periods come weeks apart?

In a typical ovulatory cycle, an ovary releases an egg. After ovulation, a hormone called progesterone helps prepare the lining of the womb. If pregnancy doesn’t occur, hormone levels change and the lining sheds. In PCOS, ovulation may happen less often or at unpredictable times. That can leave long gaps between bleeds. [2][4][6]

Androgens are hormones everyone makes. Higher androgen levels or signs of their effects can be part of PCOS. You might notice coarse facial or body hair, acne or thinning hair on your scalp. The exact combination varies. Acne alone has many possible causes. Tell the clinician when changes started and whether they’ve been gradual or rapid. [2][4][6]

Ananya marks the first day of her last few periods and notes any bleeding between them. She also lists hormonal medicines, because they can change bleeding patterns and affect how some blood tests are interpreted. [4][5]

Tell your clinician what your periods were like before the current change. Someone whose cycles have always been widely spaced may need a different assessment from someone who was regular until recently. Also mention spotting, unusually heavy bleeding or pain. Those details can help the clinician decide what to check first. [4][5][8]

03

How does insulin fit into the picture?

Insulin helps the body regulate glucose, the sugar in your blood. When tissues respond less well to insulin, the pancreas may release more to keep glucose in range. This is called insulin resistance. In PCOS, higher insulin levels can contribute to higher androgen production in the ovaries. This is one route by which metabolism and reproductive hormones can affect each other. [2][4][6]

Weight can change for many reasons. Some people with PCOS notice weight gain or find weight management difficult, and people at a wide range of weights can have the condition. Ananya asks whether her weight caused everything. Her clinician explains that weight is only one part of the assessment and that checking glucose can matter at any body size. [2][4]

Routine blood insulin tests generally aren’t recommended to diagnose PCOS or guide ordinary follow-up. The clinician can assess glucose and other risks with tests chosen for the situation. [4]

04

Does a scan have to show “cysts”?

The small round structures often seen on a scan in PCOS are follicles, which are sacs where eggs develop. The old name can make the scan sound decisive. Clinicians usually diagnose adults when at least two of three features fit, after considering other explanations. These are irregular ovulation, signs or blood-test evidence of higher androgens, and a characteristic ovarian pattern on ultrasound. A blood test called AMH can sometimes be used instead of ultrasound to assess the ovarian feature in adults. [1][4]

If irregular cycles and higher androgens are already established, a scan or AMH is usually unnecessary for the diagnosis. A scan pattern by itself is insufficient. Teenagers need different assessment because irregular periods and acne can overlap with puberty. In adolescents, both irregular cycles and evidence of higher androgens are needed after excluding other causes, and ultrasound and AMH are unsuitable for diagnosis. [4][7]

05

What else might the clinician check?

If pregnancy is possible, a pregnancy test is an early step. Depending on the history, blood tests may also check thyroid function or prolactin, a hormone that can affect cycles. Your clinician may look for other causes if symptoms begin suddenly or if facial hair or other androgen-related changes progress quickly. [4][5][8]

If PCOS is diagnosed, ask about blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar. The international guideline recommends assessing glucose at diagnosis, and an oral glucose tolerance test is the most accurate option for this condition. It measures how your body handles a glucose drink. What is available and suitable can affect which test you have. [4][9]

Ananya asks why these tests matter when she came about periods. The clinician explains that PCOS can affect future diabetes risk and other aspects of health. The next review can cover both the symptoms she wants help with now and the checks that are worth repeating later. [2][4]

Sleep and mood belong in that conversation too. Tell the team if you snore and wake exhausted, or if anxiety or low mood is affecting daily life. The guideline recommends paying attention to these symptoms alongside cycle and metabolic health. Ananya makes a note to bring up her sleep at the next visit. [4][9]

06

What can help with long gaps between periods?

When periods rarely come, the lining of the womb may spend longer without the usual effects of progesterone. Over time, that can raise the chance of the lining becoming too thick. Most people with PCOS won’t develop womb cancer. Long gaps are worth discussing so the clinician can decide whether treatment is needed to protect the lining. Routine cancer screening is not recommended solely because of PCOS. [2][4][9]

Treatment depends on what you want help with and whether you’re trying to conceive. Some people use hormonal treatment to manage bleeding and protect the lining. Other options may focus on acne, excess hair or metabolic health. Ask which symptom each treatment is meant to address and what follow-up it needs. [4][9][10]

Activity, balanced meals and support with sleep or emotional well-being can help health even without weight loss. Ananya asks for changes she can manage on workdays and which outcomes they’ll review together. [4][9][10]

She decides that her first priorities are understanding the long gaps and finding a plan for bothersome acne. Another person might want to focus on planning a pregnancy or managing glucose. Ask for a plan that reflects your priorities and sets a time to review whether it’s helping. [4][9][10]

07

What if I want to get pregnant?

Irregular ovulation can make it harder to predict when pregnancy might happen. Some people with PCOS conceive without fertility treatment. If you’re trying to conceive and your periods are infrequent, ask when to seek advice. Your clinician may check whether you’re ovulating and consider other fertility factors before choosing a treatment. [4][10][11]

A pre-pregnancy visit is also a chance to review medicines, check blood pressure and discuss glucose testing. [4][9]

When lack of ovulation is the main fertility issue and there are no other infertility factors, the international guideline recommends letrozole as a first-line medicine to help induce ovulation. In one randomised trial, about 28% of participants assigned letrozole had a live birth, compared with about 19% assigned clomiphene. The trial figures describe the groups studied. A fertility clinician can explain the options and what they would mean for you. [4][12]

If you want to avoid pregnancy, ask about contraception. Irregular periods don’t mean ovulation never happens. [4][10]

08

When should I get help sooner?

Book a review if periods repeatedly go missing, bleeding changes or symptoms such as acne or coarse facial hair are worsening. Bring the dates and tell the clinician whether pregnancy is possible. [4][5][8]

Get emergency help for heavy bleeding with faintness, collapse, chest pain or shortness of breath. Tell the care team how much you’ve been bleeding and whether pregnancy is possible. [13][14]

Seek urgent assessment if you could be pregnant and have bleeding with lower abdominal pain, even without a positive test. Severe pain, shoulder-tip pain with dizziness, or fainting can be warning signs of an ectopic pregnancy. Call emergency services if symptoms are severe or you collapse. [14][15][16]

Ananya leaves with dates for her next tests and a list of questions about her symptoms, future health and pregnancy plans. Her period tracker is useful again, this time as something she can take to a review.

Ask what supports the diagnosis, whether long gaps need treatment and which health checks are right for you. [4][5]

KEEP

The fridge-door cheat sheet

What should I bring to my appointment?

Bring period dates, a medication list and any changes in acne, hair growth or bleeding. Mention whether pregnancy is possible. [4][5]

Does everyone need an ultrasound?

No. If irregular cycles and higher androgens are established, a scan is usually unnecessary. The rules differ for teenagers. [4][7]

When should I get urgent help?

Get urgent help for bleeding and lower abdominal pain if pregnancy is possible. Seek emergency care for severe pain or heavy bleeding with faintness or collapse. [13][14][16]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Use this article to discuss cycle changes, test results and treatment with your clinician. Ask them before changing any medicine. For severe symptoms, call your local emergency number immediately.
