KIDNEY STONES

How Can a Tiny Stone Cause Such Terrible Pain?

Why a small stone can hurt so much, how clinicians check for a blockage and what helps prevent another.

BLOCKED URINE FLOWWILL IT PASS?WHEN TO GET HELP

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The pain that would not let him sit still

Ramesh is putting away the dinner plates when pain grips one side of his back, below the ribs. He sits down, stands up again and tries walking across the room. Nothing seems comfortable. The pain eases briefly, then comes back and moves towards his groin. At urgent care, he asks how a stone only a few millimetres wide could hurt so much.

A small stone can cause severe pain if it lodges in the ureter, the narrow tube that carries urine from the kidney to the bladder. Urine can back up behind the stone and raise pressure. The ureter also contracts as it tries to move the stone along. That combination helps explain the waves of pain known as renal colic. [1][2][3]

The clinician checks for infection and impaired urine flow. Other conditions can cause similar pain, so the location and intensity are clues. A urine test and imaging can help clarify what’s going on. [1][4][5]

02

How does a stone form in the first place?

Your kidneys filter blood and make urine. Urine contains dissolved substances such as calcium, oxalate and uric acid. When conditions favour crystallisation, some of those substances come out of solution. Crystals can grow or clump together into a stone. Having too little urine to dilute them can make this more likely. [3][6][7]

Think of what can happen when water evaporates from a salty solution and crystals remain. Inside the kidney, the chemistry is more complicated. Urine acidity matters for some stones, and substances such as citrate can help hold back the formation of calcium stones. [1][6][7]

Calcium oxalate stones are common, but stones can also be made of calcium phosphate, uric acid, struvite or cystine. Some are linked to particular infections or inherited conditions. A lab can analyse a stone if it is collected after passing or removed during treatment. Knowing what it is made of can guide prevention. [1][6][7]

03

Why does the pain come in waves and move?

The ureter moves urine towards the bladder with waves of muscle contraction. When a stone makes it harder for urine to get through, pressure rises above it. The ureter can tighten and spasm around the obstruction. That is why the pain may build, ease, then build again. Nausea, vomiting or blood in the urine can occur too. [2][3][8]

Pain may start in the side or back and spread towards the lower abdomen or groin as the stone moves down. Near the bladder, some people feel an urgent or frequent need to pass urine. The pain pattern is useful, but clinicians use tests to establish whether a stone is present and where it is. [1][2][3]

A stone that sits in the kidney without blocking urine may cause no symptoms. Even if pain improves after a suspected stone, follow-up may be needed to confirm that it has passed and urine is flowing freely. [1][3][8]

You may still be able to pass urine when one ureter is blocked. If the other kidney is working, it can still send urine to the bladder. This is one reason clinicians look at the scan, symptoms and kidney function together. [1][2][8]

04

What will the urine test and scan tell me?

The clinician asks about fever, vomiting, previous stones, pregnancy and kidney problems. A urine sample can show blood or signs of infection. Blood tests can help check kidney function and look for signs of infection. These details help the team decide whether it is safe to wait or whether something needs to happen urgently. [1][4][5]

Imaging helps locate the stone and look for signs that urine is backing up. For adults with suspected renal colic, the UK NICE guideline recommends urgent low-dose CT without contrast, with ultrasound instead if the person is pregnant. Ultrasound is also the first test recommended for children. Local pathways and individual circumstances can affect which test comes first. [1][4][5]

Ramesh’s scan shows a small stone in the ureter near the bladder. He asks whether the kidney can drain, whether there are signs of infection and how the team will check that the blockage has cleared. The scan result helps answer questions he could never settle by judging the pain alone. [1][4]

05

Will a stone this small pass on its own?

A small stone further down the ureter is more likely to pass than a larger stone or one higher up. The European Association of Urology summarises studies in which about 89% of stones smaller than 5 mm in the lower ureter passed on their own. The estimate describes groups in the studies. Your chances also depend on individual factors. [1]

Waiting can be reasonable when the clinician judges it safe, pain is manageable and there is a clear follow-up plan. The team may prescribe pain relief and, in selected cases, a medicine that can help a ureteric stone pass. Infection, persistent severe pain or worsening kidney function can change the plan. [1][4][9]

Ask when to return, whether you should strain your urine to catch the stone and how passage will be confirmed. A small stone can still cause trouble while it is in the tube. If pain returns or you feel unwell, use the safety advice your team has given you. [1][9][10]

06

What happens if it won’t pass?

If the stone is unlikely to pass or keeps causing problems, the clinician may suggest breaking it up or removing it. Shock-wave treatment sends focused waves from outside the body to break certain stones into smaller pieces. In ureteroscopy, a clinician passes a thin instrument through the urinary tract to reach the stone and remove it or break it up. The choice depends on the size and location, your health and what the team can safely offer. [1][4][9]

Sometimes the first priority is to drain the kidney. A ureteric stent is a small internal tube that can help urine pass the blockage. Another approach drains urine directly from the kidney through a tube placed through the skin. When infection is trapped behind an obstruction, urgent drainage and antibiotics may be needed, with stone treatment usually taking place later. [1][9]

Ramesh asks what the procedure would achieve and what follow-up it would require. If a stent is placed, ask whether and when it must be removed. [1][11]

07

What can I do to reduce the chance of another stone?

Prevention depends on the type of stone and why it formed. Drinking enough water over the day helps dilute urine. Ask what fluid intake suits you, especially if you have been told to restrict fluids. Cutting back on excess salt and keeping a normal amount of calcium from food are common steps for people with calcium stones. [1][4][12][13]

In a five-year trial of men with recurrent calcium oxalate stones and high urine calcium, stones recurred in 12 of 60 men assigned normal dietary calcium with less salt and animal protein, versus 23 of 60 assigned a low-calcium diet. The trial tested a combination of changes in a specific group. Its results help explain why cutting out dietary calcium is usually a poor starting point. [12][14]

If you have repeated stones or higher-risk features, your clinician may request a 24-hour urine collection and tailor advice to the results. Ramesh keeps a copy of his scan and asks whether the stone can be analysed if he catches it. [1][7][9]

A collection measures what your body put into the urine across a full day. The lab can check how much urine you made and whether substances such as calcium, oxalate or citrate are present in amounts that matter for your stone risk. [1][7][9]

08

When is a kidney stone an emergency?

New severe pain in the side or back deserves urgent assessment. Tell the team if you have only one working kidney, have kidney disease or are pregnant. These details affect the urgency and which tests are suitable. [1][4][10]

Go to emergency care now if severe side or back pain comes with fever or shaking chills, if you cannot pass urine, or if you keep vomiting and cannot keep fluids down. Severe pain that stays uncontrolled also needs urgent assessment. A blocked kidney with infection can become dangerous quickly. [1][3][10][15]

Call your local emergency number if someone with a possible infection becomes confused, collapses or is severely unwell. These can be signs of a serious infection requiring immediate care. [1][16]

Ramesh saves the follow-up details on his phone. He now knows why the pain was so severe, what would make it unsafe to wait and what to ask about once the episode has settled.

Ask where the stone is, how you’ll know it has passed and what can reduce the risk of another. Fever with severe side pain needs emergency assessment. [1][4][12]

The fridge-door cheat sheet

Why can a tiny stone hurt so much?

A stone can block urine in the narrow tube from the kidney to the bladder, increasing pressure and causing waves of pain. [1][2]

What should I ask before leaving?

Ask where the stone is, how to confirm it has passed and when to come back. [1][4][9]

When is it an emergency?

Get emergency assessment for severe side pain with fever or chills, inability to pass urine, or vomiting that prevents you from keeping fluids down. [1][3][10][15]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Use this article to discuss stone symptoms, scan results and follow-up with your clinician. Ask them before changing any medicine. For severe symptoms, call your local emergency number immediately.
