ALLERGIC RHINITIS

Why Do I Sneeze Ten Times Every Morning?

Why dust mites and pollen can set off sneezing, how to spot a pattern and what can help.

DUST MITES AND POLLENALLERGY OR COLD?WHEN TO GET HELP

About 8 minutes to read · Patient education

01

The sneeze that beats the alarm

Nisha wakes, pulls back the blanket and sneezes. Then she sneezes again, enough times to lose count. Her nose itches, her eyes water and she reaches for a tissue. By the time she gets to work, the fuss has mostly settled. It happens most mornings, though she never feels as if she’s coming down with a fever. She asks whether a cold can keep returning like that.

One possible explanation is allergic rhinitis. That means the lining of the nose becomes inflamed after exposure to something you’re allergic to, such as house dust mites or pollen. A burst of sneezing with an itchy nose, watery eyes or a runny or blocked nose fits that pattern. The time of day can help identify an exposure, but morning sneezing alone doesn’t establish the cause. [1][2]

Nisha’s clinician asks how long it’s been happening, whether she gets the same symptoms when she sleeps away from home, and whether they change with the seasons. [1][2]

02

Why does the nose react so strongly?

The inside of the nose is lined with moist tissue that helps filter the air you breathe. In allergic rhinitis, the immune system recognises a normally harmless substance as a target. In someone who is sensitised, contact with that substance can prompt immune cells to release histamine and other chemicals. Histamine is one reason the nose and eyes itch and the sneezes come in bursts. [2][3]

Inflammation can also make the lining swell and produce more fluid. That helps explain why a nose may start out runny and then feel blocked. Some people mostly sneeze. Others notice stuffiness that makes it harder to sleep. The symptoms can vary with exposure and from one person to another. [1][2][3]

Think of the sneeze as one part of the response. The itch, watery eyes and congestion give the clinician clues about why it happened and what treatment might help. [1][2]

03

Why are mornings sometimes the worst?

House dust mites are tiny creatures that can live in bedding and soft furnishings. If you’re allergic to them, time spent in the bedroom can mean hours near the substances that set off your symptoms. Making the bed or moving the covers can stir particles into the air. Pets, damp or mould in the room may be relevant for some people. [1][2][4]

Pollen can also cause a similar pattern, especially during seasons when the plants you react to are releasing it. An open window or time outside may matter. And some noses react to cold air, perfume or smoke even without an allergy. The trigger, where it happens and what comes with it can help distinguish the possibilities. [1][2][5]

Nisha compares a week at home with a weekend away. She notes where she slept, whether the windows were open and whether her eyes and nose itched as well. The notes make the morning pattern easier to discuss. [1][2]

Symptoms throughout the year can fit an indoor allergy. A flare confined to a particular season can point towards pollen. Some people have both patterns. When Nisha’s clinician asks about the rest of the day, she remembers that sweeping a dusty storeroom sometimes brings on the same symptoms as making the bed. [1][2][3]

04

How is this different from a cold?

Allergies often bring an itchy nose, itchy or watery eyes and repeated sneezes soon after exposure. Symptoms can recur as long as the exposure continues. A cold is caused by a virus and usually changes over several days. Sore throat, aches and sometimes a fever fit a cold more readily. The two can overlap, and you can catch a cold even if you have allergies. [1][2][6]

Non-allergic rhinitis can also cause ongoing stuffiness or a runny nose. Temperature changes, smoke and strong smells may be clues. If nasal blockage stays mainly on one side or comes with repeated bleeding, get it examined. These features may need a different assessment. [2][5][7]

Nisha says the itching is what stands out from the colds she remembers. It starts early, often with watery eyes. That detail is more informative than counting how many times she sneezes. [1][2]

THE DEEP DIVEWhat allergy tests show and what can help
05

Do I need an allergy test?

The clinician asks about the timing, likely exposures, eye symptoms, sleep and any wheeze. They may examine your nose and review products you’ve tried. In some people the symptoms and examination give enough information for an initial treatment plan. Allergy testing can help if the cause is unclear, symptoms persist or a result would change the plan. [1][2][7]

A skin-prick test or a blood test for allergen-specific IgE can show whether your immune system recognises a particular substance. A positive result means sensitisation. It fits a diagnosis of allergy when it also matches what happens around that exposure. Someone can have a positive result to a substance that doesn’t explain their symptoms. [2][8]

Ask which test would answer your question. If a dust-mite result would guide changes in the bedroom or a discussion with an allergy specialist, it may be useful. If symptoms occur only with smoke or strong smells, the clinician may look for another explanation too. [2][5][8]

Tell the clinic about medicines you take before a skin test. Some antihistamines can affect the result. Ask which medicines, if any, you need to hold and for how long. The clinic can advise based on the type of test and your health. [2][8]

06

Can changing the bedroom actually help?

Some changes are worth considering if the exposure fits. Wash bedding regularly, address damp or mould, and consider whether pets entering the bedroom make a difference. If pollen seems to be a trigger, note whether symptoms change with outdoor exposure and local pollen levels where those are available. Focus on changes you can maintain. [1][2][4]

A Cochrane review found limited evidence that measures to reduce house dust mites improve nasal symptoms. Studies tested different approaches. An expensive cover or filter by itself may make little difference. Discuss what’s practical before buying equipment, especially if you aren’t sure mites are the cause. [4]

Some people find a saline rinse helpful for nasal symptoms. For rinsing, use sterile or distilled water, or water that has been boiled and cooled, and follow the device instructions. Ordinary tap water can contain organisms that are unsafe when introduced into the nose. [1][9][10]

07

Which treatments help with itching or blockage?

Antihistamines can help with sneezing, itching and a runny nose. For ongoing symptoms, especially blockage, a corticosteroid nasal spray may be recommended to reduce inflammation. These sprays work best when used as directed. Ask a clinician or pharmacist to show you how to aim the spray slightly outwards, away from the centre wall of the nose. [1][2][11][12]

Decongestant sprays work differently and can give short-term relief. Using them for too long can make congestion worse when the medicine wears off. Ask how long a product is suitable and bring the bottle or a photo of its label if you’re unsure what you’ve been using. [1][5][11]

If symptoms keep disrupting sleep or work despite treatment, ask about reviewing the diagnosis, technique or options. Some people with confirmed allergy may be considered for allergen immunotherapy under specialist care. Mention wheezing or nighttime cough as well, because asthma can occur alongside allergic rhinitis. [1][2][11]

Nisha and her clinician agree to judge the plan by whether she can get through the morning with fewer symptoms and sleep without a blocked nose. She brings the spray back to the next appointment so they can check how she’s using it. [1][2][12]

08

When should I get help sooner?

Book a review if symptoms keep interrupting sleep, the cause is unclear or treatment isn’t helping. Tell the clinician about a persistent blocked nostril on one side, repeated nosebleeds or new wheeze. These can change what should be checked. [1][2][7]

Call your local emergency number if you suddenly have trouble breathing or swallowing, swelling of the lips, tongue or throat, or feel faint or collapse after a possible allergic exposure. These can be signs of a severe allergic reaction. [13]

Get emergency help for severe wheezing or breathing difficulty, especially if you can’t speak in full sentences or your prescribed asthma reliever isn’t helping. Follow your asthma action plan and the dispatcher’s instructions. [14][15]

Nisha keeps a few mornings’ notes for her follow-up. She can say when the sneezing began, how long it lasted and what happened when she slept in another room.

Notice where and when symptoms start, whether your eyes or nose itch, and how your sleep is affected. Ask what’s most likely to help. [1][2]

The fridge-door cheat sheet

Is it a cold or an allergy?

Repeated sneezing with itching or watery eyes can fit allergy. Note when it starts and what you were exposed to. [1][2][6]

What should I ask about treatment?

Bring the products you use. Ask which symptoms each one treats and how to use a spray correctly. [1][11][12]

When is it an emergency?

Call emergency services for severe breathing difficulty, sudden tongue or throat swelling, or collapse after possible allergic exposure. [13][14][15]

Important patient-education notice

General information only. Use this article to discuss nasal symptoms, allergy testing and treatment with your clinician. Ask them before changing any medicine. For severe symptoms, call your local emergency number immediately.
