Why Does One Street Flood While the Next Stays Dry?

The bakery street clears. Around the corner, water is still rising. Follow the rain from the roofs above a Mysuru lane to the drain below it.

7–8 MINUTE READ · 9 EPISODES

01

Two streets, one cloudburst

At the end of a Mysuru downpour, Zappy could see the white road markings outside the bakery. One turn away, a scooter rider had stopped at the edge of a brown pool that covered the full width of the lane. The rain had eased on both streets. At the corner, water was still streaming downhill into the pool.

The baker said the drain must be blocked. A customer blamed the apartment block up the road. Zappy pointed out that the bakery street had plenty of concrete too. Prof. Bolt suggested a different first question. Where did all that water come from, and where could it go?

Imagine both streets got the same rain in this fictional example. Their water levels could still differ because the rain that lands on a street is only part of what reaches it. Some comes from roofs, parking areas and roads higher up. Some leaves quickly. What remains can collect at the lowest point. [1]

From dry ground, Zappy looked back at the corner. The small stream was tracing a route he’d never noticed in dry weather.

02

Follow the slope past the street sign

Imagine spilling a glass of water at the top of a sloping driveway. The water takes the downhill route, even if that means crossing into the next property. Streets work on the same principle. Curbs, driveway ramps and the curve of the road change the route at a much smaller scale. A dip in the road can collect water from both directions. [1]

The land that drains towards a particular point is its catchment. The bakery street in our story is slightly higher and keeps sloping towards another outlet. The flooded lane lies at the bottom of a dip, where two uphill roads deliver runoff. A large catchment can feed a low point even if the patch of road actually underwater is small.

Think of an underpass. Its low point is the reason traffic can pass beneath another road. That same shape means water must be carried away through drainage, sometimes with pumping. It can be hard to see the full route from the pavement because the pipes disappear underground.

When the rain clears, the useful clues are where the road falls away, which surfaces feed it and where a low point could overflow. Look from safe ground. The deepest part of the pool can be hard to judge from its edge. [5]

03

Concrete changes how quickly water arrives

Follow one drop onto a tiled courtyard. It can run to a gutter almost immediately. Another lands in a garden, where some of it can collect in a shallow hollow or soak into the soil. Roofs and paved surfaces generally allow much less infiltration, which can increase runoff and deliver it more quickly to streams and drains. [2]

A garden still has limits. If the soil is saturated from earlier rain or compacted by construction, it can take up water more slowly. A patch of paving can also drain towards a garden rather than straight to the road. How surfaces are connected changes how much water reaches the street and how soon it gets there.

This helps explain the apartment-block argument. A newly paved site could contribute extra runoff if it sends more water towards the low lane. To know whether that happened here, you would need the layout, outlet and former drainage route. The building’s presence alone cannot establish how much it contributed.

04

Twenty millimetres is a lot of buckets

Take a hypothetical catchment with an area of 5,000 square metres. If 20 mm of rain falls evenly on it, that is 0.02 metres of rain multiplied by 5,000 square metres, or 100 cubic metres. That’s 1,00,000 litres of rain falling across the whole area. The volume that reaches a particular drain will depend on infiltration, storage and the drainage route. [1]

Now suppose that rain falls in 15 minutes. Spread evenly across that interval, the average intensity is 80 mm per hour. Spread the same 20 mm over two hours and the average intensity is 10 mm per hour. Same total depth. Very different rate of delivery. Real rainfall can also peak within either interval.

The danger at the lane is a period when water arrives faster than it can leave. A daily rain total hides that timing. It also doesn’t tell you how much rain fell upstream of the street, which can matter if showers vary across a city. The calculation assumes uniform rain to make the arithmetic visible.

The bakery can reopen its door while the lane still has a pool. One location kept passing water along. The other has a backlog to drain.

05

A drain has three places to fail

At the lane’s edge sits a grated opening. Think of it as the front door of the drainage system. Water must first reach that inlet. Leaves can cover the grate, or the road may slope so the flow bypasses it. Even a clean grate can take only so much water at a given depth and flow. [1]

Next comes the pipe or channel. It can be restricted by silt or debris, or simply receive more flow than it can carry. Other roads may be sending water into the same pipe. A grate near your feet and a narrow stretch several blocks away can both affect the pool you see.

The third place is the outlet. A pipe that normally drains into a channel may discharge less easily if that channel is already running high. Water can back up through the system. Enlarging a street inlet can help when the inlet is the limiting point. It won’t solve a high downstream water level on its own. [1]

This gives the baker’s theory a place on the list. A blocked grate is worth checking alongside the buried route and the point where the drain empties.

Inlet captures runoff→Pipe or channel carries it→Outlet discharges it

06

Why the puddle outlasts the rain

Picture a shallow dip holding water. As long as the inflow is greater than the outflow, the pool grows. As inflow eases below outflow, the water level starts to fall. If the lowest point has a poor connection to a drain, water can sit there even after the rest of the street has cleared. [1]

Try a simplified five-minute example. Suppose 1,200 litres per minute enter the dip and 800 leave it. The difference is 400 litres per minute. Over five minutes the dip accumulates 2,000 litres, assuming no infiltration or overflow and constant flow rates. If inflow then stops and outflow stays at 800 litres per minute, that stored volume would take another 2½ minutes to clear.

A real street will rarely hold those rates steady. The flow reaching the pool can lag behind the rain. The discharge can change as the water level falls or the receiving drain backs up. The depth depends on the shape of the road and how far the pool spreads.

Still, the little calculation tells Zappy what to watch from the bakery. If the pool is growing after the rain stops, there is still more water arriving than leaving.

07

Where the lake fits into the story

Zoom out from the lane to the neighbourhood. A connected lake, wetland or detention basin can hold part of a storm’s runoff and release it more slowly if there is room available and the inlet and outlet work. A water body that is already high has less room left for the next burst. Water level downstream can also affect how well a street drain empties. [1] [3]

Bengaluru offers a documented Karnataka example of the importance of those connections. A Comptroller and Auditor General performance audit tabled in 2021 examined stormwater management for 2013–14 to 2017–18. It reported encroachment on lakes and drains, loss of natural drainage and weakened connections between water bodies as land use changed. Those are historical findings for Bengaluru. Our Mysuru lane is fictional. [4]

A drain repair at one street corner can relieve a local blockage. At catchment scale, officials also need to know whether the rest of the route can take that flow, where water can be held temporarily and what happens when the system fills. A faster outlet from one place can send a sharper flow peak downstream.

A storage area can hold runoff when water can reach it and there is room available.

08

The evidence a neighbour can collect safely

After the water recedes, Zappy sketches the lane from the bakery. He marks the low point, the nearest grate and the corner where the water streamed in. A useful report records the street or landmark, the time and direction of incoming water if it was visible, how long the pool lasted and whether the location floods repeatedly. A photo from dry ground can help identify the spot.

These details can help a municipal drainage team distinguish among plausible causes. If flow runs straight past an inlet, they can check its position and capture capacity. If water comes up through an opening, they can inspect downstream conditions. If neighbouring streets have been repaved, they can check changes in road levels and drainage routes. A resident’s observations point to where to investigate. [1]

During flooding, take a dry route. Avoid walking, riding or driving through floodwater. It can hide holes, displaced covers or damaged road surfaces. Keep away from electrical equipment and follow official warnings. Report the location from safety and leave drain covers and grates alone. [5]

The VerdictBack to the bakery
09

Back to the bakery corner

The next morning the scooter is gone and the lane looks ordinary again. From the corner Zappy can see a slight dip, a driveway tilted towards it and two road edges that carry water downhill. He sketches those routes and circles the grate. He can explain why the lane is more exposed to ponding than the bakery street. Which part of the system limited drainage that evening would take an inspection.

The verdict is a balance. The bakery street had a route that could carry its runoff away. The low lane received water from a wider area and stored the excess while it waited to drain. In another neighbourhood, local rainfall, a blocked opening, a small pipe or high water at the outlet could change the result.

If it happens again, note the location and time from somewhere safe. Report repeated ponding with enough detail for the local team to trace the water uphill to its sources and downhill to the receiving drain or water body. The dry street gives a comparison right next door.
